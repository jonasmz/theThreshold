# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 46: The Cost of His Left Hand

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel, limited third person.

***

The man couldn't untie the string around his parcel.

Inara Vel watched him try twice before offering to help. They were at a bakery he'd chosen for their meeting, far from the offices where other journalists had spent months waiting for him.

“I don't want a photograph of my hand,” he said.

“I won't take one.”

He introduced himself as Iven Ro, a former calibration assistant. He had an officially recognized injury from an occupational accident during a technical training course. What mattered to him wasn't recounting how the accident happened. It was how long he'd been trying to get the administration to honor his rehabilitation agreement.

He took three documents from a folder. The first was a ruling recognizing his treatment costs and a period of incapacity for precision work. The second was a benefits schedule. The third was a certificate from the paying office: only some of the sessions had been covered.

“My hand was my livelihood,” he said. “Now I do inventory work. I can write slowly if I rest. What I can't do is hold a tool without shaking.”

Inara read the dates. The ruling mentioned no program to create human beings and identified no strategic stabilization maneuver. The course involved conventional low-power occupational training. The file did not justify turning it into a secret trial.

“Do you still have your medical records?” she asked.

“Yes. I'll show them to an expert, not to a newspaper.”

“That's enough to begin with. Which office recognized the expense?”

Iven pointed to the workplace assistance agency.

“The same office that says it doesn't have the money to pay.”

At last, he managed to undo the string and removed a small, awkwardly carved wooden object. It was a figure he'd promised to finish for his daughter.

“I don't want to be anybody's symbol,” he said. “I want to know why a signed obligation is worth less than the paper it's written on.”

***

Inara requested a public, redacted copy of the compliance accounts.

The assistance agency provided a payment register matching Iven's receipts by case number and period without exposing his diagnosis. The difference between the approved and disbursed amounts could be verified without knowing the exact treatment. The compensation fund blamed the delay on transport funds being reassigned after the corridors closed; the rehabilitation office said it had not received complete payment orders.

Joren Mal wanted to headline the story “The State Abandons Its Injured.”

“We can prove one specific failure to comply,” Inara said. “Not that every injured worker has been abandoned.”

“They've admitted the delay.”

“And we need to show how it affects a person. But the headline can't turn one case into every case.”

They visited a community assistance office handling questions about outstanding payments. The employee explained the general procedure: a beneficiary could demand enforcement of a ruling and review of its deadlines. She couldn't show them anyone else's case files.

Iven had authorized her to confirm only that a compliance complaint existed, not to reveal his clinical history.

When Inara visited him again, she found a cup filled with water and tiny screws on his table. He was practicing moving each piece from one container to another with his left hand.

“On good mornings I can manage six,” he said. “On bad ones, none.”

“Is that part of your rehabilitation?”

“Part of what I can still afford on my own.”

Inara closed her notebook. The moment was real; turning it into a moving scene without asking would be another way of taking something from him.

“Can I describe your practice with small parts? No photographs, no address.”

Iven nodded.

“That, yes. Don't write that I'll be able to calibrate again. No doctor has promised me that.”

***

The labor-rights examiner gave them an answer that complicated the story.

With Iven's authorization, she had read the ruling and checked certification of the outstanding benefits. She confirmed that he had an enforceable entitlement and that the failure to pay was documented. But administrative recognition of an injury did not, by itself, prove what technical defect had caused the accident or who had been negligent.

“The file distinguishes recognized harm from disciplinary responsibility,” she explained. “To accuse anyone, you'd need the safety report, the equipment, and the conditions of that course. None of that is here.”

Inara appreciated the precision.

Joren had found a list of former training workshops and wanted to link the accident to historical allegations about other programs. Inara reminded him that the worker had not authorized that association and that the dates, institutions, and records were different.

The paying office responded to her request for comment. It acknowledged a delay in partially executing the ruling and announced a review of its obligations without promising a payment date. Its representative said budget restrictions affected numerous cases.

“How many have been publicly verified?” Inara asked.

“This delay, definitely,” Joren replied.

“Then let's publish this one. We can add a general figure only if we substantiate it separately.”

Before publication, Iven read the quotations attributed to him. He did not hold veto power over the journalism, but he could flag a factual error. One paragraph said he'd lost “all ability to work.”

He crossed it out.

“I still work. Fewer hours, worse pay, and at something else. That's not the same.”

Inara corrected the sentence.

She'd learned that exaggerating someone's suffering could erase the very part of them that continued to resist being reduced to it.

***

At Iven's request, the edition appeared without his name.

The newspaper described a recognized compensation claim, the unpaid benefits, the human cost, and the competent offices' responses. It avoided dates and employment details that could identify him in combination; it included neither clinical documents nor accusations of unproven crimes.

That afternoon, the compensation fund issued a written commitment to pay an initial portion and submit the balance to supervised review. It was a verifiable response, not money received. Inara recorded the difference before calling Iven.

“Are you going to print that as if I'd already been paid?” he asked.

“No. When the payment arrives, we can verify it, if you want.”

There was silence.

“I don't need you to come back to look at my hand.”

“I know.”

He thanked her anyway.

In the newsroom, Joren showed her another note: a workers' association wanted to provide testimony about training and accidents. Inara asked them to offer a voluntary, secure channel separate from any judicial investigation. She wouldn't turn the accounts into proof of a single program merely because the people involved had all suffered.

They still didn't know how much harm could be attributed to conscious decisions to risk the Network, or which officials had read the ancient warnings. But there was already a present obligation an administration had recognized and only partly fulfilled.

Inara placed a copy of the commitment in her follow-up folder.

Proving the harm had required no secret archive.

It had been enough to establish what was owed, what had been paid, and how much of someone's life had been left waiting.

***End of Episode 46.***
