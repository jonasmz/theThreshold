# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 27: The Signature That Wasn't There

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, limited third person.

***

The seal on the record was genuine.

Kael Veyran had spent weeks expecting a more complicated answer. The civil registry's document examiner pointed it out beneath her magnifying lens: ink consistent with the period, pressure matching the preserved seal, and the correct register number.

“We're not disputing that the entry was made,” she said. “We're asking what documents the person who made it actually examined.”

Dalia Ferren stood beside him. Kael's old childhood entry lay open on a padded support. The documentary guardianship notation appeared in the margin, alongside the record that a woman had taken responsibility for his care. That childhood had been his, lived and remembered, apart from the specific losses Transit had left behind.

The modern copy contained an additional line: “Origin verified by supplementary certificate.” The original showed an abbreviation there, not the signature of the office supposedly responsible for verification.

“Can the copy be corrected?” Kael asked.

“We can record that someone expanded an abbreviation without checking its source,” the examiner replied. “To establish what it meant, we'll have to open the referral index under its own access rules.”

The distinction reminded him of the two dates in his employment record. He had learned to recognize the habit of turning a plausible explanation into evidence nobody had supplied.

“Then don't treat it as established,” he said.

The examiner signed a record of discrepancy. She neither confirmed nor invalidated Kael's parentage. The civil registry still had to serve people who depended on its certificates; it couldn't erase an entry merely because one field needed an audit.

When Kael looked up, he saw two children waiting in the corridor, each holding a copy of a birth certificate.

For the first time, he understood that the word *origin* troubled him less than the right not to have to explain his life every time someone demanded a piece of paper.

***

The records protection office authorized a limited comparison of the transfer index.

The custodian attended. No complete pages concerning other minors were released. Kael watched through a consultation window as the examiner compared his entry number against the referral notations.

They found two things.

First, the supplementary register had left the civil archive with a receipt from a protection unit, not from Personnel. The seal documented the transfer of a book, not Kael's registration. Second, the page containing his reference retained an old case number that the modern copy had omitted, treating it as an administrative mark of no relevance to the person concerned.

“Can we use that number to request access?” Dalia asked.

“We can ask the responsible custodian to check whether it holds information about him,” the examiner replied. “I can't give you a complete list of everyone included in the same batch.”

Kael wrote down the procedure, not the numbers of the neighboring pages.

In the margin was a note about how the child's identity had been verified: a receipt for a placement into care, issued by a medical institution, had been checked at the time of registration. It was not a birth certificate. Nor did it say who had produced a child, or how the child had come into the world.

“Is that document still here?” he asked.

“No. The records show it was returned to the issuer after examination. The Registry retained the reference and the signature of the person who verified it.”

Kael breathed slowly.

It was a new path, one that didn't begin with his employment record. It proved no relationship to the Mirror Index. It came from an act of civil registration when he was small, with different custodians and witnesses.

The examiner added a warning.

“If the issuer retains a clinical document, only an authorized party can request its contents. That number isn't a key.”

Kael looked at his hands.

His entire investigation seemed to consist of finding doors that could only be opened with another kind of permission.

***

The woman who had raised him welcomed Kael with a pot simmering on the stove.

They had exchanged letters about his missing memory of a rainy afternoon. She had never asked him to fill the gap with a medical explanation. Tonight he had come to ask about the day they began living together.

“Did they give you a document at a medical institution?” he asked once they were seated.

The woman set her spoon on a napkin.

“They gave me feeding instructions, a doctor's examination report, and a receipt confirming I could take you home. I had to go back twice because we were short on blankets.”

Kael felt a surge of tenderness he hadn't anticipated.

“Did you keep anything?”

“An expense notebook. And a photograph that was damaged by damp. I handed the placement receipt back when I registered you. They told me the original would remain with the service.”

“Did they tell you where I'd come from?”

She slowly shook her head.

“They said you'd needed care before being discharged, and that I could ask questions about your health. Some other questions they couldn't answer, or wouldn't. I couldn't tell which was confidentiality and which was ignorance.”

Kael had arrived prepared to hear a lie.

Instead, he found an old uncertainty.

“Didn't you think it was strange?”

“I thought it was strange how cold you were when I brought you home. I thought it was strange that nobody taught me how to get you to sleep. We learned after that.”

The woman stood and fetched a wooden box. She took out a notebook recording the cost of milk, coal, roof repairs, and the nights when a child had run a fever. No government seals appeared on those pages.

“This doesn't prove anything about where you came from,” she said. “But everything I wrote after I took you in is true to me.”

Kael traced a crooked line: the price of an oversized scarf.

That scarf had existed. He remembered its ends brushing against his wrists.

He could not let an investigation into his origins reduce those years to scenery.

***

Dalia asked him to keep the different records separate on the table.

The household notebook remained with its owner. The Civil Registry held the original book and the comparison record. The medical institution, if it still existed as a custodian, would have to answer for its own receipt. And Personnel was still searching for the provenance of the Mirror link through a separate employment procedure.

“These sources have independent origins,” Dalia said. “But they still don't establish the same conclusion.”

Kael agreed.

They filed a request to locate the medical placement document, limited to his own data. The protection office replied that it could process the request with accredited representation and safeguards for third parties. It added that certain historical entries had shared custody and would need to be examined by a safeguards authority.

There was no immediate access.

Kael asked for the reference to be preserved before the ordinary retention deadline expired. The examiner documented the request and the physical condition of the books. No office could reconstruct a missing signature using his name as a pretext.

When he returned home, the woman who raised him had left a note in an envelope.

*If you find something I don't understand, you don't have to explain it to me until you're ready. The years we spent together aren't on trial.*

Kael put the note beside the notebook he'd decided not to remove from her house.

His birth record needed to be checked.

His life did not.

***End of Episode 27.***
