# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 23: Where a Signature Belongs

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Daren Holt, limited third person.

***

The transport register could not leave the repository, so Daren Holt had to cross the city to examine it.

The inspection had been postponed twice. First because of the cooperation agreement with the territorial authority holding the original, and then because no vehicle was available while the strategic routes remained suspended. Daren arrived with the document examiner, a defense representative, and the prosecutor. Every party had received the questions approved by the judge.

The custodian made them wait behind a line painted on the floor.

“The examination is limited to an order releasing remains, its chain of receipt, and the corresponding reference numbers,” he reminded them. “If you encounter references to other people, you must seek permission to examine those.”

The defense representative nodded. Daren did the same.

He had spent months trying to determine how a body that was not the condemned man had reached the scaffold. A court copy of a clinical report linked the body to an earlier human trial, though its identity had still not been established. The original release order might explain an administrative movement. It could not, by itself, give the dead person a name.

When the first box was opened, the examiner leaned over the register.

The number matched the one they had requested. It carried a suffix different from the one used by the medical service. After consulting the cross-reference index, the examiner confirmed that the two codes referred to the same movement between offices.

“Does that prove the body in the square was sent from here?” the prosecutor asked.

“It proves these offices recorded a transfer under the same entry,” the examiner answered. “Physical continuity and the identity of the contents still require comparison of seals, receipt records, and other supporting documents.”

The answer was designed to prevent a premature victory.

Daren was grateful someone else had said it first.

***

The custodian opened the seal inventory.

There was a delivery entry, a time, and two receiving signatures. The physical description partly matched the medical service's copy: the container's weight and packaging code agreed, but a line about the refrigeration status had been covered by a handwritten correction.

The examiner inspected the ink without touching the page.

“The correction was entered by the duty operator and dated after the movement,” she reported. “That isn't proof of fraudulent tampering. We'll need to consult the incident register, if we're authorized.”

The prosecutor requested an extension of the examination to that entry. The defense asked for the reason to be recorded and for an opportunity to question the method. The custodian suspended the inspection while he obtained authorization from the territorial authority.

Daren stepped into the corridor.

A window overlooked a courtyard of stationary carts. Workers wearing gloves stacked empty containers without disturbing the seals on occupied ones. Maintaining temperature consumed accumulators and labor shifts. It was hard to imagine a body passing through without leaving a material trace. But every trace could have more than one explanation, and none should automatically become proof of guilt.

Mara Ellin had prepared a separate note concerning the clinical report. The documented injuries were consistent with those from a failed stabilization trial. They had not proved which order had authorized it or who had obtained adequate consent.

When the parties returned, the custodian permitted them to consult only the relevant incident entry. It recorded a brief refrigeration alarm and a subsequent inspection, without information sufficient to connect the correction to a clandestine delivery.

The entry was incomplete.

The prosecutor did not get the sentence she had hoped for. Nor could the defense declare the chain flawless.

Daren wrote down both limitations.

***

The question of consent arose in a different meeting.

At the courthouse, Mara placed two redacted forms in front of Daren. They had arrived through cooperation with the medical authority. One described general authorization for care and medical observation. The other was the form required under the regulations for high-exposure stabilization procedures. The supplied versions contained no patients' names.

“The copy we have concerning the fatal incident references the first form,” Mara said. “I haven't found an acceptance number for the specific maneuver in the clinical annex.”

“Does that prove there wasn't one?”

“No. It proves it isn't in the material I hold, and that the visible reference isn't sufficient to establish consent for that trial.”

The patients' rights advocate called in as an independent consultant listened to the explanation. She had no access to the personal records of unidentified individuals and asked that no one treat her as the automatic representative of a deceased person whose name they had yet to establish.

“A general signature can be authentic and still be insufficient,” she said. “Specific authorization might also exist in another custodian's records. The question is what information that person received, whether they could refuse, and who documented it.”

Daren examined the intervention form. It listed risks, the need for prior assessment, and an option to refuse. Approving a well-written regulation was easy. Proving someone had respected it in a room where the available energy had been insufficient to sustain the procedure was harder.

The prosecutor suggested requesting every form from the center.

“Only the authorization index and the documents relevant to this trial,” Daren corrected, “with the custodian involved. There are other people whose data we don't need.”

The request was narrowly defined: search for records corresponding to the clinical movement under investigation, inventory the documents found, and record both access granted and access refused.

They did not ask to inspect strangers' medical histories because of a similar date.

***

The judge reviewed the developments in a closed hearing.

Daren presented the transport comparison record and the medical note explaining why the general authorization reference was insufficient. He explained that they could preserve the hypothesis that specific consent had been absent without announcing it as a proven fact.

“The defense has the right to look for documentation that contradicts that hypothesis,” the magistrate said.

“Yes. And anyone whose data appears must retain their own protections.”

The judge authorized the limited request to the medical custodian and imposed access controls with participation by the parties. She did not order the repository seized or move the transport original into another jurisdiction. Any material obtained would remain under restrictions subject to review.

Afterward, Mara stood beside the door.

“What will you do if we find an authentic signature?” she asked.

“Ask what it authorized. Then whether it applied to the act that took place. And whether the person was genuinely free to refuse.”

“And if we find nothing?”

Daren gathered his folder.

“We'll document the search, the limits of the repositories, and their responses. A documented absence isn't permission to invent someone's voice.”

In the corridor, he found a communication from the medical custodian. It acknowledged the request and warned that part of the consent index had been retained by a training department outside its direct control.

Daren read the sender's name and verified the department's jurisdiction. It wasn't evidence yet.

It was the reason the next letter would have to go to another door.

***End of Episode 23.***
