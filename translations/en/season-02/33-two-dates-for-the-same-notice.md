# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 33: Two Dates for the Same Notice

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel, limited third person.

***

The first date appeared in an archive open to the public.

The second was in an equally public catalog a few streets away. Inara Vel wrote them on separate sheets before placing them side by side. Both described the distribution of a safety supplement for ancient regulators. One indicated that the supplement had been published before a change in operating procedures. The other placed it afterward.

Joren Mal leaned over the newsroom table.

“Is one of them lying?”

“We don't know whether they're describing the same event.”

Inara had found the catalogs through a municipal procurement notice: additional funds had been assigned to verify technical manuals. The notice disclosed neither installation details nor their condition. It explained that the review would compare versions of procedures and their distribution certificates.

The first repository was the historical library of professional manuals. Its catalog entries gave an editorial registration date and a supplement code. The second was the manual circulation archive of a technical college. Its entries recorded warehouse arrival dates and delivery acknowledgments.

The difference might matter. It might also be explained by the fact that editing, printing, registering, and distributing were not the same act.

“We can show the dates don't match,” Joren said.

“Yes. But first I want to ask the custodians what each date represents.”

The library replied that its field was called “edition.” The college maintained that its date referred to “receipt.” The discrepancy didn't disappear; it changed shape.

Inara asked to examine the underlying records used to create each catalog entry.

She requested no blueprints or operating parameters for the regulator. The heading, supplement number, incoming stamps, and cataloging rules would be enough.

For the first time in weeks, the problem seemed simple.

In principle, it could be resolved by a humble question: when had someone actually held that document in their hands?

***

The library reading room smelled of bookbinding glue.

The archivist showed her a reproduction of the edition entry. There was a registration mark and a marginal note reading “superseded in circulation.” The note had no date of its own. Inara photographed only what she was permitted to: the heading, the register stamp, and the explanation of the reproduction method.

“Who wrote that note?” she asked.

“It isn't recorded. It could be an annotation by the librarian at the time or a later restorer. We're preserving the original unchanged.”

At the technical college, a more suspicious manager greeted her.

“We're not hiding a manual,” he said before sitting down. “What we have are delivery receipts from workshops.”

“I want to know how your supplement number relates to the library's.”

The manager compared the entries. His list included a suffix missing from the other one, indicating a special circulation batch.

“It could be the same revision printed for another equipment family,” he said. “Or a reissue. Without the packing register, I can't be certain.”

“Does that register exist?”

“Part of the inventory was transferred when the original warehouse closed. I've requested its dispatch list.”

Inara submitted her inquiry in writing. She had learned not to replace a missing document with the smile of someone who seemed to be telling the truth.

As she left, she saw two students waiting for the list of manuals required for their certification. One asked whether they'd have to study another version of the procedure.

The manager replied that they would continue using the approved edition until a competent technical authority ordered otherwise. He couldn't change a training program just because a journalist had found two dates.

Inara recognized both the sense and the danger in that answer. If the material was wrong, waiting had a cost. If the suspicion was unfounded, changing it hastily had a cost, too.

***

Back in the newsroom, Joren had drafted a headline: “Safety Warning Hidden for Years.”

Inara handed it back without even discussing the typeface.

“We haven't proved it was a warning about a known danger,” she said. “We've proved that two repositories recorded different points in the history of a supplement whose scope hasn't yet been established.”

“Isn't that enough to ask why?”

“Yes. To ask, not to assign intent.”

They reviewed a response from the municipality. The safety authority confirmed that it was examining the distribution history of technical instructions but could not disclose parameters for installations still in use. It offered to publish the general procedure for selecting applicable versions once that procedure's relevance had been established.

Inara wrote a short piece about traceability problems in public technical manuals: what an edition date means, what a receipt date means, and why the two must be distinguished before claiming someone received a warning at a given time. She included both repositories' responses and the review schedule.

Joren objected to the ending.

“There's no culprit.”

“There's a verifiable question that nobody had formulated precisely before.”

That afternoon a messenger arrived with a certified copy of the college's cataloging regulations. The clause concerning edition suffixes indicated that some copies were distributed for training, not for field operation. It did not establish which copy had reached the Annex, or when.

Inara added the information before printing.

She would not publish a conclusion that might lead a technician to think there were new instructions for operating an installation. The article avoided capacity figures, locations, and operating sequences. Its subject was the custody of the document, not how to activate the Network.

***

The article appeared on page two the following day.

A worker from the manual warehouse came to the newspaper wearing his uniform. He didn't want Inara to identify him as a whistleblower, and he didn't ask her to publish a secret. He showed her a public circular about preserving delivery acknowledgments.

“On some shifts, receipts were lost when they moved the warehouse,” he said. “Now they're all supposed to be in another collection. But nobody told us which one.”

“Did you see the originals?” Inara asked.

“I saw sealed boxes. I can't tell you what was inside.”

Inara asked whether he would submit an institutional inquiry about the inventory's location. He agreed that she could reproduce the circular's public reference number, but not his name or shift.

After he left, Joren closed the office door.

“Could this connect to the Commission?”

“We haven't accessed the Commission's collection or established any link,” Inara answered. “We're not drawing a line between two repositories just because both contain boxes.”

She called the college and the library to tell them about the new inquiry. Both agreed to preserve their catalog entries and respond about the transfer. Neither gave her a private register or a list of officials to blame.

The response would not come immediately.

Inara filed her two sheets with the different dates in a folder marked *Review Pending*. Beneath them she added a third sheet: “Meaning of the Fields.”

She had spent enough time writing headlines to know the appeal of an immediate accusation.

This time, she wanted to know exactly what had happened before saying that anyone had been warned.

***End of Episode 33.***
