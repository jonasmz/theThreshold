# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 19: The Test No One Performed

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel, limited third person.

***

The press was running when Inara Vel decided to stop it.

Joren Mal turned from the workshop door, the clatter of the type still pounding behind him.

“If we stop now, we lose the morning edition.”

“If we print this accusation as it stands, we lose something worse.”

On the table lay a column naming three Pelder Warehouses employees as responsible for an irregular laboratory certificate. A contributor had matched a list of transport workers' shifts with the name of the company that prepared the packaging for the impounded replacement part.

“We don't know who wrote the certificate,” Inara said. “Pelder transported the box. We've already confirmed it isn't the laboratory. Those shift records don't prove its workers altered anything.”

Joren reread the lines.

“But Oversight confirmed the test doesn't belong to that part.”

“That we can prove. Not the rest.”

That day, Inara had received a public ruling from the oversight authority. It described the comparison of a test report attached to a municipal warranty delivery: the report number referred to a different piece of equipment, and Vesta Laboratory had certified that it had never tested the specified unit. The public case record omitted workers' names and restricted technical information, but included the dates necessary to reconstruct the contractual claim.

One more question remained before she could print the word *fraud*: had money actually been paid for supplies represented as compliant?

Inara grabbed her coat.

“Keep the page without the names,” she ordered. “I'm going to the Treasury.”

Outside, residents lined up for water-supply slots. A man tapped an empty container against the ground to keep his daughter awake. They didn't know who had signed which certificate. They knew their faucet still delivered too little.

Inara walked past without turning them into a front-page photograph.

***

The Treasury payment ledger contained a closed entry.

An official showed Inara the public extract: the date the warranty replacement invoice was approved, the amount paid for declared preparation and testing expenses, the order reference, and the administrative certification submitted to release the payment. She did not reveal private accounts or the names of employees who processed the transaction.

“Was the amount actually disbursed, or just set aside?” Inara asked.

“Disbursed. There's now a recovery claim, and other payments pending under the same order have been suspended during the review.”

“Did the laboratory receive any money?”

“There's no record of a direct municipal payment to the laboratory. The supplier for the transaction received the invoiced amount. How it distributed the money internally isn't shown in this entry.”

“Can you certify the connection between the report and the payment?”

The official indicated a case number.

“Oversight established that in its ruling. We certify the disbursement and the administrative documents used to authorize it. We cannot say who wrote a particular sheet or whether anyone intended to deceive.”

Inara compared the documents.

An organization had been paid on the basis of a technical requirement declared fulfilled. The alleged conformity report had not been issued for the corresponding part. It was a materially false attribution of a test, verifiable from the records of two separate institutions. Whether it amounted to a crime, and who was responsible, would be for the courts to decide.

The finding no longer rested on a journalist's suspicion.

Inara requested certified copies of the payment entry, the technical extract, and the recovery demand. She paid the fees with money from the edition Joren had agreed to delay.

As she left, a woman carrying water recognized her.

“Are they going to repair the pump now?”

Inara looked at the containers.

“I don't have that answer yet.”

“Then write that, too.”

She didn't need to note it. The words had been following her for days.

***

Arven's representative agreed to receive her questions in a room open to the public.

Inara showed him the document identifiers, not any worker's name.

“Treasury confirms the payment. Vesta confirms the cited test concerns a different unit. Oversight links the two records. Who submitted the certification, and what checks did Arven perform before requesting payment?”

The man asked for a few minutes. He returned with a written response.

Arven acknowledged that it had submitted the warranty file to the administration through its supplier network. It maintained that the association of the report number with the delivered unit could have resulted from a filing error and that it had begun an internal review. It rejected the suggestion that the discrepancy proved criminal fraud or justified naming individual employees.

“Will you offer a refund?” Inara asked.

“We will respond to the administrative demand within the allotted time.”

“Is there another conformity test for that unit?”

“We have no alternative certified report to present today.”

At Pelder Warehouses, a supervisor reiterated in writing that the company had only received and dispatched the package and had performed no tests. Vesta reaffirmed the authenticity of the laboratory register submitted to Oversight and clarified that it was not authorizing disclosure of its other customers' information.

Inara gave all three organizations until press time to correct any factual inaccuracies. She obtained no suspects' names and did not ask for any.

Back at the newspaper, she found Joren measuring the space they had lost on the front page.

“We could headline it ‘The Testing Fraud,’” he suggested.

“We can say someone was paid for a test attributed to a part the laboratory never received. That's more accurate.”

“Doesn't sound as big.”

“It's big to whoever paid the bill.”

***

The article appeared the following morning.

It traced the public documents: a certificate used to justify a municipal payment, a laboratory that identified the report as belonging to another unit, and an audit that initiated recovery of funds and an inquiry into responsibility. It explained that the false attribution of a document in the transaction had been established. It did not claim a criminal conviction or identify whoever physically created the false association.

A separate section described explanations still requiring investigation: a mistaken link between files, alteration by a third party, failures in the supplier's controls, or a deliberately misleading submission. Intent had yet to be determined.

Joren placed the water schedule beside the article.

“Do we really need to print it again?” he asked.

“Yes. A certificate doesn't repair a pump.”

Before the deadline, Inara had called Sera Nhal for a general technical statement. The engineer publicly confirmed that the part remained in quarantine and had not been installed. She disclosed neither photographs nor protected records. Readers did not need every internal measurement to understand why service was still restricted.

The print run was smaller because of the delay. Several news vendors complained of losses. Inara showed them the accounts and accepted that her editorial decision had a price for them, too.

That afternoon, a message arrived from Oversight: the authority had registered the supplier's challenge, preserved the originals, and opened adversarial proceedings to resolve the recovery claim. The appeal did not erase the documentary facts established through comparison, but it required the defenses to be considered before assigning responsibility.

Inara filed the notice beside the published documents.

She had not named a guilty person.

She had shown the city a transaction in which the money, the paperwork, and the technical evidence did not match.

This time, the blank space in her article was not the name of a laboratory.

It was the name of the person who would have to answer for using that laboratory's name for a test it had never performed.

***End of Episode 19.***
