# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 29: What No One Taught Him to Obey

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Lyra Saren, limited third person.

***

The woman was carrying a wooden box too heavy for the vehicle she'd hired.

Lyra Saren found her in the third dock's yard, arguing with the driver over freight charges. He offered to move it as ordinary cargo; she insisted it couldn't be left out in the rain.

“They're notebooks,” she explained when Lyra approached. “Some are from when the boy was little. I need to take them to a certifying officer so she can record the condition they're in.”

Lyra recognized the woman who had raised Kael. She didn't know her well; she'd seen her during the most difficult days of the earlier investigation, always worried that officials talked about Kael as though he weren't there.

“A family notebook doesn't require an entire cart,” Lyra said. “But it does need someone who won't leave it in an anonymous storage depot.”

The woman glanced at her hands.

“I don't know whether I ought to show these to anyone.”

Lyra didn't ask what they contained. She explained that a certifying officer could record the condition of the box and its pages without indiscriminately reading a person's private life. She'd learned the distinction while watching a driver protect his route book when another office tried to gather up everyone's documents.

Ema appeared with the transport inventory.

“We can arrange a closed-table service and have the owner accompany the shipment,” she said. “The association won't pay for it with the shelter's funds.”

The woman accepted a quote she could afford. Lyra offered to walk with her to the nearby office, without assuming ownership of the documents or opening them.

Before they lifted the box, a loose sheet slipped from between two covers.

Lyra caught sight of a sentence in a child's handwriting: *I don't want people telling me what I'll be when I grow up.*

She put the sheet back without commenting on it.

***

Seris Hal, the certifying officer, refused to accept the notebooks until their owner explained what she wanted deposited.

“I don't expect you to certify that every memory happened exactly as it's written,” the woman said. “I want to make sure that a year from now, nobody can claim I changed those pages today.”

Seris examined the inventory they'd prepared and asked that the household notebook be separated from letters received from institutions. The letters could contain information about other people. There was no authorization to reproduce them in full.

Lyra watched from a chair. Her presence didn't make her a witness to Kael's childhood. She could testify that the box had arrived closed and that its owner had presented certain notebooks that day. Nothing more.

“Do you need a copy of everything?” Seris asked.

“Only the pages that let me preserve what was mine,” the woman answered. “Kael can decide later which parts he wants to see.”

The certifying officer prepared a limited record of the date, page count, condition, and physical markings on the covers. The originals would remain with their owner. Authorized reproductions would be sealed and made available under the agreed access conditions.

The woman followed the movement of the stamp with her eyes.

“When he was little, he had a habit,” she said, speaking to no one in particular. “If I told him he ought to learn a trade so he could serve in an office, he'd get angry. He said he wanted to choose for himself. Then he ended up working with documents. I don't know whether that means he listened to me or made an entirely different choice.”

Seris lifted her pen.

“I can't certify an interpretation of that story.”

“I'm not asking you to.”

Lyra felt that the woman had spent years carrying a doubt no office should be expected to resolve.

***

On the way back, the rain forced them to shelter beneath an awning.

The woman held the box, now empty of its copies, while the originals were in a small bag tucked close to her body. Lyra offered her a travel blanket. The woman declined with a smile.

“Kael told me there's an afternoon in our house that no longer lives in his memory,” she said. “He believes what I wrote him, but he doesn't remember it. Did anything like that happen to you?”

Lyra watched the water streaming from the gutter.

“I lost the memory of a promise I made before returning from Selivar,” she replied. “I know I kept it because there are witnesses and documents. I can't recover the moment when I chose to make it.”

“Does that make it any less yours?”

“No. But some days I wish I could feel it again.”

The woman ran a finger along the seam of her bag.

“Sometimes I wonder whether I raised him the way I wanted to, or the way other people expected me to.”

Lyra considered her answer. She didn't know every agreement surrounding that childhood. Nor did she have the right to absolve or condemn someone based on a story told in the rain.

“Raising someone means making decisions that affect their life,” she said. “That doesn't mean they obey every decision. There's a difference between teaching a child to cross a street and deciding where that child must go for the rest of their life.”

The woman looked toward the harbor.

“I didn't teach him to want the things he wanted.”

“One story can't prove that either,” Lyra answered. “But you can tell him what you saw. He'll decide what to do with it.”

Someone opened a nearby door, letting steam drift out of a kitchen. Lyra caught the smell of hot soup and remembered a conversation with Ema that no loss of memory had taken away from her. Life was made of small choices, too, choices no institution recorded.

When the rain let up, they walked to the docks.

The woman paid for the transport and kept the receipt. Nobody presented the box as though it contained the answer to Kael's origins.

***

Kael arrived late in the day to collect the woman.

Lyra watched him stop in front of the notebooks.

“You didn't need to have these certified for my sake,” he said.

“I didn't do it only for you,” she replied. “I wanted to know that if someone challenges what I wrote tomorrow, I can show what the pages looked like today. And I wanted to ask whether you'd like to read them, or prefer I keep them put away.”

Kael took his time answering. Lyra did not interrupt.

“I want to read them,” he said at last. “But not all tonight.”

The woman agreed.

Lyra offered to help arrange the next trip if the civil registry ever requested a formal presentation. She promised no access to restricted archives and did not claim to have witnessed events that occurred before she knew them.

When Kael turned toward her, he looked tired.

“Do you think a record can prove why someone chose a job?”

“It can show when they applied, who accepted them, and what opportunities were available,” Lyra answered. “What it can't do is choose for that person afterward.”

Kael gave a brief laugh.

“That sounds like something Dalia would say.”

“Then you ought to listen to her more often.”

They said goodbye beneath the dock lamps.

Lyra watched Kael pick up the notebooks without the woman who had cared for him needing to ask. It wasn't evidence of origin, or obedience, or destiny.

It was a decision Lyra had just witnessed.

And so when the woman thanked her for accompanying them, Lyra answered with a certainty that didn't depend on remembering any lost moment:

“I didn't do anything for you today that I couldn't have chosen not to do.”

***End of Episode 29.***
