# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 02: Under Two Seals

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Daren Holt, limited third person.

***

The notice arrived as they were clearing the tables from a hearing room.

Daren Holt recognized the letterhead of the department that had submitted the restricted report on the body presented at the execution. The court held a certified copy of that report. It described an earlier death during a stabilization trial involving a human subject. Examining it had justified further proceedings, but had not yet established the dead person's identity.

The notice announced a reorganization of the issuing facility's archive. The original clinical records would be transferred to another custodial unit over the coming weeks. The department offered to retain their registry numbers but requested that pending applications be resubmitted once the move was complete.

“If they change the boxes and the people responsible, we lose our way of checking this copy,” the court clerk said.

Daren studied the two signatures. Neither authorized the destruction of documents. But there was no packing inventory, no seal register, and no identification of those responsible for the new storage facility.

“We can't dictate how a supranational facility organizes itself,” he replied. “We can request preservation and put our reasons on the record.”

“The prosecution wants us to seize the entire archive.”

“We don't even know what's in the entire archive.”

Mara Ellin, the physician who had examined the body, was waiting by the door. She carried an inventory of the samples still held under court order. Daren showed her the notice without removing it from the case file.

“Could you conduct another examination with what you have?” he asked.

“I could repeat certain comparisons, if the court authorizes them. I can't reconstruct an original clinical record from tissue samples. And the seals on those samples don't, by themselves, prove who authorized the use of the body.”

Daren set the pages on the table.

They had corrected an erroneous public certification. That decision did not entitle him to treat everything else as settled.

“I'll request a preservation hearing,” he said. “With the defense present. And an explicit response from the original custodian.”

***

The lawyer for one of the people under investigation spoke before Daren had finished his submission.

“My client cannot challenge a report to which he has insufficient access. We will not accept the indefinite preservation of secret documents to support an accusation that has no end.”

The judge waited for him to finish.

Daren had prepared three requests. He read them without adding a fourth.

First, preserve the certified copy and the record of how it had reached the court, without releasing either to the public. Second, ask the issuing authority to freeze the relevant originals until any transfer could be documented. Third, permit an authorized representative of the defense to examine the relevant portions under protective measures, with an opportunity to identify inconsistencies and request expert analysis.

“Why aren't you asking for every file in the program?” the judge asked.

“Because we haven't established that they concern this death. We have a batch identifier, a compatible timeline, and a biological profile. We need to verify the source and the authorizations for transferring the remains. We do not need the medical history of everyone who passed through that department.”

The prosecutor objected that the document might be altered during the reorganization.

“That's a risk we must prevent,” Daren replied, “not an alteration we can claim has happened without evidence.”

The defense requested a copy of the sample inventory and the method used to connect the samples to the report. Mara explained which results could be reproduced, which material would be consumed by further testing, and why any additional use required authorization.

The judge issued a narrow ruling.

The court copy would remain under two seals, subject to periodic review. The parties would gain access to relevant material through an adversarial procedure, with unrelated personal information redacted. The cooperation office would send an urgent preservation request to the external custodian, specifying exactly which entries were required. There would be no forced search outside the court's jurisdiction and no indiscriminate opening of storage facilities.

“How long will these protections last?” the lawyer asked.

“Until the review date established in the order,” the judge answered. “Confidentiality must be justified, just as access must.”

Daren felt the result fell short of what the investigation wanted.

It was also the first answer he could defend without inventing powers the court did not possess.

***

The custodian's message arrived the next day through the cooperation channel.

Daren went to the court archive to witness its entry into the record. The reply acknowledged the registry entries described in the request, agreed to preserve the originals temporarily in their current location, and suspended their transfer pending a joint inventory review. It did not disclose the names of trial participants or grant access to the facility.

“At least they won't change shelves while we're asking questions,” the clerk said.

“The reply says ‘current location,’ not ‘shelves.’ Let's not add what it doesn't certify.”

She made a weary face.

“Sometimes I forget who I'm talking to.”

There was an additional condition. The custodian would allow an authorized certifying officer to inspect the seals and entry register, but required the appointment to be agreed upon by both jurisdictions. The court office proposed someone other than those who had prepared the original medical report.

Daren approved the approach. The same authority should not be asked to certify its own work without an independent means of checking it.

While the record was being prepared, Nela Ordan sent an inquiry through her employment adviser. She wanted to know whether her testimony about the conscious man in the lobby would be made public when the case's confidentiality restrictions were partially lifted.

Daren answered no. The public ruling did not authorize publication of her address or details that could reveal her location. The parties could examine her testimony under safeguards, but the city's interest in Taren's whereabouts did not turn a witness into property of the case file.

Only then did he return to the medical report.

A note in the margin of the reproduction referred to a receipt for human remains. He had not overlooked it: the document had already appeared among the earlier requests. What was new was a notation in the preservation notice identifying the department responsible for it.

The receipt was not in the clinical archive they had just secured.

It belonged to the administrative delivery register of a different office.

Daren noted the agency and asked whether it could issue a certification without protected names.

The answer arrived before the end of the day. The office retained the incoming entry, but the outgoing copy was recorded under an order issued by a contracted transport unit.

“Another custodian,” the clerk said.

“Another question,” Daren corrected.

They had no new proof of the dead person's identity, nor the signature of whoever had ordered the removal of the remains. They did, at least, have a preserved clinical original and an independent administrative trail.

Daren closed the file.

This time, if anyone denied that the body had left the facility, there would be two different places where they could ask the same question.

***End of Episode 02.***
