# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 10: No Single Hand

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Lyra Saren, limited third person.

***

The messenger arrived with the receipt Lyra had been expecting—and an order no one had asked for.

At the third dock, workers were unloading grain sacks while Ema checked the weight of each cart. The driver laid an acknowledgment on the table for the correspondence sent to the regional office: Sena Vard's envelope had been received, opened by authorized personnel, and entered into the inquiry concerning a station whose reopening remained suspended.

The second sheet bore a different seal.

“They asked me to collect every copy of that inquiry and take them to a single archive,” the driver explained. “The official said nothing would get lost that way.”

Lyra read the document.

It named a generic recipient, but contained neither an authorization from the regional office nor an inventory of the records to be collected. The request even included the delivery receipt the driver was obliged to retain as proof of his service.

“Did you give them anything?” she asked.

“No. My route book doesn't get handed over because someone else orders it. He told me to come back tomorrow.”

Ema set the scales aside.

“Then you won't hand it over tomorrow without authorization, either.”

The driver nodded, though his face showed concern.

“It's a major client. If they refuse me the next contract, I won't earn enough to keep the vehicle running.”

Lyra knew that kind of pressure. No one had to threaten violence to make a worker surrender evidence. It was enough to remind him who paid for the journeys.

She requested a certified copy of the demand and of the driver's delivery acknowledgment. As a party to his own transport service, he could provide both, as long as he disclosed no protected correspondence belonging to others.

“I'm not taking your route book,” Lyra said. “We need an office to confirm in writing whether this order is part of a legitimate procedure.”

***

Sena received her at the association's office, where one of the lamps had been turned off to conserve power.

“The inquiry arrived,” she said. “The station has requested a review of two versions of a return instruction. No maneuver has been carried out. The regional authority holds the technical file.”

Lyra placed the supposed single-archive request on the table.

Sena read it carefully.

“There's no statement of Safety's authority here, and no transfer order. I don't even know whether the named office exists as a receiving unit. It might be a badly worded administrative consolidation request.”

“And if it isn't?”

“Then the risk is greater. But we can't decide that from the tone of the paper.”

Sena explained what she could show Lyra: a summary of the inquiry without destination references, the notice suspending reopening, and the regional office's public registration number. The station kept the original procedures and the versions needing comparison in its own storage facility. Nobody had authorized shipping them to the docks.

“What I sent from here was background from the hearing,” Sena said. “Not an order to move originals.”

“Someone wants to gather every receipt.”

“Someone is requesting them. We don't yet know why, or whether they have the power to do so.”

Lyra thought about her own message to Sena, written in Selivar, whose moment of creation she could no longer remember. She had accepted that a document could prove a past choice without restoring the experience of making it. She would not let someone else confuse preserving a document with owning its meaning.

“Can we protect proof of delivery through a channel separate from the technical file?” she asked.

Sena consulted the list of authorized custodians.

“The regional office will preserve what it received. The driver can lodge his transport record with an independent certifying officer. Two different sources. Neither requires the other custodian to hand over everything.”

“Would a copy of my letter be enough?”

“Your letter proves what you asked for. The driver's book proves what he transported and who tried to change the delivery. Let's not mix them up.”

***

Seris Hal received the driver, Lyra, and Ema in a narrow room beside her records office.

She would not accept an opened package without an inventory. She asked the messenger to explain which documents belonged to him and why he had the right to lodge them. He produced his route book, the delivery acknowledgment, and the collection request. There were no operating reports or travelers' medical records among them.

“I can certify that these materials were presented today, in this condition, by this person,” Seris said. “Not that everything written in them is true.”

“That's all we're asking,” the driver replied.

“And I cannot determine whether the other office has jurisdiction. You'll need to bring that question before the proper authority.”

The certifying officer examined the pages. She recorded their numbering, visible ink, damage to the paper, and signatures the driver said he recognized. She removed no pages from the book. She authorized a certified reproduction of the relevant pages and returned the original register to its owner with a record of its presentation. The copy would be deposited under seal, subject to her office's access rules.

Lyra paid part of the fee. Ema insisted on recording the expense separately from the shelter's accounts.

“I don't want helping him to end up looking like we're buying his silence,” she said.

The driver looked at her.

“I'll pay my share, too. It's my work they're trying to alter.”

Seris sealed the package and recorded the date.

There was nothing heroic about the room. A small lamp, a worn stamp, and the noise of a cart struggling to turn in the street.

Yet Lyra felt they had prevented something: the driver no longer had to choose between keeping his receipt and having someone believe his word.

***

The regional office replied the next day.

It confirmed that the station's technical file remained in its custody and that no order had authorized its removal. An incident review had been opened to determine who issued the single-archive request. The office asked that the delivery records be kept intact, but did not demand that Lyra send them without an inventory.

Sena read the communication beside Lyra.

“Now we have two things independent of one another,” she said. “They preserve the technical background they received. Seris preserves proof of delivery and of the attempt to collect the receipts. Neither office can silently rewrite the other's history.”

“Does that prove they meant to hide the file?”

“It proves someone asked to gather its records without demonstrating authority. Intent still needs investigating.”

Lyra accepted the distinction.

A little later, the driver returned to the docks with news. The person who had requested collection had not appeared. Instead, another communication had arrived, this time naming a different department and making a broader demand. It requested the complete file, including the original supporting records and custody receipts, before the scheduled inspection.

It contained no verifiable authorization number.

Ema looked at the sheet, then at Lyra.

“Do we hand it to Seris?”

“First we ask the person who received it to preserve the original. Then we notify the regional office through the channel it provided.”

The driver said he would do it. He no longer needed to pretend he could resolve a dispute between offices on his own.

Lyra stepped outside.

She had fulfilled her promise to Sena long before that afternoon. The missing memory had not returned. Nor had she acquired the right to act as judge or guardian of every document she touched.

But a demand for delivery had failed because two custodians had refused to become one.

And someone whose authority they still had not verified was continuing to try to bring them together.

***End of Episode 10.***
