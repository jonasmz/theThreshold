# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 01: The Link Without an Owner

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, limited third person.

***

The letter did not contain an answer. It contained a deadline.

Kael Veyran read it while water boiled in the kitchen. He had asked an independent office to explain why his employment record still contained a reference to the Mirror Index. The office did not deny the series existed, nor did it dispute the match between a chronological key and his civil birth record. It warned of something less dramatic: during a records migration, the link had been left outside the inventory of designated data custodians.

*If no department acknowledges responsibility for the link, the auxiliary reference will be purged at the end of the review period.*

Kael set the kettle aside.

An auxiliary reference might mean nothing. It might also be the only trace of the office that had entered a piece of information into his file. Erasing it without recording the change would make it impossible to determine how it had gotten there.

He read the signatures again. The Continuity Commission held the restricted index in custody. Personnel held his employment history. Neither had certified who was responsible for the connection between them.

The letter set a deadline for objections. It did not authorize Kael to open the index, and it did not say his name appeared inside it.

On the table lay a delivery schedule he had prepared for Ema. A vendor's cry drifted through the window, followed by the squeal of the first tram. Kael was supposed to take the schedule to the docks before noon.

He touched the folder containing his childhood records. It preserved an account of a rainy afternoon he could no longer remember experiencing. He had learned how much it could hurt to lose something that remained written down somewhere else.

The water cooled while he copied the deadline onto a sheet of paper.

This time, he would not let a record disappear without asking who had decided it was expendable.

***

At the review office, a clerk slid a two-page form toward Kael.

“You may object to the deletion of your personal link,” she explained. “You cannot use this procedure to request the commission's protected documents.”

“That's what I'm trying to do.”

“Your statement asks us to identify the office responsible for creating the link. That's different from asking us to preserve the entire series.”

Kael studied the first box.

“If I check ‘preservation,’ what exactly will be preserved?”

“The field in your employment record, its modification history, and any existing acknowledgments of access. Not the files that field may point to.”

“And who keeps the working copy?”

“The office that resolves the incident, under inventory control. We can order the data frozen while jurisdiction is determined, but we cannot certify that its contents are true.”

A pane of scratched glass separated them across the counter. Behind her came the thump of rubber stamps and the rattle of a fan that stopped every few revolutions. Kael remembered how often he had been the one sitting on that side of a desk, explaining what a request did and did not permit.

“I also want the date someone proposed deleting the link entered into the record,” he said.

The clerk looked up from the form.

“That we can record.”

She pointed him to the section governing future corrections. An authorized amendment had to preserve the previous entry, the reason for the change, and the name of the person who made it. It was no absolute guarantee against loss, but it meant the loss could be challenged afterward.

Kael submitted his objection with a copy of his civil birth record—not as proof that he belonged to the Mirror Index, but to explain his direct interest in the key. He handed over only the relevant page. The clerk returned the rest.

“Why haven't you attached the complete letter from your employment file?”

“Because it contains information you don't need to decide this.”

She stamped his receipt.

The dry crack of the stamp sounded absurdly small compared with everything Kael wanted to know.

“The provisional measure is subject to review,” she warned. “We'll notify you if it's granted. Do not assume the deadline has been suspended until you receive the ruling.”

Kael put the acknowledgment away.

On his way out, he saw a woman arguing about an error in her pension seniority. She carried three certificates, and no one could find the record connecting one job to the next. She was not asking to uncover any institution's secrets. She was asking for the years she had worked to remain hers.

Kael waited until someone attended to her before asking again where he could check the status of his petition.

***

Dalia Ferren accepted the receipt with a less severe expression than Kael had expected.

“For once, you didn't try to solve a jurisdictional gap by appointing yourself custodian,” she said.

“I don't have the original.”

“Not having it rarely stops you when you think a question deserves an answer.”

Kael accepted the rebuke.

Dalia read the warning about deletion. Then she asked to see the two earlier notices: the partial certification that had revealed the name of the series, and the request for verification filed at the end of the investigation.

“We need to separate three things,” she said. “The existence of the Mirror Index, the link retained by Personnel, and the interpretation of the date. Only the first has been certified as the name of an archival collection. Only the second appears in your personal data. The third still allows for mutually incompatible explanations.”

“I know.”

“I want you to put that in your statement.”

Kael picked up his pen.

The lawyer also told him to make a secondary request: if the reviewer could not determine jurisdiction before the deadline, the office should at least preserve the record of the dispute and the audit trail. It was not an order to seize documents or permission to examine other people's records.

“And if Personnel says it isn't responsible?” he asked.

“Then let them say so under signature. The commission, too. The absence of a responsible office must not, by itself, become a way of erasing the question.”

The office clock struck eleven.

Kael remembered his promise to deliver Ema's schedule. Dalia noticed his haste as he gathered the papers.

“Another hearing?”

“A delivery at the docks. It's not a case file.”

“I'm glad obligations like that still exist.”

***

Ema found Kael beside the crates of blankets, checking departure times with a driver. Reduced supplies meant the shipment had to be split between two vehicles. Neither could use a closed strategic route, and the extra fuel would have to come out of the shelter's budget.

“I thought you weren't coming,” she said.

“I had paperwork to deal with.”

“Paperwork can wait, too, when other people are counting on you.”

Kael said nothing. He helped shift a crate and checked that the list of destinations matched the actual cargo. One wheel was loose; the driver refused to leave until it was tightened.

No stamp could solve that problem.

They searched for a tool and lost part of their allotted departure window. When the vehicle finally pulled away, Ema noted the delay so the shelter would know what to expect.

“Getting the date right doesn't make the supplies arrive,” she said. “But it keeps people from waiting for nothing.”

Kael stood watching the road for a moment.

In his pocket were two identical dates, written on documents that did not explain their relationship. He had spent the morning trying to ensure the link between them could not be erased as a matter of routine.

He returned home at dusk.

A certified notice from the review office was waiting for him. The provisional suspension of deletion had been granted for the field in his employment record and its associated traces, subject to a time limit and inventory control. The Mirror Index itself remained beyond his access.

It was a narrow victory. Enough to let him sleep.

Then he read the final line.

*The Continuity Commission confirms custody of the series but does not acknowledge issuing the reference entered into the employment record. The intermediary department that created the link must be identified.*

Kael laid the page on the table.

There was no longer an immediate risk of that trace being erased.

Now he knew someone had left a documentary doorway between two offices—and neither would admit to having built it.

***End of Episode 01.***
