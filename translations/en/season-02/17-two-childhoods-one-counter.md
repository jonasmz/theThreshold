# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 17: Two Childhoods, One Counter

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, limited third person.

***

The girl across the counter didn't want a birth certificate. She wanted them to stop calling her grandfather unknown.

Kael Veyran was waiting his turn at the civil registry when he heard her say it. She looked about ten. Beside her, a gray-haired woman held a fee receipt and a worn photograph.

“He's in the register,” the woman said. “What we need is an explanation of why the new copy doesn't have the surname recorded in the earlier entry.”

The clerk asked them to wait for the historical records reviewer.

Kael looked at his own queue number. He was there for a reason similar in form, though not in substance: he wanted to learn what had become of a supplementary register referenced by the documentary guardianship code from his childhood. His birth certificate remained valid. The woman who raised him had appeared in the register from the beginning. What was missing was an explanation of a verification process kept outside the ordinary civil records.

The woman turned and noticed the folder he was carrying.

“Did they change something in yours, too?” she asked.

“I'm still trying to find out what an old notation means.”

“Half this office seems to exist for that.”

The girl opened her hand to reveal a smooth stone painted blue.

“My grandfather taught me how to look for these stones after the rain.”

Kael nodded. There had been a time when he would have replied by describing an afternoon of raindrops striking metal and wood, and an orange shared in a kitchen. He still had those facts in his own notes, but he could no longer relive the memory.

“It's a beautiful stone,” he said.

Satisfied, the girl put it away.

Kael wondered why that exchange hurt more than the archive's written response.

***

The historical reviewer saw the family first.

Kael stayed in the corridor and heard only what the woman chose to tell him afterward. The official had found two different spellings of the grandfather's surname. One appeared in the childhood residence register, where he had been recorded decades earlier. The other was in an employment register based on a faulty transcription.

They had not discovered another identity. They had found a possible error that required comparison with the originals.

“And you?” the woman asked when Kael's name was called.

“My case may take a while.”

“They all can.”

Kael went in with Dalia Ferren, who had come to accompany him on his limited request.

The civil registry supplied a partial answer: the supplementary register had existed, but its books were transferred in two separate batches in successive years. One batch had a documented receipt; for the other, only the receiving department's code survived.

“Which batch contains my reference?” Kael asked.

“We won't know until we compare the transfer index,” the reviewer said. “The entries are grouped by transfer date, not by the names of the people concerned.”

“Can we request that comparison?”

“Yes, under two conditions. The review cannot disclose other minors' entries, and the custodian must certify which books were received even if your entry isn't found.”

Dalia asked the official to put those conditions in writing.

Kael thought of his employment record and the missing transmission certificate. It would have been easy to assume that any old register with an absent signature must be connected to that other problem. There was no proof.

“I'd like to keep the two procedures separate,” he said. “The one concerning my civil birth record and the one concerning my Personnel file.”

The reviewer seemed surprised that Kael did not want to combine them.

“Of course. Similar archival methods are not sufficient grounds for transferring information between case files.”

***

In the corridor, the gray-haired woman was still waiting for her receipt. Her granddaughter had drawn a house with oversized gutters on the back of a payment slip.

“They found two versions of the surname,” the woman explained to Kael. “She thinks they're going to pick one and erase the other.”

“And what do you think?”

“I think we need to know where each came from. Then we can correct whatever needs correcting without pretending the mistake never happened.”

Kael smiled.

“That's more than I've asked of several offices.”

The woman gave him a curious look.

“Really?”

“There's an old mark on my birth record. I still don't know which authority added it.”

“And that's why you came back?”

“Because I want an answer that doesn't depend on what someone assumes about me.”

The girl looked up.

“My grandfather said stories are the things people do, not the letters other people put on them.”

Her grandmother stroked her shoulder.

Kael did not reply immediately. The statement was simple, almost too perfectly suited to his mood. Yet there was something true in the way the girl knew her grandfather through a blue stone rather than an incomplete entry.

“The letters matter, too, when someone needs to collect a pension,” the woman said. “Not every battle can be won with memories.”

Kael was grateful for the correction.

The girl put away her drawing, and her grandmother returned to the counter. Neither asked Kael to stay. The two families had their own affairs, and that separation, too, was a form of respect.

***

Dalia received a notice before they left the building.

The reviewer agreed to process a joint request about procedure, not about personal content: publish the rules governing transfers of supplementary registers and explain how any person could request a search for their own entry without gaining access to anyone else's. There was no need to merge Kael's individual file with that of the family he had met.

“Shall we submit it?” Dalia asked.

Kael thought of the woman who had paid twice for an incorrectly transcribed surname. He could afford a specialized search, but other people would lose working days every time an office sent them to a different repository.

“Yes. Let them publish the procedure. The personal records should remain protected.”

The lawyer filed the request.

Two days later, the office confirmed that it would post general instructions on its notice board and preserve the records of transfers still under review. It gave Kael no information about any other person.

His individual response arrived separately. The first historical custodian had received an index of registers, but his guardianship code appeared in a section marked “review pending.” The office did not explain why and referred the inquiry to a protection authority that had yet to issue a decision.

Kael placed the letter beside the birth record naming the woman who had cared for him.

His childhood had not split into two simply because two offices had been involved in it.

But another childhood—that of a girl carrying a blue stone—had shown him something his own questions had not.

A correction meant nothing if making it erased the life the record was supposed to serve.

***End of Episode 17.***
