# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 11: The Last Reserve

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Sera Nhal, limited third person.

***

The first warning didn't come through the communicator.

It came from the sound of glasses beginning to rattle in the control room.

Sera Nhal looked up from the test bench. She had placed the uncertified replacement plate under lock and had just checked for the third time that nobody had removed it from storage. On the other side of the wall, the pump had been operating at reduced load for days.

The glasses rattled again.

Her colleague appeared in the doorway.

“The diversion to the medical storage facility is losing pressure. They're asking us to keep the cooling circuit running until the replacement arrives by road.”

Sera checked the readings. The distribution system shared a secondary feeder with the pumps and several service facilities, but not all those loads fell under her authority. A loss of hydraulic pressure could weaken cooling in certain heat exchangers. It did not mean the entire facility had stopped refrigerating its contents.

“What are they measuring over there?” she asked.

“Return temperature outside the usual range. The room thermometer still reads acceptable.”

“Then we have a warning. We don't have a resolved emergency.”

Sera pulled on her gloves and asked for the morning's recording strips. Two sensors showed similar trends; one had been calibrated the previous week, and the other was due for inspection. There was no justification for choosing whichever one offered the most reassuring number.

The shift supervisor arrived with a proposal.

“We can divert power from the pumping branch to the medical facility for an hour.”

“And the lower district's water?”

“The reserve should hold.”

Sera remembered the reserve calculations. They applied to a specific level of demand, not to an hour chosen for convenience.

“I need the current reading and authorization from Services. I also want to know how much backup capacity the facility has of its own. If we spend our reserve so they don't have to spend theirs, all we've done is move the problem.”

***

The medical storage facility smelled of damp metal and disinfected cloth.

Sera entered through the maintenance access, accompanied by an authorized operator. A white line marked the corridor she was allowed to inspect. Beyond it were sealed chambers whose contents were not covered by her work order.

Ada Rehn, the refrigeration supervisor, showed her a power chart.

“The backup compressor has enough charge for one short cycle,” she explained. “After that, its accumulator and refrigerant supply need to recover. Road transport was supposed to bring a replacement seal yesterday.”

“It didn't arrive?”

“A different component arrived. We refused to install it because the gasket doesn't match the equipment specifications.”

Sera looked at the part lying in a tray. It wasn't the quarantined plate from her workshop. It had a different number, a different function, and a different origin. The supply error was real; declaring a shared conspiracy would still be a leap.

Ada indicated two gauges.

“I want to know whether the drop we're seeing comes from the cooling water or from our own valve.”

Sera requested permission to measure at the shared connection points. They made their checks without opening the chambers or changing the load settings. The external return dropped below its expected margin whenever the municipal pump entered its recovery phase.

“The intervals coincide,” Sera said. “I'll take another reading against your reference pattern. That's not enough for me to order anything inside those chambers.”

A metal wheel squeaked in the corridor.

An attendant was pushing a small crate toward a waiting area. He asked Ada whether deliveries to a clinic should be suspended.

“Not yet,” she answered. “But we'll warn them the dispatch window may change.”

The man looked at Sera as though she had brought an authorization.

“I can't promise we'll maintain the temperature indefinitely,” Sera clarified. “We're establishing how much margin is left.”

The attendant pushed on. The crate contained no answer for Sera. It contained something another person needed to receive before it became unusable.

***

The second measurement confirmed a localized loss.

The medical circuit was still within its safe operating range, but its margin narrowed whenever the municipal reservoir demanded recovery. Sera took the finding to the operations room. Forcing the main pump was out of the question: its worn valve had not been repaired, and the new plate still lacked a certified test.

The shift supervisor proposed cutting off deliveries to the lower district for forty minutes.

“That's fewer users than the clinics,” he said.

Sera felt the calculation trying to turn one need into permission to disregard another.

“Don't choose based on head count without consulting Services and Health. Some homes have patients who depend on water. We don't know who they are, and it's not our place to ask for their names.”

She prepared three alternatives for municipal coordination: a limited, announced reduction in nonessential flow; measured use of the medical facility's own reserve; and postponement of an industrial load that could be rescheduled with compensation. None avoided every cost.

Services approved the first measure within its jurisdiction; Health agreed to draw on part of the medical reserve under independent monitoring. The industrial workshop received notice and instructions for filing a claim.

Ada called again.

“Our accumulator can support the approved interval,” she said. “But if the road shipment is delayed again, we won't have another identical cycle.”

“We'll record that. If the margin drops ahead of schedule, we stop the maneuver and activate alternative distribution.”

Sera named no one as responsible for the malfunction. Her instruments could describe a pressure drop and the equipment's capacity, not the decisions made by those who had ordered the wrong part.

The maneuver began with a staged reduction.

The return line shuddered. Then it stabilized a few points above the prescribed limit. Sera continued monitoring through the entire interval and refused to increase demand when a small margin became available.

At the storage facility, Ada confirmed that the temperature was holding.

Not that the risk had disappeared.

***

By dusk, the vehicle carrying the correct replacement arrived.

Sera was present as Ada checked its identification and test report. The transport seal matched the order. The component came with the document missing from the previous shipment, but it still needed to be checked against the specific equipment before installation.

“We can complete the repair with the supplier tomorrow,” Ada said. “We're not improvising a replacement today with the reserve depleted.”

Sera approved the caution. They had protected the temperatures at the expense of water flow, accumulator capacity, labor hours, and an industrial operation that would lose production. None of that turned their temporary fix into a permanent solution.

As they signed the shared readings, Ada set a second sheet aside.

“One customer wants to extend refrigerated custody for another week,” she said. “It's unrelated to this failure. The order comes under a different agreement, and the authorization still doesn't identify who will be responsible if our reserves drop again.”

“What are they storing?”

“I can't tell you. And we don't need to know to calculate the power requirement. We need a custodian willing to accept the cost and the criteria for interrupting service.”

Sera looked at the paper without touching it.

“Then don't sign a guarantee of continued service that your equipment can't make.”

Ada nodded and put it away.

Outside, Sera heard the pump again, still exhausted. It wasn't the sound of victory. It was the sound of a machine whose next shift would need a genuine part, rested operators, and someone willing to pay the price of keeping things cold.

***End of Episode 11.***
