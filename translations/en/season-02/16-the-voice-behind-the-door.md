# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 16: The Voice Behind the Door

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Daren Holt, limited third person.

***

The boy's first question was whether he could leave without giving his name.

Daren Holt sat across a small table from him in the judicial guidance office. The room was not part of the restricted case concerning the body in the square. Daren had asked to use it because the young worker had come seeking protection and feared losing his job if his statement circulated among contractors.

“You can stop the interview,” Daren answered. “To request a formal protective measure, we'll have to establish your identity before the proper authority. That doesn't mean your name has to appear in a public announcement.”

The employment adviser seated beside the boy confirmed that she had explained this. She had been retained through the haulers' assistance fund, not by the prosecution. The young man, an assistant on the cart that broke down near the old bridge, was named Tomel Rian.

Daren wrote the name on a restricted cover sheet, separate from the general summary. He did not ask for the route notebook Tomel had kept in his own custody.

“Tell me what you remember, without starting with what you think the man wanted,” he said.

Tomel pressed his fingers against his knees.

“At the toll station, he asked whether we were still making copies of receipts. I told him the boss kept his route book. Then he said there'd be fewer contracts for people who insisted on holding on to old paperwork.”

“Were those his exact words?”

“Not exactly. He said, ‘Business will remember those who make deliveries difficult.’ I remember that part.”

“How did you know he meant the book?”

“Because he nodded toward it. It was next to the seat.”

Daren let the silence stand. He was not going to turn the boy into a witness in a case he knew nothing about. Tomel had not said the man worked for the office that had requested consolidated records. Nor had he seen the man take any documents.

“Can you describe him without my showing you any names?” Daren asked.

Tomel gave a general description and admitted that he might be confusing the man's coat with that of another traveler. Daren recorded the uncertainty.

The adviser laid a hand on the table.

“What my client is asking for is the right to keep working while the incident is investigated.”

That was the heart of the request. Not a mysterious identity. A paycheck.

***

The guidance office recorded the account as a preliminary report and, with Tomel's authorization, forwarded it to the territorial court empowered to address worker intimidation and to the labor inspectorate. Daren had no authority to attach it to his criminal investigation.

An official wanted to include the full account in the case concerning the square because both matters involved requests for documents.

“No,” Daren said. “First we need to establish relevance and obtain a reasoned decision. The fact that both cases mention receipts doesn't make them the same case.”

Mara Ellin was waiting outside to discuss the examination of the original release order for the remains. Daren met with her in another office after closing the door to the employment interview.

“The territorial custodian confirmed that the seal on the register hasn't changed,” Mara explained. “The codes will be compared with the parties notified. The defense has already submitted its questions.”

“Any new names?”

“None authenticated. A clinical report is no substitute for identification.”

Daren heard the file cabinet creak beneath the table. The court was still trying to keep two difficult promises: preserve the evidence and avoid turning absent people into convenient explanations.

“We have another access problem,” Mara said. “The department that requested preferential delivery still hasn't established its authority. It asked to attend the inspection.”

“That's for the custodial authority and the judge to decide. It isn't an invitation I can issue.”

Mara nodded.

After she left, Daren looked at the physically separate folders. The arrangement might be slower. It was also the only way to prevent one new statement from becoming permission to open every other file.

***

The territorial court granted a protective hearing the following day.

Daren attended as a procedural liaison at the adviser's request and with the magistrate's authorization. He did not conduct the questioning or represent the boy. Tomel chose to testify from a separate room with controlled access, while his employer and the inspectorate would receive only the portion of the ruling they needed to comply with it.

The magistrate examined the sequence: the date at the toll station, the route sheet the boy voluntarily presented, the station's shift entry, and his statement about the words he had heard. The toll record confirmed the cart had passed through. It did not establish who had spoken or what had been said.

The company's representative requested the complainant's name so it could respond.

“This is not a proceeding to impose a sanction on the company,” the magistrate replied. “It concerns a provisional preventive measure. If adversarial proceedings are opened over alleged wrongdoing, you will have access under the applicable safeguards.”

Tomel kept his eyes lowered.

The court ordered his identity protected from publication and unnecessary commercial communications, directed the labor inspectorate to monitor for retaliation, and provided a protected contact channel. It did not prohibit dismissing anyone for any reason, and it did not find the unidentified man at the toll station guilty. The company could respond and submit records.

Outside, Tomel asked whether the order guaranteed that he would keep his job.

“I can't promise you that,” the adviser said. “It allows you to file a complaint if you suffer consequences connected to your statement and requires the situation to be reviewed.”

“The boss already knows it was me.”

Daren did not contradict him. In a small crew, anonymity had practical limits.

“The ruling protects your information from unnecessary circulation,” he said. “Real protection also depends on someone checking what happens to your shifts tomorrow.”

Tomel took a deep breath.

“Then I want them to record when I worked this week.”

The adviser opened her notebook. It was a precise request, and this time an office was obliged to answer it.

***

That afternoon, Daren received two notices.

The first came from the labor court: the company had supplied shift records from before and after the statement, with no changes recorded so far. The inspectorate would continue monitoring. The second came from the office holding the original release order for the remains: the agreed examination would proceed, though its schedule had to be adjusted to allow the parties to attend.

There was no evidence connecting the two documents.

Daren prepared to answer them separately.

Before he closed his office, the court clerk showed him a petition from Tomel's adviser. It requested that if another case ever needed the boy's testimony, neither his name nor his statement be forwarded without prior notice, a relevance assessment, and a review of the protective measures.

Daren signed the acknowledgment.

He had spent much of his career believing that testimony was protected by locking it inside a folder. Now he understood that the person who had to go back to work after the folder was closed also needed protection.

He turned off the light in the guidance room.

There was no one behind the door anymore.

Across the city, Tomel would have to report for his next shift carrying the same uncertainty he had brought with him.

***End of Episode 16.***
