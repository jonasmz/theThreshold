# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 32: No Apology Required for Refusing

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, limited third person.

***

The envelope contained no order. It took Kael several minutes to believe that.

It had arrived through Iria Solen, the reviewer responsible for enforcing the access conditions on his individual report. The sender was an operational assessment office, and the letter invited him to undergo voluntary compatibility testing with Network equipment. The outside of the envelope said nothing about why he might have been considered. Inside, the grounds for the invitation were restricted: the office knew from its own historical records that there were individuals associated with an old program, but did not claim to possess his current personal file.

Dalia Ferren had insisted that no communication reach him outside the independent channel.

“This doesn't mean they know everything about you,” she warned as she read it. “And it doesn't mean they should. The letter is an invitation delivered to a rights holder under safeguards. We need them to explain how they authorized the contact.”

Kael looked at the signature.

The authority had recognized his right to keep his identity confidential after his artificial origin was established. He had consented to an anonymous notice intended to preserve other records, not to a roster of candidates available for experiments. The office contacting him now said it had used an intermediary contact procedure and would not learn his name unless he chose to respond.

“Can I refuse without even confirming that I'm the person they're looking for?” he asked.

Dalia examined the procedure.

“Yes. The reviewer can communicate your refusal without identifying you. If they try to make your employment or rights depend on your answer, they'll have to explain themselves before the safeguards authority.”

Kael folded the letter.

He didn't want to decide out of fear. He wanted to know exactly what they were asking.

He requested a copy of the general protocol, with other participants' data and strategic locations removed. He also asked who would pay for transport, who would assume responsibility for an accident, and what would happen to any physical or mnemonic information collected.

The office would have to answer those questions before receiving a yes or a no.

***

The protocol arrived in redacted form.

It called for a medical interview, low-power physiological response assessment, association exercises, and simulated technical tasks. It did not authorize a real node operation. Nor could it guarantee that the findings wouldn't prompt a request for a riskier procedure later.

Kael read it at the table where he prepared his employment indexes.

The word *voluntary* appeared six times. The word *withdrawal* appeared only once, at the end, and there was no explanation of how data already collected would be destroyed. One clause permitted results to be retained for “future continuity needs” without specifying who would determine those needs.

Dalia pointed to the section.

“Consent can't be extended indefinitely by a phrase like that. We can ask them to change it.”

“So I can participate afterward?”

“So the next person doesn't receive the same ambiguity. Your decision is separate.”

Kael thought of the woman who had raised him. He had told her what he knew about his origins, and she had not asked him to explain who had sent her to care for him. They talked about the documents in brief moments between ordinary chores, as though the house refused to become an office.

“My body isn't collateral for a repair,” he said.

Dalia said nothing.

Outside the building, a deliveryman was trying to maneuver two crates off a cart whose wheel had become stuck. Kael got up to help him. Together they pushed the wheel free of a crack and carried the crates to the doorway. The deliveryman thanked him and went back to work.

The effort left an ache in Kael's palm.

He had never asked whether his arms met a requirement designed by someone else.

Now he could feel the urge to question every movement in those terms. It struck him as another way of letting the program continue making choices for him.

He returned to the table and wrote a three-line reply.

He did not authorize testing.

***

The office requested a meeting with the reviewer.

Kael agreed to attend through a protected connection, with no image or identifying details visible to the office's representatives. Iria presided and defined what could be included in the minutes: a potentially contacted rights holder had responded to an invitation, and the intermediary procedure would be examined. Nothing else.

A technical official explained that the Network needed operators capable of responding to certain ancient sequences.

“There's a risk of further deterioration if we can't recover our ability to make adjustments,” he said.

“Was the invitation based on any individual test result of mine?” Kael asked.

“We have no authorization to inspect your personal clinical assessment,” Iria replied before the man could answer. “The office must explain its recruitment system without using this channel to infer who's on the other end.”

The official acknowledged that several invitations had gone out through authorized custodians, without a list of names in his office. Each contact's legality would require review.

“If you refuse, we might be losing an opportunity to protect a great many people,” he said.

Kael felt the pressure in the words, even though they weren't phrased as a threat.

“What evidence do you have that my participation would solve the problem?”

“No guarantee. Only a hypothesis worth examining.”

“Then you can't turn that hypothesis into a moral obligation for me.”

Iria recorded his refusal and the absence of any associated penalty. She ordered a review of the data-retention clause before any new invitations were issued. The office retained the right to design protocols, not to demand participants.

When the session ended, Dalia offered him the transcript.

Kael read it. The line recording his answer was accurate. It didn't say the program had failed forever or that he lacked the ability to operate.

It said he did not consent.

***

Tessa Ord received Kael at Personnel about an entirely different matter.

A supervisory archivist position was open on the preservation shift. He had the necessary experience and needed no compatibility certificate or extraordinary examination. The inherited Mirror Index link remained preserved as a technical trace that could not be used in employment decisions.

“I'd like to offer you an interview,” Tessa said. “On the strength of the work you've done here.”

Kael stared at the posting.

“The other office isn't involved?”

“I don't have information about your private affairs. We're reviewing work records. If someone asks for something else, they'll have to establish their authority.”

It was the first offer he'd received since learning his origins that didn't make him feel he was concealing a crucial fact. Not because his origins had stopped mattering, but because he'd found someone who didn't try to turn them into a professional qualification.

He accepted the interview.

At home, he put a copy of his refusal beside his individual report. He didn't want to show it to the woman who had raised him just yet. They had enough to talk about without every conversation ending in front of a form.

He opened a notebook and prepared answers about inventories, paper restoration, and supervising shifts. He could explain what work he wanted to do and where his limits lay. Those questions interested him.

On the last page, he wrote a sentence to remember if the interview became difficult:

*An institution may need help. That doesn't give it the right to choose my life for me.*

He closed the notebook.

The next day, he'd go to talk about his future, not the function someone had designed his beginning to serve.

***End of Episode 32.***
