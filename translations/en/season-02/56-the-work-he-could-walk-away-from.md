# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 56: The Work He Could Walk Away From

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, limited third person.

***

The new contract had a box for recording the reason someone withdrew.

Kael Veyran read it three times. It was an invitation from the conservation team to participate in the next review of instrumental procedures. The proposed work involved comparing public sources and manual editions, identifying which instructions remained valid for isolated benches, and preserving the history of corrections.

The box read: “Withdrawal due to insufficient aptitude.”

“That's not the same as deciding not to continue,” Kael told Tessa Ord.

Tessa frowned.

“There should be an option that doesn't evaluate the person. This work requires no clinical information.”

Weeks earlier, they'd corrected a contract that mixed archival work with biological-response checks. The new proposal no longer included those tests. But an exit clause could turn the right to leave into a record of incapacity.

Kael understood the force of small phrases. He'd spent half his life watching a field in a register outlast everyone who'd tried to explain it.

“If I withdraw because the duties change, because I can't attend, or simply because I no longer want to participate, the file should say so,” he added.

Tessa called in the labor representative. The technical office agreed to replace the box with three options: tasks completed, contract ended at the worker's discretion, and dispute over scope. Each would record paid hours and allow challenges to an inaccurate description.

The colleague who'd feared losing income listened to the agreement.

“Won't that make it harder to justify replacements?” he asked.

“It'll make it harder to invent reasons why someone left,” Kael answered.

He signed the corrected version.

He didn't submit his private report or confirm anything about his origins to anyone.

He had accepted a task.

He hadn't surrendered the right to leave it.

***

The new repository was full of paper.

A group of archivists had to bring together older editions of instrumental references before the laboratories prepared another external examination. The originals belonged to different custodians: a technical school, a municipal archive, and a calibration workshop. They couldn't be housed together without authorization.

Kael arranged a consultation table using identified reproductions. One column recorded the edition date; another, the date of arrival at the repository; a third indicated whether the text had been used for instruction, repair, or merely to describe a method.

Rena Tor arrived from the training repository.

“I found a correction sheet nobody entered in the index,” she said.

Kael read the heading. The supplement clarified the name of a thermal reference and warned that a laboratory result couldn't be extrapolated directly to fatigued installations. It named no human subjects and granted access to no strategic configuration.

“Has the sheet been authenticated?” he asked.

“We have the incoming entry and the edition number. We still need to compare the distribution stamp.”

He added it as “pending verification,” not as an instruction in force.

A young technician wanted to use it immediately in the assembly checklist.

“It describes exactly what we need,” she said.

“It might describe what you need and still be an edition that never applied to your equipment,” Kael answered. “Let's not adopt it until we establish which apparatus it was written for.”

The technician left annoyed. Shortly afterward, she returned with the reference number of her own bench. It didn't match the supplement's.

Kael pointed out the difference without congratulating himself.

What mattered wasn't that he'd stopped someone. It was that both of them could check why they'd reached different decisions.

At the end of the day, Tessa handed him his task report. Kael had completed the provenance index, touched no powered instrument, and kept every reproduction linked to its custodian.

That was the work he'd agreed to do.

***

A Licensing delegate appeared the next day with a request for Kael to attend a hearing.

He wanted Kael to explain why he'd considered an older reference relevant to the instrumental validation program. The invitation asked him to “participate in the demonstration” before the control panel.

Kael requested a definition of that phrase.

The delegate replied that he would only need to point out pages in the manuals. But the demonstration room also contained a low-power bench on which technicians would conduct a test.

“I can explain the catalog in writing or from a separate room,” Kael said. “I don't need to stand beside active equipment to establish which book was printed first.”

“It would be more convincing if you were there.”

“That's a presentation preference, not a requirement of my job.”

The delegate insisted the intervention would be safe. Kael didn't dispute the laboratory's expertise, which belonged to independent specialists, or claim to know the risks of equipment he hadn't inspected.

“Those responsible for safety will decide whether to authorize the test,” he replied. “I'm authorized to speak about documents. I won't let a photograph of me beside the machine become evidence that I took part in its operation.”

Tessa supported the proposal. The archival report would be presented in an earlier session, with questions from the parties and an official record. If the engineers later decided to run a test, they would do so without using the archivist's body as part of the demonstration.

The delegate accepted.

Dalia Ferren received notice only through the channel Kael had authorized for legal advice. There was no reason to disclose the confidential ruling on his origins to the technical office.

When he returned home, the woman who'd raised him found him repairing a lock.

“Was it hard to say no?” she asked.

“Harder to explain that I could still help.”

She watched him try the ordinary key in the door.

“You learned that here, too,” she said.

Kael smiled.

It was a lock he'd chosen to install.

***

The Archive's presentation lasted less time than the meeting that had arranged it.

Kael explained his three documentary sources, the differences between editions, and the limitations of a copy. The specialists asked about a reference table. He displayed the original page and clarified that he couldn't certify the operation of the instrument it described.

The report was accepted as evidence of documentary provenance, not as an engineering opinion.

When the time came for the test, Kael left the room. Tessa stayed with him while the technicians independently verified the safety procedures. He didn't need to hear the results to justify the work he'd done.

Outside, an assistant gave him the final report. His name appeared in the contributions section with an exact description: “comparison of editions and provenance.”

It didn't say operator, patient, or suitable individual.

The last page contained the same withdrawal box he'd challenged at the beginning. Now it allowed someone to record their own choice without labeling it a deficiency.

Kael left it blank. He would keep working as long as the contract respected its limits.

Outside the building, he watched workers load accumulators onto a cart bound for civilian services. They weren't free laboratory components. They had destinations, invoices, and people who would depend on them through the night.

The Network was still damaged. The work of repairing it would be slow, perhaps insufficient.

Under his arm, Kael carried a paper index others could use without knowing the history of his body.

His contribution was small.

It belonged to him.

***End of Episode 56.***
