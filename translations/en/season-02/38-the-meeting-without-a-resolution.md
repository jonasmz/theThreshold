# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 38: The Meeting Without a Resolution

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Maelor Thane, limited third person.

***

The index recorded a meeting. The resolutions register didn't.

Maelor Thane had asked the technical secretariat for the original authorization behind a revision to a rest-period table used in return-system facilities. The preliminary examination confirmed that two editions existed and had circulated at different times; it had not yet established that they applied to the same configuration. Until that could be shown, no numerical discrepancy could be treated as a deliberate alteration of a safe operating limit.

Vessa, the secretary, placed three documents on the table: a meeting notice, an attendance list, and a summary of topics discussed by an old service board. The third line read, “Procedure revision; resolution awaiting transcription.”

“Where's the transcript?” Maelor asked.

“The register jumps to the next item. I've checked the books from that week and the annex index. There's no signed record under that number.”

Talia Mer, who had come to explain the Annex's current readings, studied the meeting notice.

“Does that mean the change was never authorized?”

“It means we haven't found a resolution in this series,” Maelor replied. “An incomplete register doesn't prove there wasn't another record.”

The current table had been used for years. The Council had retained it as a maintenance procedure, even though an alternative copy and present-day measurements suggested that it deserved scrutiny. Maelor knew the old originals had been moved, lost, and reconstructed. He also knew that blaming every gap on an accident was a convenient way to avoid investigating.

“I need the board's transfer inventory,” he said. “And the regulations establishing who had authority to approve this kind of change.”

Vessa closed the folder.

“That could take us into another jurisdiction.”

“Then we'll use the proper door.”

***

The old records room was no longer a meeting room.

A crew was stripping damaged plaster from the ceiling when Maelor arrived with an archivist and a Commission representative. Document boxes occupied temporary shelves raised off the floor so moisture wouldn't reach their bindings.

“We can't stop repairs until you're finished,” the works supervisor said. “If another ceiling panel falls, we'll lose the entire repository.”

The archivist indicated a protected table away from the dust.

“We'll work from the inventory. The originals won't be moved unless it's necessary and authorized.”

Maelor read the transfer register. A series of technical files had moved from the board to the supervisory collection. The entry listed volume, weight, and seal, but no individual titles. Years later, a successor office had returned part of the series; its receipt stamp accounted for one package less.

“Was it lost?” the Commission representative asked.

“I can't say what that package contained,” the archivist answered. “The number might have changed during reclassification. We don't have complete outgoing and incoming indexes here.”

They found a loose label inside a maintenance folder. It bore the meeting number, but the handwriting was modern. An assistant suggested treating it as proof that the minutes had once been there.

Maelor shook his head.

“A new label may reflect a later search. We'll preserve it as evidence of archival work, not as a historical original.”

The works supervisor called down from a ladder: they'd found a leak above another shelf. The archivist requested help moving only the threatened containers, with dispatch and relocation recorded.

Maelor left the table and helped hold the tarp while the crew moved the boxes. Water struck the floor and formed a dark puddle beside his boots.

It wasn't the first time that important documents had depended on the labor of someone paid to fix a roof.

***

The regulations turned up in another department's legal archive.

They did not give a board unilateral authority to change all safety instructions. They distinguished presentation changes, maintenance adjustments for identified equipment, and changes to operating limits affecting strategic facilities. The last category required two signatures: one from the technical officer in charge and another from a supervisory body outside the workshop proposing the change.

“Was the table an operating limit?” Vessa asked.

“That's exactly what the expert needs to assess,” Maelor said. “If it merely changed guidance for a different series, approval may have followed another procedure.”

The legal adviser recommended searching the supervisory file. Its index contained an entry from the same period whose description might correspond to a revision of intervals. But it gave neither a meeting number nor a signed copy of the decision.

Maelor requested preservation and comparison of both series by a competent authority, with copies available to those entitled to participate in the technical review. The Commission agreed to seek cooperation rather than confiscate the legal collection.

An impatient councilor offered a faster solution.

“We could declare the entire supplement invalid and revert to the previous manual.”

Talia interrupted.

“The equipment we have today isn't identical to the older configuration. Applying an old table without studying the replacements could be as dangerous as continuing to use the new one.”

Maelor supported her.

They would maintain the ban on testing and rely on passive monitoring informed by current measurements, not by the age of a document.

“We need to decide how to operate safely today,” he said. “Separately, we need to establish how yesterday's instructions were authorized.”

The councilor fell silent.

It wasn't a dramatic decision.

It avoided turning historical suspicion into improvised technical instruction.

***

Vessa returned with the first report from the comparison of collections.

The supervisory unit acknowledged having incoming records from the relevant period and offered a controlled inspection. It did not confirm that the missing minutes were among them. It required a mandate restricted to the manual review because the repository also held files concerning people and restricted operations.

“Do we accept those conditions?” Vessa asked.

“Yes. We don't need private identities to find out who authorized a technical procedure.”

The updated maintenance report showed that the return discrepancy persisted. Talia had repeated readings without imposing a new load, and independent calibration of the instruments was underway. Every day of waiting cost staff hours and supplies hauled along civilian roads.

Maelor wrote a provisional instruction to Licensing: neither disputed table could serve as authorization for testing until the configuration and safety comparison was complete. Containment and monitoring would continue within restricted margins.

Then he reviewed the list of requested materials.

A historical archive note mentioned that before certain revisions, workshops submitted risk observations through channels separate from the meeting minutes. Their contents were not attached. That wasn't evidence that such a warning existed for this meeting.

“Let's find the safety-notice index,” he told Vessa. “Not just the resolutions index.”

“If we find something, what are you expecting it to say?”

Maelor looked out the window. Night-shift lamps were coming on beyond it, powered by a reserve no office could multiply.

“I'm not expecting a confession. I want to know whether someone described a danger before others had to live with it.”

The missing minutes remained missing.

But their absence was no longer an excuse not to look for the documents that should have accompanied them.

***End of Episode 38.***
