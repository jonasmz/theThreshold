# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 24: A Question Before the Test

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Maelor Thane, limited third person.

***

The first person to refuse a place on the review panel was no enemy of the Council.

She was a physician who had spent twenty years caring for its operators.

“I won't sign a report when the question has already been answered for us,” Mira Desen said, seated across from Maelor Thane. “Your letter seems to ask us how the procedure should continue. First we need to decide whether anyone had the right to begin it.”

Maelor read the document he had drafted again.

He had requested an ethical review of the methods of human continuity associated with a historical resolution that he had finally been allowed to compare under restrictions at the Continuity Commission. The resolution established an institutional choice: developing people capable of responding to certain ancient Network credentials rather than waiting for a technical alternative to be reconstructed. The documents did not yet identify the individuals involved.

“You're right,” he said. “I'll rewrite the mandate.”

Mira set the letter on the table.

“I also need to know who can stop a trial while we deliberate.”

“At facilities under my command, I've prohibited new trials involving people without verifiable consent and independent technical review.”

“And the others?”

“I've asked the authorities responsible to adopt equivalent measures. I can't pretend my signature has force where I have no jurisdiction.”

It was a less dramatic answer than Maelor would have liked to give. It was also the only answer he could defend.

In the corridor, a brief alarm announced a variation in load. Maelor listened as the team adjusted the distribution without opening any branch. The Network still needed maintenance, even though the institution had spent decades confusing its duty to contain the system with a right to use people.

“We can't stop the monitoring,” he added.

“I'm not asking you to,” Mira replied. “I'm asking you not to use it as an excuse to repeat a trial.”

***

The Continuity Commission raised three objections to the revised mandate.

The first concerned jurisdiction: it would not accept a body created by Maelor making unilateral decisions about all Council archives. The second was confidentiality: some records contained information about potentially vulnerable people. The third was budgetary: specialist hours would be taken away from a network already short of personnel.

Maelor did not dismiss any of them out of hand.

“I propose a review panel with a limited mandate,” he said. “A network safety expert, a specialist in the rights of affected individuals, a clinical preservation professional, and a legal expert outside the chain of authorizations under investigation. No one will examine a named personal file without grounds, access controls, and safeguards.”

The Continuity chairwoman indicated the list.

“Who selects representatives of the affected individuals if we're not free to identify them?”

“A safeguards authority that can invite them through a protected channel without disclosing their names to my office. Participation must be voluntary. I won't assume that one person wants to speak for others simply because they share a file category.”

“That could take months.”

“Then the report will have to state which questions it couldn't answer while it waited.”

The technical representative asked whether the ethics panel could observe a controlled operation.

Mira, attending with authorization, interrupted.

“We are not going to authorize trials on people to find out whether the earlier trials were ethical. There are technical test benches, unpowered procedures, and limited simulations we can examine first.”

The representative held her gaze.

“Those may not reproduce the full biological response.”

“Exactly. The fact that a simulation is incomplete doesn't justify turning someone into an instrument without their consent.”

Maelor recorded both positions. He needed a genuine technical evaluation of alternatives, not a condemnation written to sound satisfying or an automatic defense of what had already been decided.

At the end of the meeting, the commission agreed to negotiate the mandate under independent oversight.

It released not a single name.

***

Talia Mer found Maelor at the Western Annex while he was reviewing overtime shifts.

“If you assign our two best technicians to a panel for three weeks, we'll have nobody to cover their shifts,” she said.

The fatigued joint in the shared return line remained under passive monitoring and restricted operating conditions. The Needles were still closed to new strategic operations. Maintenance could not be postponed for the sake of a moral discussion.

Maelor worked through the hours with her.

“We can schedule consultation shifts instead of pulling them both at once,” he proposed. “I'll request a specific budget for people to cover their operating duties.”

“From which account?”

“The Commission's responsibility-review budget. If they refuse, that refusal will have to be justified. I won't charge the cost to Safety without declaring it.”

Talia studied the shift chart.

“Some technicians took part in calibrating systems years ago that will now be investigated. Will they be treated as guilty?”

“Not merely because they worked on them. They must be able to report what they observed and what they didn't know. If anyone bears responsibility, that must be established through the appropriate process.”

“Then put in writing that they won't have to surrender their medical records to keep their jobs.”

Maelor did.

When the room fell quiet, he was struck by the fact that such an elementary protection required a special order. For years, the system had demanded that its operators bear risks no machine could enter as a debt.

Talia took the signed sheet.

“And if a technician refuses to testify?”

“We won't treat the refusal as grounds for suspicion. The review will have to distinguish voluntary testimony, professional duties to report safety concerns, and the right to privacy.”

The engineer filed the paper beside the shift calendar.

It was the first time Maelor had seen an ethical safeguard occupy a concrete line in the maintenance budget.

***

The final mandate arrived two days later.

The review body would have initial access to redacted resolutions and procedures, custody inventories, and safety criteria. On the basis of a reasoned ruling, it could ask competent custodians to examine other materials under protective measures. It would not receive a general list of individuals and could not order interventions involving them. It was tasked with evaluating consent, guardianship, risks, technical alternatives, and avenues of redress.

Its first report did not have to offer an operational solution.

It had to determine which questions were legitimate before asking anything of a person.

Maelor signed the portion within his authority and forwarded the remaining sections for territorial ratification. He made public only the existence of an independent review of safety procedures and rights, without identifying restricted archives or individuals. The Continuity Commission retained custody of the annex explaining its origins.

Vessa checked the notifications.

“One acceptance is missing,” she said. “The office holding the training protocols demands to know the outcome before it decides whether to show them.”

“It cannot control a conclusion just because it holds a source.”

“It also has confidentiality obligations.”

“Then let it present its grounds to the reviewing authority, not to me.”

Maelor picked up the last sheet in the file. It listed documents whose access remained unresolved and the preservation measures that had to continue in the meantime.

It wasn't a complete victory. The report would take time, staffing would cost money, and the facilities would continue operating under limits no commission could repair.

But at least the usual question would no longer come first: how much capacity could be extracted from a person?

The first question would be whether that person had been free to say no.

***End of Episode 24.***
