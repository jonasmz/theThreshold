# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 07: Two Dates for the Same Field

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, limited third person.

***

The notice said they had found the error. It did not say which of the two documents was wrong.

Kael Veyran opened it after returning from his medical appointment. The doctor had confirmed that he could resume ordinary duties as long as he observed rest periods. He had not promised to restore the rainy afternoon Kael had lost during Transit. Kael filed the report with his medical papers, away from the Personnel records.

The review office was calling a short hearing to correct the creation date of an inherited reference in his employment history. The notice compared two records.

In the reproduction Personnel had supplied when Kael first filed his claim, the link had been added during a migration that took place after his appointment.

In a control sheet discovered only recently, the same field carried a date years before he had joined the Archive.

Both dates were labeled *link creation date*. They could not simply describe the same operation.

Kael forced himself to read the cautionary section. One entry might date the creation of the reference in the originating system; the other, its transfer into his employment file. Or an operator might have selected the wrong column. None of those possibilities proved the Mirror Index described Kael.

He wrote three questions in the margin of a blank page: who generated each field, what action did they call “creation,” and where was the source record from before the migration?

Then he took the tram.

A woman argued with the fare collector because the line had reduced its frequency to save energy. Kael gave up his seat to an older man carrying repair tools. The man thanked him and explained that he had started a double shift to cover for a sick colleague.

Kael thought of how dates organized shifts and wages. They could also determine whether someone had the right to claim the time they had lived.

This time, he didn't want the office choosing whichever date was most convenient simply because the other was hard to explain.

***

Tessa Ord was waiting at the Personnel counter when he arrived.

She had been appointed to verify that the copy Kael had consulted matched the one supplied during his earlier proceedings. She had not brought the box of reproductions still held by the Archive, or anything from the restricted collection.

“The first sheet hasn't changed,” she said. “This is the version you used to start your appeal.”

“And the other?”

“The reviewer found it in the migration inventory. I don't have it.”

A staff member opened the comparison hearing. Dalia Ferren accompanied Kael as his adviser. Two certified copies lay on the table, each identifying its stated source and extraction date. Neither the original Mirror series nor permission to examine it had been provided.

“Both systems use the expression ‘link creation date,’” the staff member explained. “We can select the more recent value and issue an administrative correction.”

Kael looked at Dalia. She said nothing.

“What document supports that choice?” he asked.

“The fact that the link appears in your employment history after you joined.”

“That proves when it appears in the copy we have. Not when it was created in the originating system.”

The staff member frowned.

“Do you want to leave an error in your record?”

“I want both values to stay identified until we know which operation each represents.”

Dalia intervened.

“My client does not oppose a traceable correction. He opposes replacing one unexplained value with another. We request that the discrepancy be recorded without assigning blame to an unidentified operator.”

The staff member checked the form.

She could register a disputed field and request the migration mapping document. That document would describe how columns had been transferred between offices. By itself, it would not grant access to personal records in the restricted collection.

Tessa recognized the procedure.

“In other migrations, a column called ‘original date’ ended up being used for the day a ledger was digitized,” she said. “I don't know whether that happened here. I'm only saying the field's name isn't enough.”

Kael was grateful she hadn't tried to help him by drawing a conclusion.

At the end, he received an acknowledgment: both dates would be preserved, and the conversion method would be examined. The office could not yet determine which date described the event they were looking for.

***

Dalia suggested stopping for something to drink before returning to her office.

In the café, the lights flickered, and the owner switched off one lamp to avoid overloading the circuit. Kael studied the reflections of the two certificates on the table. He had expected an older date to open a door. Instead, comparing them had created more work.

“They might describe two events,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Or two different mistakes.”

“That too.”

“Something bothers me. The newer system doesn't record who made the first connection.”

Dalia took a sip of tea.

“That's a matter for a provenance audit. Not a declaration about your birth.”

Kael put the sheets away. He thought of his civil birth record and its chronological key, identical to the one associated with the Mirror Index. He would have liked to place that coincidence beside the new dates and create a single story. But “creation” could mean too many things.

“Can I ask to see the conversion map?”

“The portion concerning your data, if the authority considers it relevant. A general diagram may contain no personal information. If other people's data are involved, the fields will need to be separated.”

“And if they can't find it?”

“Have them record the search and its result. Don't turn an absence into an imaginary presence.”

An ambulance moved slowly along the street, carrying a refrigerated case secured beside the driver. Kael could not know what was inside it, and had no reason to associate it with his file. He caught himself wondering and looked back down at his cup.

He was finding it difficult to learn the difference between a possibility and a clue.

***

The reply to the comparison request arrived three days later.

The reviewer had located the migration manual in an administrative repository separate from Personnel. The phrase “link creation date” did not refer to one universal field. Under the old procedure, it meant the first addition to the originating register; under the new procedure, the day the receiving employment history accepted the reference.

Both dates could be correct.

But the manual imposed a requirement neither copy could satisfy: to maintain a link between the two offices, a signed record of the transformation had to exist. Without it, the receiving system could retain the data only as a reference whose attribution remained unverified—never as proof of identity.

Kael put the paper on the table.

The answer removed an apparent contradiction and replaced it with a concrete absence. No one had found the signed transformation record explaining why that index had become linked to his file.

The office was not accusing him of having a false identity. Nor was it validating the reference. It preserved both dates with their separate meanings and extended protection of the audit trail while the missing document was sought.

At the bottom was a question addressed to the former migration service: *Which department authorized the transmission of that identifier?*

Kael copied the sentence into his notebook.

For the first time, the search did not depend on a date seeming extraordinary.

It depended on a signature required by an ordinary rule—a signature no one could produce.

***End of Episode 07.***
