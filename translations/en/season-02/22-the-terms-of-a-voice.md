# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 22: The Terms of a Voice

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Lyra Saren, limited third person.

***

The man waiting for Lyra at the docks had brought a folding chair and an order to stand up.

“The regional office needs a supplemental statement,” he said, displaying the seal. “If you can travel today, you'll make tomorrow's hearing.”

Lyra Saren did not take the document.

“What kind of statement?”

“The inquiry into return-system instructions. You've already testified once. The official assumes you can clarify whether the procedure is safe.”

Ema stopped counting sacks.

“That's a big assumption.”

Lyra agreed to read the notice. It wasn't a court summons. It was an administrative invitation to testify as a technical witness about events she had previously observed. It listed the general subjects but set no limits on the distribution of her testimony and offered no protocol for protecting clinical data. Nor did it explain why her physical presence was essential.

The station under review was still barred from reopening. The Needles remained under a separate restriction, and the exceptional authorization that had allowed Lyra to travel to Selivar was no longer valid. No hearing officer's signature could turn such a journey into an authorized operation.

“I can't go,” she said. “I have medical restrictions on prolonged travel. I can review questions and answer about what I actually saw, within limits.”

The messenger pointed to the final paragraph.

“They say they'll miss the date for a ruling.”

“That has a cost. I know. But I'm not going to certify a facility I've never visited.”

The man folded the chair and put it away without arguing. Lyra stared at her own signature on the earlier statement, the one Sena had asked permission to quote.

The promise to accompany Sena in Selivar still did not exist as a lived memory, though Lyra knew she had fulfilled it by testifying.

She didn't need to recover the missing moment to decide which words she would make available to others now.

***

Sena Vard read the questions at the association office.

They fell into three groups: what differences Lyra had noticed between versions of a manual; what facts she could confirm about her authorized journey; and what technical conclusion she drew about a station she had never seen.

“The first two can be answered within a narrow scope,” Sena said. “The third belongs with an inspector who's examined that facility.”

“They want my experience to serve as a shortcut.”

“They want to resolve a delay. That doesn't necessarily imply bad intentions. But it doesn't give them authority they don't have, either.”

Lyra asked to set out conditions: remote testimony from an authorized office; a trusted person present with her; advance notice of the subjects; the right to correct the hearing record; and exclusion of clinical information and strategic references. Her previous testimony could be quoted only within the consent she had already given.

“Are you going to demand complete confidentiality?” Sena asked.

“No. They can publish a version of what I said about stopping a test when the configuration is unknown. They can't publish my medical notes or present my conclusions as an inspection of the station.”

Sena pointed to another clause. The hearing procedure provided for testimony to be made available in a training archive for operators.

“They want to keep the entire recording here.”

“I'm willing to let the hearing retain the complete version in its restricted custody,” Lyra said. “For training, an excerpt that's been reviewed and correctly attributed. Nothing more.”

Sena looked up.

“They may refuse.”

“Then I'll decide whether to submit a written statement and put on record why I couldn't attend.”

Before closing the folder, Lyra added a question for the regulator: if an inspection found that the equipment required an expensive repair, who would finance the time it remained shut down?

That wasn't her decision to make. It was the reason someone else wanted such an easy answer from her.

***

The regional office accepted some of the conditions.

The hearing took place over a communicator from a room at the association, with a verifying official at each end. The complete recording was restricted to the proceeding, and the public version would be reviewed to remove protected data. They did not agree that Lyra could veto relevant questions, but they did agree to record her objections.

An inspector displayed the first diagram from the manual.

“Do you recognize this sequence?”

“I recognize a written sequence we saw in an earlier version,” Lyra replied. “I don't know whether the mechanism you want to open has the same configuration.”

“Does your testimony prove the procedure is dangerous?”

“It establishes that applying it without first verifying the return capacity and the equipment's reference specifications would be unjustified. I don't have readings from that equipment.”

The inspector showed her a sensor table. Lyra asked him to identify it as information from the regional case file, not as measurements she had taken herself.

During the session, someone asked whether the price Lyra had paid for an earlier Transit rendered her incapable of assessing risks.

“I haven't applied to operate anything,” she answered. “My medical experience neither replaces nor invalidates technical measurements. What I can claim is limited by my sources.”

The verifying official recorded the objection. The medical note was not made public.

When the session ended, Sena waited while Lyra reviewed the minutes. One paragraph said the witness had “confirmed the need to suspend the station indefinitely.”

Lyra asked for a correction.

“I said reopening could not be recommended without the comparison. I didn't set a time frame or decide anything about the permits.”

The official checked the recording and amended the sentence.

It was a difference of just a few words. It could mean months of wages for people waiting to return to work.

***

Ema had the supply board spread out on the table when Lyra returned.

The association had lost a morning's work to the hearing. Two deliveries had been delayed, and the use of the borrowed office would have to be paid for. Lyra recorded the hours involved; she did not pretend that testifying was a gesture without consequences.

A regional message arrived at the end of the shift. The station would remain suspended until a physical inspection was completed. The hearing had helped clarify which questions the qualified technician needed to answer; it had not replaced that work.

“At least they didn't make you go in person,” Ema said.

“Because I refused to accept that it was necessary.”

Sena arrived with the public version of the minutes. It had been separated from the restricted recording and contained neither clinical data nor destination references.

Lyra read the final line. It stated that her testimony was limited to events she had witnessed and identified documentary versions.

It was what she had been saying from the beginning.

Then Sena pointed out a new request added to the proceeding. An operator from another facility wanted to play part of the hearing for his staff. He was seeking guidance about when a witness's experience stopped being evidence and became an instruction.

Lyra set the paper on the table.

“That question deserves a written answer from the technical authority,” she said. “Not another promise from me.”

She looked at the acknowledgment of her testimony. She had chosen to speak without handing over her entire life as collateral.

And for the first time, the document made clear which part of her voice still belonged to her.

***End of Episode 22.***
