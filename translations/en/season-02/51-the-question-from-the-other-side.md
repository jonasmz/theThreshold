# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 51: The Question from the Other Side

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Daren Holt, limited third person.

***

The recording strip didn't tear when they unwound it. For Daren Holt, that was the only thing that went exactly as planned.

The examiner had prepared a reading support that kept the old paper under minimal tension. In a room at the territorial courthouse, with defense counsel and the prosecutor present, she began displaying the first section of the mechanical record saved from destruction. The drum had belonged to a medical training service. The registers established that it had been loaned to a testing department, but they hadn't identified the fatal session.

The prosecutor pointed to an abrupt change.

“That drop matches the interruption described in the clinical report.”

“It matches in shape,” Mara Ellin answered. “We still haven't established the instrument's exact scale or the date of this strip.”

The defense attorney requested a pause. She wasn't trying to hide the trace. Instead, she produced an inventory page documenting a recording-head replacement between two periods of use.

“If the head was changed,” she said, “the calibration mark might belong to another configuration.”

Daren had expected that objection. What he hadn't expected was for the defense to bring the original maintenance entry, made available by the custodian, which he himself had not yet received.

The judge asked where the document had been.

The defense attorney explained how she'd obtained access and submitted an identified copy for examination by the opposing party. The original remained in the technical repository. The court ordered its provenance verified before treating it as the basis of an expert conclusion.

Daren felt the weight of an uncomfortable possibility: the curve might not say what he'd imagined.

“Then let's compare them,” he said.

The judge looked at him.

“That's why we're here.”

***

The examination resumed two days later, with the recording-head entry authenticated and a calibration table reconstructed from two independent sources.

The change on the strip was real. But it covered a longer interval than the initial reading had suggested. There had been a load interlock and incomplete recovery, without anything in that result identifying a person connected to the machine.

The prosecutor maintained that the equipment had recorded the fatal procedure. The defense argued that it might have captured an abandoned classroom test.

“Which of those hypotheses can the instruments exclude?” the judge asked the examiner.

“Neither, at present. We can rule out the mark being merely a defect in the paper. We can't link it to a particular session without the assignment register or a witness with direct knowledge.”

Mara explained that the death's clinical report documented injuries consistent with a failed stabilization attempt. The similarity of the traces strengthened one line of inquiry, but the material link between the strip and that patient was still missing.

Daren studied the prosecutor's face. They had spent weeks securing the equipment's preservation. Nobody was pleased to discover that the evidence demanded still more work.

Then they called Elia Ren, the former assistant who had testified about training courses. She appeared with her rights adviser and a previously agreed list of questions. The defense was permitted to question her about dates and responsibilities without exposing other apprentices' names.

Elia recognized the type of recorder she'd seen in classrooms, not this particular drum.

“I never entered the stabilization room,” she said. “I never saw anyone connected to that instrument.”

Daren wrote down the distinction. Elia had come to testify, yet the most valuable part of her answer might be the boundary she drew.

***

After the hearing, Daren found Elia seated in the courthouse courtyard beside a planter holding a leafless plant.

“I was afraid that admitting I didn't know would hurt the case,” she confessed.

“It would have been worse to say you'd seen something you hadn't.”

Elia had also brought her shift notebook. The examiner had verified the continuity of the paper, ink, and payment notes across the limited period under examination. Entries matching the institution's attendance list confirmed that Elia had worked there on particular dates. They didn't establish that any particular person had consented or been subjected to the fatal trial.

“They asked about the apprentice they removed,” she said. “I don't know if he's still alive.”

Daren didn't promise an indiscriminate search.

“We can consult only relevant records through authorized procedures. If we find someone who can speak, they'll have the right to protection.”

Elia looked down at her hands.

“Then what changes today?”

“The defense was able to challenge a reading. The reading was corrected. And we can establish that you were present without making you a witness to a room you never entered.”

The woman was silent for a moment.

“That doesn't sound like much.”

Daren thought of every time a court had tried to bring a story to an end with a statement broader than the evidence allowed.

“It's what we can say without taking anyone else's voice away.”

When Elia left, the court clerk handed him a notice from the medical archive. An index of sessions had been located, with partly legible dates and a custody heading. It did not yet contain the dead person's name. The office requested territorial authorization to reproduce only the period under investigation.

Daren asked that both sides have the same opportunity to examine it.

***

The judge issued orders to continue the investigation, not conclusions about guilt.

She authorized a limited comparison of the sessions index, preserved the originals with their custodians, and ordered that experts for both sides be allowed to challenge the methods, calibration, and attribution of dates. Unrelated people's clinical records would remain protected. The court would not move an entire library simply because doing so was convenient.

The prosecutor requested permission to announce that they had proved the machine's use in the fatal trial.

“That has not been proved,” the magistrate replied. “You may report the procedural status without presenting that hypothesis as a final finding.”

The defense didn't receive a declaration that the strip was unrelated to the investigation, either. Its arguments had narrowed the scope of an inference, not removed the need to investigate.

Daren stepped out into a brief rain shower.

He had lost the comfort of a straightforward timeline. In return, the revised calibration table made it possible to ask precisely what might have happened over a longer load interval. Mara could reconsider the documented injuries without forcing them to match the wrong numbers.

At the corner, two workers were unloading boxes destined for the court. One came open, spilling damp folders onto the ground. Daren helped collect them and waited while the workers recorded which documents had touched the pavement. None could go back into the archive as though nothing had happened.

He thought the judicial process resembled that work far too closely.

When a piece wasn't where they expected, they had to count it again.

And when the other side was right, they had to put that in writing, too.

***End of Episode 51.***
