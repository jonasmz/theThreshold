# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 09: The Original on the Other Side

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Daren Holt, limited third person.

***

The man who had come to demand the document did not have a court order.

He carried a letter bearing the letterhead of the department that held the records from the trial in which someone had died before the public execution. He introduced himself at the courthouse as an administrative liaison and requested that the certified copy held by Daren Holt be returned to his agency for “consolidation of records.”

“The copy entered these proceedings through formal cooperation,” Daren said. “Your office holds the original. What needs consolidating?”

“Duplicate records of the same entry,” the liaison replied. “Keeping them separate may compromise medical information.”

The concern was not absurd. The report included information about people whose identities had not been established. Confidentiality was meant to protect them. But the court copy had also been admitted through a process involving the parties, experts, and documented seals.

“Who authorized its removal from our custody?” Daren asked.

The man pointed to an administrative director's signature. It was neither a court order nor an agreement between jurisdictions.

“I can enter your request into the case file and submit it to the judge,” Daren said. “I cannot hand over evidence at the counter.”

The liaison frowned.

“I didn't say you had to give it to me today.”

“The letter requests immediate return. I'm responding to what it says.”

The man accepted a stamped acknowledgment of receipt and left.

Daren remained by the window for a moment. In the square, two workers were dismantling a barrier that was no longer needed. Some pedestrian access had been restored, yet no one knew where Taren Oss was. The city moved forward in sections, like a case file.

He returned to his desk.

It wasn't enough to prevent evidence from leaving through the wrong door. He needed to establish which office had the right to answer for each item.

***

Mara Ellin arrived with the sample inventory.

The physician drew a line connecting three references: the tissue samples held by the court, the certified clinical copy, and the record of the remains' preparation that they had requested from the outside department. Only the first two were physically available to her laboratory.

“The profiles are compatible with what's documented in the report,” she said. “But I can't authenticate the order releasing the remains with what I have here. That order isn't among the medical exhibits.”

The court clerk added the latest cooperation response. The original release order belonged to the archive of a transport operator under a different territorial authority. The medical department retained only the reference acknowledging its receipt.

“Can we request a reproduction?” Daren asked.

“We can ask,” the clerk replied. “The other authority will need to define the relevant pages and assess third-party rights.”

Daren remembered the body displayed before the crowd. They had shown it was not the condemned man. Then they had found documents linking it to an earlier human trial. They still did not know who had ordered the remains moved.

It was easy to start treating every missing page as confirmation of a conspiracy. It was harder to accept that a company might have kept an original under procedures legally different from the court's.

“Let's make the request precise,” he said. “Release order, delivery and receipt signatures, seal inventory. If it includes other patients' information, separate it. If they refuse anything, require a reason.”

The prosecutor joined them to review the letter. She accepted the limits but added an urgent preservation measure: until access was resolved, they should ask the other authority not to move, purge, or use the original in another proceeding without recording its movements.

The defense was notified. Its representative demanded the right to participate in the technical comparison if it was allowed.

Daren did not object.

Evidence did not become trustworthy simply because only one side was allowed to touch it.

***

The response did not authorize physically transferring the original.

The outside authority agreed to preserve the document under its own inventory and offered an on-site examination by an authorized expert, with a seal record and a certified copy of the relevant fields. The parties could observe through the agreed procedure or submit questions to the expert. The original would not leave the facility.

“It's less than we asked for,” the prosecutor said.

“It's something we can verify without turning our court into the owner of another archive,” Daren replied.

The document examiner called in to assess the proposal identified a detail. The order number in the medical copy included a receipt suffix; the transport operator recorded the number without it. The difference might reflect routine office practice, or it might mean the records referred to different orders.

“I need to see the numbering system before I say they match,” she said.

Daren requested that the explanation be included among the questions to the outside custodian. They would not compare the codes character by character as if every office used the same system.

While they finished the record, a notice arrived from the clerk's office: another department had requested to know when examination of the order would be permitted. It was not an objection from the defense. Citing a records security review, it asked that the result first be forwarded to its own office.

“Does it have jurisdiction over this case?” Daren asked.

“It hasn't established any.”

“Register the request. Don't send the result. And ask by what authority it's intervening.”

The clerk nodded.

They had not proved someone was trying to conceal evidence. But they had two requests seeking control over the circulation of a document whose original they did not possess.

***

The judge convened a short hearing.

The administrative liaison had filed his demand for return; the prosecution and defense could state their positions. Daren explained that keeping a court copy did not authorize public disclosure of medical information. Nor did it allow an outside office to unilaterally revoke an agreed admission of evidence.

The judge rejected immediate return and ordered periodic review of confidentiality restrictions. She authorized comparison against the original at the outside facility, subject to that authority's agreement and adversarial safeguards. The inventory of the court copy and its access log would remain intact.

“Can we say the original and our copy concern the same transaction?” the defense asked.

“Not yet,” Daren replied. “That's precisely what we're asking to verify.”

The ruling also recorded the second request for preferential delivery of the findings. Before answering, the court would require the issuing authority's identity and a legal basis. An administrative message did not authorize transferring the file to a third office.

After the hearing, Mara stood with Daren in the corridor.

“What if the original doesn't match?” she asked.

“Then we'll need to find out why. And accept that the report we've relied on might need correcting.”

“You don't seem to like that.”

“I don't. But I'd like it even less if a dead person remained unnamed because we preferred to protect a mistaken conclusion.”

Mara returned to the laboratory.

Daren received the day's final communication. The outside authority confirmed the earliest available time for the comparison and requested notification of the parties.

Beneath that confirmation, the court clerk had added a line:

*A third party has requested a complete copy of the preservation file before the inspection. No authorization to access it is on record.*

Daren signed the order refusing release.

It was no longer just the original that someone wanted under a single hand.

***End of Episode 09.***
