# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 45: The Record of a Refusal

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, limited third person.

***

The form said “documentary assistance.” On its second page, it asked for a living response.

Kael Veyran read it beside the Archive window, where Tessa Ord had gathered employees selected for a manual-comparison project. The technical authority was seeking staff capable of recognizing older editions during a restricted demonstration. The work appeared to match his skills: reading indexes, verifying seals, identifying editions.

Until he reached the authorization annex.

“The collaborator may, when requested, confirm personal response indicators to validate the access procedure.”

“What does this mean?” Kael asked.

Tessa came over. She'd received the pages as part of an interagency contract without examining every technical clause.

“I assumed it meant identifying the person entering the room.”

“It doesn't say only that. It could authorize a compatibility test.”

The Personnel supervisor took the form and frowned.

“Then we can't present this as ordinary conservation work.”

Kael knew the distinction too well. His origins had been established in a private hearing; he'd refused a voluntary invitation to compatibility testing, and that refusal was protected by a safeguards authority. None of that appeared in his employment file. Nor should it have to appear now to explain why a clause was unacceptable.

“Can we request a written description of the scope from the contracting office?” he asked.

“Yes. Until we receive it, we won't summon anyone.”

A colleague objected. He needed the contract's extra hours. The corridor suspension had reduced available work, and his family was counting on that income.

Kael didn't blame him.

Whoever had drafted the form had counted on making a legitimate task and an uncertain test look like the same thing.

***

The technical office replied two days later.

“Personal response” encompassed noninvasive physical identification and, depending on equipment needs, possible low-power compatibility checks. The agency said participation was not mandatory. Yet the contract allowed people to be removed from the project if they failed to complete the checks, without specifying which checks were essential to cataloging manuals.

Tessa convened a meeting with labor representatives.

“We have two tasks mixed together,” she explained. “One requires archivists. The other would require a separate authorization, technical jurisdiction, and specific consent.”

An external supervisor insisted the demonstration was safe.

“We're not asking anyone to operate the whole Network. Only to observe a response within limits.”

“Then specify which facility, what power source, and what withdrawal protocol,” Kael answered. “Above all, who decides that looking at a manual requires a biological response?”

The man looked at the attendance list.

“You seem to know a great deal about these procedures.”

Kael held his gaze.

“I know how to define a scope of work. That's what I was hired to do.”

Tessa intervened before the question could become personal. She reminded everyone that employees could not be required to disclose health information or biological origins for documentary work without a legal basis and appropriate protections.

The supervisor offered to move the assessment to a later date but did not remove the clause from the general contract. It remained too broad for the work promised.

Kael left with a certified copy of the reply. He hadn't agreed to any test or revealed his history.

At least the office could no longer say the scope was unknown.

***

Dalia Ferren received the copy that evening.

“Your earlier refusal concerned a personal invitation,” she said. “This is different. Someone is trying to bundle additional permissions into an archival work contract.”

Kael sat across from her.

“I want it in writing that I don't authorize biological, mnemonic, or access-response testing. But I don't want to abandon work I know how to do, either.”

Dalia examined the protected record of his earlier refusal. That document couldn't be sent to Personnel without Kael's authorization. Nor was it necessary for challenging the employment clause: any worker could demand separation between archival duties and interventions involving their body.

“We can submit a general objection to the contract,” she said. “Through a separate channel, we can record your individual refusal of any operation they might propose. The safeguards authority will decide who needs to be informed.”

Kael remembered his childhood notebooks, the expression of the woman who'd raised him when he told her part of the truth, and the work offer he'd earned on his own merits. He wanted those things to remain parts of his life, not entries in a list of technical criteria.

He signed two documents.

The first requested removal of any implied physical-examination authorization from documentary duties and protection of benefits already earned by people who withheld consent. The second, restricted, specifically reiterated that he did not authorize multifactor testing or Network maneuvers. It permitted disclosure only of the existence of a valid restriction to officials authorized to enforce it, without revealing his origins.

“Could I work on an instrumental alternative?” he asked.

“On archival tasks, if they really are archival tasks and you choose to participate,” Dalia replied. “You're not forbidden from helping other people. You have the right to define how.”

For the first time, the distinction between those choices seemed clear.

***

The technical office returned a revised contract.

Documentary tasks would be limited to identifying editions, preserving originals, and preparing public or redacted indexes. Any compatibility assessment would require a separate procedure, authorized by the competent body and voluntarily accepted by specific participants. No Archive employee would be enrolled by default.

Tessa signed the revised scope. The labor representative also demanded a clause governing payment for hours already committed during the administrative suspension; the agency agreed to settle those under the current collective agreement.

The colleague who'd protested approached Kael beside the paper depot.

“I thought you were going to make us lose the contract,” he said.

“So did I.”

“And now?”

“Now they're hiring us to do what we know how to do. If they want something else, they'll have to ask for it by another name.”

The man nodded and went back to counting boxes.

Kael later received Iria Solen's ruling on his protected declaration. It confirmed his refusal to participate in personal assessments and maneuvers, prohibited treating his consent to the clinical comparison establishing his identity as operational authorization, and ordered that the data remain separate from his employment record. Interested offices could challenge the limits of their own powers; doing so would not entitle them to his personal history.

Kael filed the ruling with Dalia and kept a double-covered copy.

At the end of the shift, Tessa offered him his first assignment under the corrected contract: reconstruct the edition history of a maintenance guide and establish which checks appeared in its public annexes. There would be no demonstration on an active installation and no biological identification of employees. The work could be done in a room lit by an ordinary accumulator.

“Do you want to accept?” she asked.

Kael looked at the books stacked on the table.

A deteriorating system needed help. The old warnings now coming to light made it more urgent to understand its procedures, not less. None of those urgent needs diminished his right to choose.

“Yes,” he replied. “To read, compare, and document. If the scope changes, we'll talk again.”

Tessa handed him the room keys.

Kael entered without offering a sample, a memory, or a promise of obedience.

He brought the only thing he'd agreed to put to work: his judgment.

***End of Episode 45.***
