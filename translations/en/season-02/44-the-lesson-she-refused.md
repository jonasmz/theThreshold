# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 44: The Lesson She Refused

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Daren Holt, limited third person.

***

The woman requested a chair near the exit.

Daren Holt had seen her arrive at the courthouse with a rights adviser. She carried no complaint folder, only an appearance card that allowed the judge to establish her identity without writing it on the copies intended for the public.

“I don't want to be called a victim before anyone listens to me,” she said.

The magistrate agreed. The testimony would be heard in private, with narrowly defined questions and the right to pause. The defense had received the scope of the examination and could question her through authorized procedures. There would be no Imprinting mirror or invasive methods intended to verify memories.

Daren explained why they had come.

His investigation into the body at the scaffold included a clinical report from a fatal human trial. The dead person was still unidentified, and specific consent for the maneuver had not been located. The training department, consulted about an index, had confirmed that it offered programs for operators. A former employee had voluntarily come forward to describe how participation was requested.

“Your testimony alone won't establish what happened to the deceased,” Daren clarified. “We need to know whether the general procedures allowed people to refuse and what records were kept.”

The woman identified herself to the judge as Elia Ren, a classroom assistant during a period she documented with her employment contract. She'd witnessed preparatory sessions and attendance recording. She had never entered a final stabilization room.

“They taught us to call the additional tests ‘mandatory practice,’” she said. “When someone asked whether they could refuse to enter, they were told they'd lose their place in training.”

The adviser offered her water.

Elia accepted before continuing.

Daren wrote down her exact words, without adding the conclusion he most feared.

***

The defense asked who had given that instruction.

Elia remembered a classroom supervisor but wasn't certain of his surname. Her shifts had changed several times. Some procedures were communicated orally; others were written on notices removed at the end of the week.

“Did you see anyone physically forced to participate?” the lawyer asked.

“No.”

“Did you hear anyone refuse?”

“Yes. An apprentice said he didn't want to continue. They took him off the list the following day.”

“Do you know why?”

Elia lowered her eyes.

“They told me he wasn't fit. I never saw an assessment of his fitness.”

Daren didn't step in to rescue her answer. The distinction mattered: removing someone from a list could be punishment, a medical precaution, or an administrative action. Other evidence would be needed to establish that it had been intended to force consent.

The prosecutor requested permission to display a training-form template with names and medical information redacted. Elia recognized its design, but not the date of that particular version.

“There was a box labeled ‘voluntary participation,’” she recalled. “Sometimes people signed it before anyone explained what the tests would involve.”

The defense asked whether she could guarantee that this had happened to everyone.

“No. I'm speaking only about shifts I worked.”

The judge recorded the period and the limitations. Daren watched Elia's hand tighten around the chair's edge. The witness was describing something important without turning it into an unlimited truth.

When that part ended, she asked for a break.

Nobody treated her need to step outside as a retraction.

***

The adviser reported that Elia wanted to provide a shift notebook.

It was hers, not the training center's. She'd kept a record of her hours because her wages varied and she needed proof of the time she had worked. The columns contained crossed-out entries and apprentices' initials. She did not authorize their names to be made public.

“Can we inspect it without entering the whole notebook into evidence?” Daren asked.

The judge permitted a limited examination of the identified period and entries related to attendance changes. A document examiner would assess the physical medium, sequence of writing, and amendments, while the parties could observe. Elia would keep the original under the temporary custody arrangements she chose, unless a new, reasoned judicial order required otherwise.

Elia agreed to bring it the next day.

Mara Ellin, attending as a consultant on the clinical case, spoke during the recess.

“Attendance at training isn't consent to a stabilization trial. Nor is it proof that the person who died attended that course.”

“I know,” Daren replied.

“Then don't ask the notebook for something it can't give.”

The prosecutor arrived with a message from the training department. It confirmed that a general shift list from Elia's period had been found and offered to preserve it under a territorial order. It sent no names or clinical records to the court.

Daren prepared a request for the judge. It would seek preservation of the register and comparison of a minimal set of dates. No general comparison of every apprentice was authorized.

When Elia returned, the magistrate asked whether she wanted the hearing to end.

“I want to answer one more thing,” she said.

She explained that weeks after the apprentice was removed from training, she'd seen him working at a cleaning station in the same facility. She didn't know whether he'd chosen that job or had been reassigned because he'd refused to continue.

“I remember his expression,” she said. “I can't explain his employment contract.”

Daren preserved both halves of the sentence.

***

The hearing ended without anyone being found guilty.

Elia received a protected copy of her testimony and an acknowledgment that she could request corrections to the transcript. Through interagency cooperation, the judge ordered preservation of the training center's relevant lists without releasing their full contents until standing, relevance, and the rights of those listed were determined. The witness's personal information would remain restricted to the court file.

“Did it do any good?” Elia asked in the corridor.

Daren wanted to offer an answer that sounded like justice had already been done.

“We know which facts need checking now,” he said. “And there are records that can't be discarded without us knowing.”

“Are you going to call the apprentice?”

“Only if we can locate him lawfully and his testimony is relevant. He'll have the right to choose how he participates and to receive protection. We're not going to track him through a notebook exposed to the public.”

Elia nodded.

Outside, court workers unloaded boxes of paper delivered along an overburdened road route. Investigations kept accumulating while the city struggled to supply itself.

Daren returned to his office. On his desk lay the inventory of the preserved load drum and the partial answer from the consent custodian. Neither resolved the case of the body.

The new testimony raised another question: if a program called something voluntary when refusing could cost someone their work, how free was the person who signed?

Daren didn't turn the question into a settled accusation.

He drafted a request for comparison by date, document, and authority, leaving the “responsible party” line blank.

The next signature would need to prove more than the fact that someone had been present.

***End of Episode 44.***
