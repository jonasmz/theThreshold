# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 14: The Missing Pages

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Maelor Thane, limited third person.

***

The first page bore a signature. The second held a line of deletions. Of the third, only the bottom corner remained.

Maelor Thane studied the three fragments the Continuity Commission had sent to his office in response to a request for independent review. The request sought to document which categories of information could be retained during an administrative transfer without bringing clinical, employment, and identity data together under one office.

It was a limited request. It did not demand that every file be opened.

The commission had responded with an extract of old rules and a warning: part of its index remained restricted, while other portions lacked a complete description of their custody history.

“Is this all?” Maelor asked.

Vessa held the dispatch inventory.

“It's everything the commission acknowledges authorizing for this procedure. The originals are divided between a historical repository and the service that managed the migration.”

Maelor knew why that separation mattered. For years, records of human beings had been treated as components in a continuity system. Protecting those collections had sometimes protected the people concerned; at other times, it had prevented those responsible from having to explain themselves. His office gave him access to certain technical decisions of that regime, not every identity or every act committed under its rules.

“I want a list of what was omitted,” he said. “Not personal information. A list of the pages withheld and the authority responsible for restricting them.”

“The commission says revealing the titles could enable unauthorized cross-referencing.”

“That may be true. But then it needs to explain the risk to the competent reviewer. ‘Restricted’ isn't a reason that justifies itself.”

Vessa made a note.

Outside, shift-change sirens sounded. The city's workshops would not stop needing energy while the Council argued about privacy.

***

Talia Mer arrived with the Western Annex report.

The fatigued joint on the shared return line remained under observation. Denying the Needles reopening request had prevented a test under unsafe conditions, but maintenance still required extra staff, and the municipality's conventional transport costs continued to rise.

“I haven't come to ask you to open the route,” Talia said. “I've come to ask you to stop calling a reduced load temporary when it's already changing entire budgets.”

Maelor accepted the rebuke.

“What does the team need?”

“A review date determined by measurements, not speeches. And written instructions to stop any test if the return line hasn't regained its minimum safety margin.”

“You'll have both.”

Talia glanced at the commission's papers, which lay beneath a cover.

“Reviewing old files again?”

“I'm reviewing who's accountable for what gets preserved. That affects technical records, too.”

“Technicians can't answer for a manual someone removed from the system before their shift.”

“Nor should they have to.”

Maelor considered the temptation to label as an administrative mistake something that had, on occasion, been a deliberate decision to conceal harm. He had watched colleagues choose the least uncomfortable explanation far too often before asking for evidence. He did not know who had altered the annexes to the reopening request, and he could not use the history of other cover-ups to settle that case.

He authorized a limited audit: examine both copies without changing either, reconstruct the movements of the documents, and take statements from the personnel who received them. Licensing would keep its copy; Custody would keep its own. An independent review would report which changes could be demonstrated and which still had no identified author.

Talia rose.

“That won't repair the return line.”

“No. But it will prevent a badly drafted order from forcing us to damage it further.”

***

The Continuity Commission summoned Maelor to a windowless room.

Three representatives and an officer authorized to certify the proceedings were present. A new packet lay on the table. It contained no personal case files, only a table of custodianships and classification rules, redacted to avoid exposing names. Each row identified which agency had retained particular types of entries and which procedure applied when a personal link needed review.

“We've separated what can be disclosed from what still requires special authorization,” the chairwoman said. “This release does not permit identification of people recorded in the series.”

Maelor checked the signatures.

“Who certifies that no inventory rows have been omitted?”

The chairwoman showed him a formal record: the packet was cross-referenced to a complete index under dual control. The certifying officer attested to the pages present and those excluded by ruling, without certifying the substantive truth of their contents.

It was better than three loose pages.

It was not enough.

“The explanation of transfers between departments is missing,” Maelor pointed out. “Some categories move from one office to another without any visible acknowledgment of receipt.”

One representative replied that those movements had occurred under earlier rules. It might be true. Maelor could not manufacture a signature retroactively just to reassure the present.

“Then establish what you cannot prove,” he said. “And preserve the originals while the proper authority determines who has the right to compare them.”

The chairwoman regarded him for a long moment.

“We can forward this table to the independent data reviewer under the stated restrictions. Not to a civil court without a specific request, or to the public indiscriminately.”

“That's a reasonable scope for this proceeding.”

The representative also handed over a list of exceptions. It contained no names, but it did contain a category Maelor recognized from his years in Custody: records protecting minors whose details had been verified outside the ordinary civil registry.

He had known that category before assuming his current office. He knew it had sometimes been used to protect vulnerable people from harm. He also knew the regime had permitted decisions made without listening to those who would later have to live with the consequences.

He could not resolve that contradiction at a conference table.

He could prevent the records from disappearing again under the pretext of reorganization.

***

Back at the Annex, Maelor dictated a letter to the independent authority.

He attached a description of the table, the restriction order, and an offer of comparison within the commission's jurisdiction. He required any request concerning an individual to be considered with protections for third parties and reasoned explanations for refusals. He granted no access to complete case files, credentials, or strategic facilities.

Vessa waited until he finished signing.

“Will there be protests within the Council?”

“Probably. But I haven't ordered names disclosed. I've ordered that someone be allowed to demand an answer about who holds them.”

“There's another communication,” she said. “One of the offices asked to identify the source of a reference wants all future inquiries centralized before the independent authority examines them.”

Maelor held out his hand.

The request bore a valid receipt number but did not include the authorization needed to displace the reviewer's jurisdiction. It was another proposal for administrative consolidation. It might reflect legitimate concern about sensitive data. It might also prevent independent comparisons. The document itself did not establish intent.

“Don't carry it out,” he said. “Ask for its legal basis and preserve the communication.”

Vessa withdrew.

Maelor looked at the table the commission had agreed to release. It was a small fragment of a much larger history.

For the first time, an independent agency would have a formal way to ask about the pieces that were missing.

***End of Episode 14.***
