# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 18: The Price of Leaving It Untouched

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Sera Nhal, limited third person.

***

The rejected part was taking up the space needed for a replacement they could actually use.

Sera Nhal arrived at the municipal warehouse before the guard shift changed. In a corner, beneath two seals and a moisture-resistant cover, lay the warranty replacement plate she had been unable to install because its certification was missing. The warehouse manager showed her the inventory: the rental period for the isolated storage area had expired, and the facility needed the space for incoming pump gaskets.

“We can't keep a disputed part indefinitely and go on paying for storage nobody budgeted for,” he said.

“And we can't destroy it or return it without documenting its condition,” Sera replied.

The manager pointed to a recent order. The supplier had sent a certificate supposedly completing the earlier delivery and offered to collect the unit in exchange for another.

Sera did not touch the plate. She read the copy at the counter.

The certificate cited a continuity test carried out by Vesta Laboratory. It listed a date, a report number, and an equipment family. The individual serial number matched the plate, but the attached table described a geometry that did not correspond to the measurements taken on receipt.

“Could be a template error,” the manager said.

“Could be. Until we check, I'm not signing anything that says the part is accepted.”

“If we don't send it back, we'll be charged for another week of storage.”

Sera put the certificate in a document sleeve without replacing the earlier record. Both papers needed to be preserved with their receipt dates. A new document did not make the old one true simply by arriving later.

***

An oversight inspector arrived with an independent technician and a representative of the supplier.

It was not a court hearing. They were there to verify a contractual delivery and decide which materials needed to be retained for a possible claim. Sera explained the limits of her first measurements. She had not tested the plate under service load or certified a manufacturing defect. She had observed its dimensions, serial markings, and the absence of an adequate test when it was received.

“We have a report,” the representative said, indicating the new sheet.

“The laboratory can confirm whether it issued the report,” the independent technician replied. “In the meantime, we can photograph the markings and compare the physical description without opening or altering the part.”

The representative insisted that the company needed to recover its material to complete the warranty process.

“You may apply for its return with an inventory, retention of samples, or an agreement on reproductions,” the inspector explained. “You aren't authorized to take it away before we determine what must be preserved.”

Sera saw the warehouse manager's hands tighten. Every day of delay meant paying a guard and postponing receipt of the gaskets needed to repair equipment elsewhere.

She proposed moving the plate to a smaller technical enclosure with rigid support and protection against moisture, without breaking its seals. The transfer would require two witnesses, temperature records, photographs, and a new inventory. It would free part of the floor, but it would not be free.

“Who pays?” the representative asked.

“For now, the municipal contingency fund,” the inspector said. “We reserve the right to recover the expense from the appropriate party under the contract. We won't presume anyone's guilt.”

Sera did not celebrate the solution. She knew how many hours it would take.

***

The transfer began at dusk.

Two workers secured the box on a low-vibration cart. A third read the seal numbers aloud. Sera recorded every movement without touching the protected surfaces. The inspector and the technician signed off on the photographs before the box entered its new enclosure.

Halfway along the corridor, the auxiliary lamp went out.

It wasn't an attack. The accumulator's reserve had been exhausted by a workday longer than planned. The cart stopped. The manager wanted them to continue in darkness; only a few steps remained.

“No,” Sera said.

“The corridor is clear.”

“We have a part that could be disputed in a case file tomorrow. If we knock it now, we won't be able to distinguish new damage from old.”

One worker removed her glove to check the spare flashlight. Sera asked her not to touch the box with a bare hand. They went to the distribution board, confirmed another circuit had available power, and requested authorization to connect a maintenance light.

Minutes passed.

The youngest worker checked the clock.

“My replacement's already at the door. If I stay, I'll miss the last transport.”

Sera spoke with the manager. They agreed that an accredited worker would take over and that the municipality would record the overtime it had required. She would not demand invisible sacrifices from the people preserving the evidence.

When the light returned, they completed the transfer. The seals were intact. The box occupied less space, but the new enclosure still required monitoring and humidity control.

Outside, a truck carrying gaskets waited its turn. The driver complained that he had lost two hours.

Sera listened without defending herself.

“Is there a claim I can sign?” she asked him.

“They'll deduct the delay from my pay if I'm late getting to the next dock.”

The manager supplied a certificate showing the truck's arrival and departure times. It wasn't automatic compensation, but it would allow the driver to claim his loss under the contract. Sera also asked them to record the additional power consumed by the lighting accumulator and the relief worker's pay. If anyone reviewed the decision, they needed to see its full cost, not just a photograph of an undamaged box.

Preserving evidence could harm people who had nothing to do with it.

***

Vesta Laboratory's response arrived two days later through the oversight channel.

Sera read it beside the inspector. The laboratory certified that the submitted report number corresponded to a real test, but one performed earlier on a unit from a different equipment family. The laboratory had never received the warranty plate identified in the copy, nor had it issued a document linking that test to the plate's serial number. It attached an authenticated reproduction of its own register entry, with unrelated commercial information removed.

“So the certificate is fake,” the manager said.

“The attribution of that test to this plate is incorrect,” Sera replied. “Oversight still needs to determine who added it, who used it, and whether any other legitimate test exists.”

The inspector had another development. In a municipal payment document, the main supplier had declared that the replacement was delivered with proof of conformity included. The warranty order and the new certificate did not establish who had prepared them, but the case file could be checked against the timing of the payment request.

Sera asked for the plate to remain sealed until the inspector decided how it should be preserved. She would not let a misattributed report become an excuse to install material that had still not been verified.

Later, reviewing the budget, she calculated the cost of her decision: two extra security shifts, the move, the lighting, and the truck's delay.

She did not adjust the figure to make it look smaller.

That box was costing money without producing a single drop of water.

Yet if they allowed it to vanish, the next part would arrive with the same paperwork, and nobody would be able to explain why it had been accepted.

***End of Episode 18.***
