# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 31: The Number That Shouldn't Fit

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Maelor Thane, limited third person.

***

The report arrived with one figure circled in red.

Maelor Thane recognized Talia Mer's handwriting before he read her signature. The engineer had attached three recording strips, the Western Annex's load inventory, and a table taken from the service manual they still used to schedule shifts.

“The return voltage takes longer to dissipate than this table allows,” Talia explained. “We repeated the measurement with the night shift and the day crew. Neither result matches the published interval.”

Maelor spread the papers across his desk. He knew the Network was fatigued, the Needles remained closed, and the Annex depended on monitored containment rather than a lasting repair. What disturbed him about the report wasn't the severity of the situation. It was something less obvious.

“Does the manual identify the same damper configuration?”

“It identifies a family of regulators. The identification plates on our units match that family, but the replacement history is incomplete.”

Maelor examined the bottom portion of the document. The procedure allowed a brief test after a rest interval that the Annex's sensors now indicated was insufficient.

“Was that test performed?”

“No. The crew stopped when they saw the readings. The last time we performed it was before the load reduction.”

They had avoided a risk without establishing why the manual prescribed a different procedure.

“I need the original version of that table, not another printout,” Maelor said. “And whatever replacement documentation we can obtain.”

Vessa appeared in the doorway carrying a request from Licensing. The office wanted a date set for a return-system test so it could restore conventional transportation capacity associated with the corridor.

Maelor held the document between two fingers.

“Tell them there will be no test until we know which configuration we're dealing with and which interval we're comparing.”

“They'll demand a public explanation.”

“We'll release whatever measurements can be disclosed without exposing the installation. I'm not going to conceal the fact that an operational restriction exists.”

***

Talia led him to the Annex's monitoring gallery.

The floor vibrated with an almost imperceptible rhythm. Two technicians watched a display of mechanical needles while another recorded dissipator temperature and available reserve capacity. There was no window onto any Domain; the strategic connections remained disabled, and the installation carried only its authorized containment load.

Maelor listened to the readings through one cycle.

The indicator fell, lingered too long in the same range, and finally returned to its resting mark. The sequence involved no additional power; it followed the ordinary fluctuations of the operating system.

“This is what the report calls a delay,” Talia said.

“Could it be a measurement error?”

“It could. The calibration is current, but I want an outside laboratory to check the recorder before we use its readings to challenge the manual.”

Maelor appreciated that she wasn't defending her own data out of pride.

“What does the maintenance log say?”

Talia showed him a working copy. It documented a partial replacement and two calibrations, but the sheet that should have linked the replacement part to the regulator version was absent from Licensing's file. The technical repository claimed to have a parts index, but had not yet answered their request for the original.

An operator approached.

“If we keep reducing capacity, the early-morning crew will lose its transport from the depot.”

“I want a written estimate of the impact,” Maelor said. “And of the cost of maintaining longer monitoring shifts.”

The man nodded without looking relieved.

Maelor stayed beside the dissipator for several minutes. The machine was doing exactly what they asked of it, within the narrow range of what it could still do. The people keeping it running would have to sleep less, walk farther, and explain to their families why the restrictions continued.

He would not call that a solution.

***

The manuals archive held a copy that appeared to be older.

The technical librarian spread it beneath an examination lamp. Its binding had been repaired several times; the spine bore a collection label, not a certified publication date.

“This table specifies a different rest interval,” Maelor observed.

“Yes,” the librarian replied. “But the page belongs to a section with supplements. I can't say it's an earlier instruction just because the book itself is older.”

There were therefore three separate facts: the current readings, a service manual in use at Licensing, and an archived sheet specifying another safety margin. The problem wasn't to choose whichever document best matched the measurement.

“Is there a revision index?” Maelor asked.

The librarian pointed to a series of codes. Two copies carried different numbering in the supplement, and both might legitimately have circulated if they covered different equipment versions.

“I need their provenance,” he said. “Who distributed each revision, and which unit it was approved for.”

“That may be recorded in the workshops' delivery acknowledgments.”

Maelor requested that both copies be preserved without disturbing their markings. He didn't remove them. Instead he requested identified reproductions showing shelf number, reproduction date, and a warning concerning the supplement's condition.

Talia, standing beside him, read the final line.

“If one of these intervals is wrong, someone may have spent years operating with less margin than they needed.”

“Possibly. But first we need to know when each instruction was written and whether it applied to the equipment we have.”

“And if nobody kept the receipts?”

Maelor looked at the librarian's hands, stained with dust from the book's spine.

“Then we'll record what we can't attribute. We won't invent an author just to turn a contradiction into a confession.”

***

Back at his office, Maelor found a municipal budget memorandum waiting.

Reduced corridor availability had forced the city to hire additional ground transport for supplies. The requested compensation exceeded the allocated reserve and threatened to displace repairs to water pipes.

Vessa waited for an answer.

“If we extend the suspension, there will be complaints,” she said.

“There'll be complaints if we lift it and the equipment fails, too.”

Maelor drafted three separate instructions. The Annex would maintain its limits with redundant measurements; Licensing would preserve the version it had been using without destroying records; and the technical archive would permit an independent expert to compare revisions under its own custody. He ordered neither the Needles opened nor any test involving people.

A shortened version of Talia's report would circulate to justify the civilian expense, without publishing strategic locations or naming supposed culprits. The full documentation would remain available to authorities empowered to review safety.

When he finished, Vessa showed him another sheet.

“An office wants to add this sentence to the response: ‘The historical manual was wrong.’”

Maelor crossed it out.

“We still don't know which version applied. And a mistake doesn't tell us who knew about it.”

The day ended without a decisive revelation. But a test date had been canceled, the originals remained with their custodians, and someone would have to account for where each instruction had come from.

Maelor signed the ruling.

He worried that the Network might deteriorate because of a misunderstood order.

He worried more that once the discrepancy was discovered, his colleagues would turn it into a simple story before they understood it.

***End of Episode 31.***
