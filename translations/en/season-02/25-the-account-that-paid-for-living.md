# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 25: The Account That Paid for Living

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel, limited third person.

***

The retired accountant refused to let Inara Vel photograph his face.

He arrived at the newsroom carrying a folder of receipts he said he had retained when a maintenance company was dissolved. He had brought no private clients' names. The pages listed payments for heating, bedding, food, and repairs at a building outside the industrial district.

“These were charged as facility preservation,” he explained. “But it wasn't a machinery building.”

Joren Mal wanted to ask immediately about the people who had lived there.

Inara raised a hand.

“First I need to know what right you have to show me these copies, and whether the originals are still available.”

The man produced the company's liquidation record. He had been assigned to retain billing duplicates and deliver the master ledgers to the commercial archive. He was permitted to show service receipts without disclosing guests' private information, though an audit would still have to verify their provenance.

“What was in that building?” Inara asked.

“Care staff. Kitchen shifts. A garden. I saw school supplies arrive when I went to repair a boiler.”

“Did you see children?”

“Once or twice, from a distance. Nobody ever gave me a list. I didn't ask for one, either.”

For years, the man had assumed it was a protected residence. It might have been. The question was why its services appeared in facilities ledgers rather than under an identifiable care agreement.

Inara agreed to copy only the invoice numbers, dates, descriptions, and visible paying department. She did not take the originals.

The accountant left without asking for his name to be published.

Joren looked at the empty folder left on the desk.

“This could be enormous.”

“Or it could be a legitimate administrative classification we don't understand yet,” Inara replied. “First we need to establish why it was used.”

***

The building was still standing.

It wasn't abandoned. Half the property now served as a municipal tool depot. The other half had been leased to a neighborhood association for daytime activities. Inara made no attempt to enter the locked rooms.

A woman at the entrance identified herself as the association's current administrator.

“We don't know who lived here before,” she said. “All we have is the lease and a floor plan showing the areas we're allowed to use.”

The lease was public. Inara noted when the civil administration had taken over part of the property and the earlier category shown in the property register: protected residential services, with no residents identified.

It did not say who had been housed there or under what guardianship arrangement.

Inara walked to the back of the courtyard. Along the wall were the outlines of old planting beds and a rusting swing that was no longer safe to use. A neighbor sweeping the sidewalk told her he remembered workers leaving at different hours and trucks delivering food through a side entrance.

“Were there minors here?” she asked.

“I saw little people playing some afternoons. Could've been any of the employees' kids.”

Inara wrote down the entire answer, including the uncertainty. She wasn't going to turn a swing into proof of secret child-rearing.

She returned downtown with the public lease. The dates of earlier occupancy overlapped with some of the accountant's receipts, but they did not explain who had authorized the funding.

At the Treasury, she requested a historical payment extract associated with the property.

A clerk found an annual expense for preservation of a service residence and explained that the beneficiary details were protected under a confidentiality order predating the current administration.

“Which authority can explain how the expenditure was classified?” Inara asked.

“Oversight, for the public portion. If you need an old act concerning personal guardianship, you'll have to go to the protection authority. We have no right to release individual records.”

Inara accepted the distinction.

A heating bill could show that someone had paid to keep a house warm.

That alone gave her no right to know who had slept there.

***

The comparison with Oversight took three working days.

The office recovered a financial transfer agreement linking the maintenance expenditure to a protected assistance fund historically administered through an intermediary organization. The public extract had omitted that purpose and presented the disbursements solely as property maintenance.

“Is that illegal?” Inara asked.

The auditor shook her head.

“Confidentiality may be justified to protect beneficiaries. What we need to determine is whether the oversight body received a sufficiently clear description of the purpose of the spending. An account can protect identities without concealing its nature from the people responsible for auditing it.”

“The receipts mention food, clothing, and study materials.”

“I haven't examined the originals you're referring to. The agreement did authorize accommodation and care expenses under administrative guardianship. The problem is that the public category doesn't distinguish those from maintaining storage buildings.”

Inara felt the discovery take on a more uncomfortable shape than a simple accusation.

Money had been allocated to protect people, and the purpose of that money had been obscured behind a physical description of a property. That did not prove the guardianship was illegitimate. Nor did it prove that anyone had been free to refuse it.

The auditor showed her a recent ruling. A review of the old accounts had begun, and orders had been issued to preserve commercial ledgers, agreements, and payment records. Personal files, if they existed, would remain with their protection custodians.

“Can we publish the building's location?” Inara asked.

“The property register is public. But if you link a location to a vulnerable group, you may identify people who are still alive. I'd recommend explaining the flow of funds without reproducing addresses or dates that could allow readers to infer who lived there.”

It wasn't an editorial prohibition. It was a possible harm Inara had no wish to cause.

She requested the advice in writing.

***

Joren was expecting a headline about hidden children.

Inara brought him an article about hidden money.

The evidence was set out in separate columns: commercial duplicates whose provenance was still awaiting definitive verification, the public property entry, Treasury extracts, and the Oversight ruling on the classification of a protected residential expense. Not one source contained a minor's name that could lawfully be published.

“Why call it secret?” Joren asked.

“Because the purpose of the payments didn't appear in the report available for public scrutiny, even though it was stated in a restricted agreement. We can verify that. I'm not going to claim the care was fictional or that the confidentiality was unnecessary.”

“And who lived there?”

“We don't know. Nor should we get that answer by exposing someone.”

Inara invited responses from the successor to the former administrator and from the protection authority. The first acknowledged that the agreement had existed, without naming beneficiaries. The second explained that it would review whether historical confidentiality had improperly limited financial oversight and how to permit individual claims without opening a register of minors to the public.

The newspaper reported the existence of the guardianship funding arrangement under an opaque classification and the opening of an accounting review. It named no restricted Council program and connected the property to no identified person. Too many years, custodians, and possible explanations lay between the documents.

When the edition reached the streets, the retired accountant sent one last note. He remembered a detail not included in his duplicates: after the maintenance contract ended, a correspondence service for the same administrator had continued to be paid, even though the rooms no longer received visitors.

Inara did not turn the recollection into a headline.

She asked him for an invoice or ledger that could corroborate it.

If one existed, it would show that someone had continued paying for an office to receive letters even when there was nobody left whose room needed heating.

But a letter could be a request, a complaint, or a denied answer.

And before opening it, someone would have to prove they had the right to read it.

***End of Episode 25.***
