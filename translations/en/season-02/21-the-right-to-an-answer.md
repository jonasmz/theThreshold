# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 21: The Right to an Answer

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, limited third person.

***

The letter denied a request Kael had never made.

He had asked to access any personal data linked to his name in a series known as the Mirror Index, if such data existed. The response, issued by a classification office of the Council, stated that it could not authorize “general consultation of restricted operational archives.”

Kael Veyran laid the envelope on the table.

“I never asked to consult the entire collection,” he told Dalia Ferren.

The lawyer read the document with a degree of concentration that irritated him more than any comment could have.

“We can challenge the scope of their answer. But we're not going to pretend the office has admitted to holding a file on you.”

The series name appeared in the inherited link in his Personnel record, frozen under a provisional order from the independent reviewer. The date of creation and the date of receipt had been distinguished. The transformation certificate was still missing. Separately, Kael's civil birth record referred to an old documentary guardianship arrangement whose current custodian had not yet been fully identified. Neither line of inquiry proved that they were connected.

It had taken Kael days to frame his request without combining conclusions he could not support. The refusal handed him back exactly the confusion he had tried to avoid.

“I want to know whether they have information about me,” he said. “I don't want to know who the others are.”

“Then we'll demand a decision on that precise question. And an explanation of why confirming or denying its existence might affect someone else's rights.”

Dalia pointed to the notification date. There was a deadline for the appeal.

Kael felt as though another office was measuring his life in business days.

Rain began falling outside the window. He recognized the sound of water striking metal and wood, but the autobiographical memory he had lost after his journey to Selivar did not return. No letter had restored it, and this one could not, either.

He put the response in a folder separate from his civil birth record.

It was a small decision. For the first time that day, it belonged to him.

***

The independent reviewer's office occupied a building where the lights had been dimmed to comply with energy restrictions.

People waited in line with folders: a retired woman seeking a wage correction, two workers disputing custody of a certificate, and a man who needed proof that he had completed a shift. Kael sat beside them, without the special credential he no longer held.

The reviewing officer, Iria Solen, had participated in the preservation of the Personnel link. She was not the custodian of the Mirror series. Nor could she open strategic registers on her own authority.

“The office responded as though you'd requested access to the entire collection,” she said after reading the notice. “I can require them to distinguish personal data, third-party information, and protected methods. I can't guarantee they'll answer yes.”

“Can you verify whether my name is in there?”

“Only if there's legal authority for the comparison and the custodian provides what's needed under safeguards. Otherwise I'd be answering a question about material I haven't examined.”

Dalia asked about the risk that the link might be purged.

Iria showed them a schedule. The provisional order had preserved the metadata without allowing alterations. A hearing involving Personnel and the migration service was due soon. The protection would not last forever.

“We'll request an extension until we identify who transmitted the reference,” she explained. “I don't have to declare its contents true in order to preserve it.”

Kael looked at the field name. For weeks he had wanted to tear it out of the system and hold it up before every official as proof. Now he understood why the word *proof* required more care.

“And if the signature never turns up?”

“The file may record that the data has no verifiable provenance. That affects how it can be used against you. It doesn't necessarily tell us what happened before your birth was registered.”

The statement offered no comfort.

It offered a distinction he could defend.

***

Personnel called Kael and Tessa Ord to a preservation review.

On the table lay two certified copies of his record, the migration manual, and an inventory of unsuccessful searches. The issuing service had replied that it did not hold the signed transformation certificate in its ordinary files. It had not ruled out the possibility that another repository had received it.

A Personnel representative proposed removing the link from his active employment record so that an unverified reference could not affect future employment decisions.

“That might protect me,” Kael admitted. “But if you delete it without preserving its history, no one will ever be able to explain how it got there.”

Tessa confirmed which copy she had seen during the initial proceedings. She attributed neither an author nor an original date to the entry.

Dalia requested a two-part solution: prohibit the link's use as proof of identity or as a disciplinary record, while preserving its modification history under audit custody.

The representative agreed to the first measure. For the second, she insisted on an order from the reviewer.

“We've requested one,” said Iria, attending within the bounds of her authority. “Today we're not deciding anything about another office's archives. Only the integrity of this record.”

The minutes recorded that Personnel would not use the reference as evidence of Kael's personal origins and would preserve the technical record of its incorporation while the appeal was pending.

Kael signed to acknowledge receipt, not to endorse the contents of the link.

On their way out, Tessa walked with him to the corridor.

“If you ever need a work reference, my office can certify the duties you actually performed,” she said. “Not that mark.”

Kael looked at her.

He had spent so long defending his file that he had almost forgotten it also held years of work he had chosen to do.

“Thank you,” he said. “Maybe I'll ask for one soon.”

***

The second refusal arrived after dark.

The classification office had corrected its description of his petition: it now acknowledged that Kael was requesting only his own personal data. Even so, it refused to confirm or deny the existence of individual entries in the collection, citing the risk of identifying third parties and exposing protected procedures. It included the responsible authority, the legal grounds for restriction, and a route for supervised appeal.

It wasn't the answer Kael wanted. At least it was no longer an answer to a different question.

Dalia read the notice over his shoulder.

“This can be challenged precisely. We'll request confidential examination by an authorized independent reviewer, without indiscriminate disclosure.”

“And if that review says I have no right to know?”

“They'll have to explain why. I won't promise you an outcome.”

Kael set the letter on his table beside Personnel's preservation acknowledgment. One office denied him access to material he did not even know concerned him. Another had agreed not to use an unattributed link against him.

He could not combine those decisions and turn them into the truth he was seeking.

But neither was he obliged to accept a classification as an answer to who he was.

He wrote the first paragraph of his appeal.

He did not ask the state to define his life.

He asked it to explain what right it had to conceal from him—even from him—any data it intended to use about that life.

***End of Episode 21.***
