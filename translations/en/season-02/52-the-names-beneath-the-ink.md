# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 52: The Names Beneath the Ink

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel, limited third person.

***

The envelope contained twelve names and a request to publish only one.

Inara Vel opened it on the newsroom table, away from the window where sunlight made every document easier to read. An association of former residents of protected housing had asked for help locating records that might belong to them. Among the copies was a list of beds, years, and shift supervisors.

The one name that could be published belonged to the association's representative. The others had not consented.

“How do we know the people on this list actually lived there?” Joren Mal asked.

“We don't know yet. We don't even know whether they all want us to look for them.”

The representative's name was Vesna Lar. She had grown up in a residence she remembered for its courtyard and tall windows. She knew of no connection between that place and any strategic program. She'd gained access to her own civil records and found a reference to an assistance fund that no longer appeared under the same name in public registers.

Inara remembered the guardianship arrangements she'd investigated months before: accommodation payments classified as maintenance expenses, confidential agreements, and a building whose location she'd never published. They were possible context, not permission to declare every residence part of one scheme.

“I want to know why I can't request my own file,” Vesna said. “I don't want the others to wake up and see their names in a newspaper.”

Inara separated the copies. She left the residents' list intact, sealed under the newsroom's inventory procedure. On a working sheet she recorded only the paying agency's designation and the public agreement numbers.

“I'll investigate the access procedure,” she said. “First I need your permission to describe your case without making you identifiable.”

Vesna agreed. She did not authorize publication of her photograph, address, exact admission date, or the names of those who'd cared for her.

***

The protection office responded with a seven-page form.

It allowed each person concerned to request a search for their own data through an intermediary, without having to disclose their identity to every institution that had participated in their guardianship. It provided for legal assistance, safeguards for third parties, and the right to know whether confidentiality restrictions could be reviewed.

The mechanism existed in the regulations. In practice, nobody had assigned enough staff to handle the historical requests.

“We have four officials for dozens of transferred collections,” the service manager explained. “Some folders still don't have a nominal inventory we can lawfully consult.”

Inara requested statistics: applications received, answered, and pending, without names or individual case files. The office supplied an administrative summary with a date, counting methodology, and a warning that records might overlap between custodians.

Vesna reviewed the figures with a rights advocate.

“If I submit my request, can they use it to look for the other people on the list?”

“Not without a separate legal basis,” the advocate answered. “Your consent applies to your own information.”

Vesna signed her personal application. She did not hand over the list.

An assistant tried to attach the entire roster “to make the search easier.” The advocate stopped him before he copied the names.

“Find the file using the information the applicant has agreed to provide,” she said. “If another comparison is necessary, ask her.”

Inara left without looking at whatever personal file the office might eventually locate.

There was a difference between accompanying someone to a door and walking through it behind them uninvited.

***

The newspaper's editor wanted an image.

“No faces, no building. What will readers look at?”

Inara set a redacted reproduction of the access form on the table beside a chart showing the office's recorded processing times. The delays exceeded those specified by the procedure itself, though they couldn't, on their own, establish deliberate concealment.

“They'll see a right written into the rules and a route that isn't working,” she replied.

Joren suggested drawing a plan of the building from the earlier investigation. Inara refused: even without an address, the combination of courtyard, years, and number of residents might identify people still under confidentiality protections.

The newspaper sought comment from the protection authority and the successor to the former administrator. The first acknowledged delays and announced an inventory audit. The second maintained that some restrictions had protected minors and that not all agreements fell under the same jurisdiction.

Both responses appeared in the article.

Vesna authorized one unattributed quotation: she'd found a reference to her guardianship but still didn't know how to obtain the complete documentation. Before printing it, Inara read the details aloud.

“The word ‘residence’ is fine,” Vesna said. “Remove the year and the description of the windows.”

Inara did.

The article explained the procedure, the gap in service, and the need to preserve the collections. It reproduced no lists and attributed neither a biological identity nor a predetermined fate to the residents.

There was no mystery in the headline about to explode.

There was a public question about who was responsible for safeguarding private rights.

***

Publication produced a response less visible than a spectacular investigation.

The authority announced an advisory desk for people seeking historical records. It committed to accepting individual requests and publishing aggregate progress data without turning the office into a list of people available to other agencies.

Vesna called Inara.

“I received an acknowledgment,” she said. “They still haven't found my file.”

“Then the acknowledgment is the only thing we know you've obtained.”

“It's more than I had.”

After the call, Joren brought her an anonymous message. Someone offered a copy of another residents' list, this time with notes about technical aptitude. It included neither a source nor authorization to disclose it.

“It could matter,” he said.

“It could. Save the message without starting a search by name. First we'll ask for provenance and a lawful means of examining it.”

Inara thought of the man with the injured hand, whose promised payment still needed verification, and the witnesses who had chosen to speak within limits. None deserved to learn that a story about rights had turned into a catalog of other people's lives.

The newsroom emptied after the final edition.

Inara reviewed Vesna's folder. It held consent, statistics, official responses, and an application receipt. It did not hold the life the woman was hoping to understand.

The folder containing the names remained closed.

For the first time, Inara considered that keeping it closed was also part of doing the job well.

***End of Episode 52.***
