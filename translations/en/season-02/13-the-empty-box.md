# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 13: The Empty Box

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel, limited third person.

***

The woman refused to sit beside the newsroom window.

She asked for a table away from the apprentices and kept her hands on a closed folder. Inara Vel had agreed to hear her out without recording her voice or taking photographs.

“My brother authorized medical transport, not publication,” the visitor said. “When I complained about the cost of storing his medication, they sent me a summary that includes details of his treatment. I don't know who might have read those details before I did.”

Inara asked permission to examine the letter. The woman unfolded only the administrative response. It showed a list of institutional recipients and an unfilled consent box.

“Did your brother leave this blank?” Inara asked.

“I don't know. He was too ill to deal with forms. The nurse explained the transfer to him. That did happen. But nobody told us his medical history would be sent along with the bill.”

Inara separated the pages.

The issue was not whether the treatment had been necessary. It was whether medical information had circulated beyond those entitled to know it. A missing signature on this copy did not, by itself, prove a lack of consent. The original might be held in another archive, or a different authorization might have been used.

“I can investigate the procedure,” she said. “I won't publish your brother's name or his treatment.”

“I want it to be clear that he isn't a missing box.”

“I want to understand that, too.”

The woman closed her folder.

“Then ask why the only line they left blank was the one saying we could refuse.”

***

At the public compensation office, Inara requested the data-handling protocol for medical deliveries.

The official provided the current version and an earlier one. Both covered payment for refrigerated storage and the transmission of minimal information to the organization funding a service. Neither permitted sending a complete medical history to justify an ordinary invoice.

“What happens when the person concerned can't sign?” Inara asked.

“The applicable care procedure takes effect. There may be a representative, a clinical attestation, or a narrowly defined legal exception. An empty box doesn't create a presumption of consent.”

“Who verifies that an exception was legitimate?”

“The health authority, not Oversight. We can review whether the charges were correct and which data fields the payer required. We cannot inspect individual medical histories.”

Inara requested a public copy of the interoffice communication template. The approved version used abbreviated identifiers to justify expenses. The older form included an open-text field called “user observations,” whose length depended on whoever completed it.

“When did you stop using that?” she asked.

The official pointed to the replacement circular. It had been issued before the transport restrictions, although some offices had continued using printed forms from older editions.

“That doesn't demonstrate that complete medical histories were sent,” he warned. “Only that the form allowed more information to be written down than was necessary.”

Inara noted the distinction.

A flawed design could create an opportunity for abuse without proving anyone had taken advantage of it. She needed testimony from people who had seen what was actually sent, and why.

***

At the lower district dispensary, the receiving supervisor agreed to discuss the general procedure.

The building was full of small sounds: glass bottles being arranged on wood, a dripping faucet, a child dragging a chair. Two shelves stood empty because deliveries had arrived late.

“I can't show you patient files,” the supervisor said. “But I can show you the form we use now. It's posted at the counter.”

Inara compared the new template with the one she had seen at Compensation.

“Did you used to write diagnoses in the open-text field?”

“Some employees wrote down the reason a shipment was urgent. Others only noted the need for refrigeration. When we were told the format had changed, we withdrew the old sheets.”

“When did you receive that instruction?”

The supervisor searched for acknowledgment of the circular. She couldn't find it in the recent correspondence register. There was a training note signed by two workers, but no date showing when the new batch of forms arrived.

“I can't tell you whether an order went missing or the printer was late,” she admitted. “I can confirm we stopped circulating the old forms once the new ones arrived.”

A nurse came over to ask about a delivery. She told the supervisor that a family had been asking for three days about a box held in storage. The carrier had requested another supporting document, and nobody knew which of the two forms they were supposed to use.

Inara didn't interfere. The nurse had a problem to resolve with authorized personnel, not with a journalist.

The supervisor looked at the stack of printed forms.

“If you publish this, explain that burning every record won't fix it. Without the paperwork, some people can't get their medication.”

The words stayed with Inara on her way back to the newspaper.

***

Joren Mal read the draft and pointed to the word “consent.”

“Can we say the municipality disclosed data without permission?”

“No. We can say the previous form allowed information that the payment procedure didn't need, that a specific complaint has been filed with the authority, and that the consent box on the supplied copy is blank. We haven't seen the original.”

“That's not the same as having no consent.”

“Exactly.”

Inara had requested responses from Compensation and the health office. The first confirmed it was reviewing which fields had been transmitted to the payers. The second said it would undertake a protected examination of the family's complaint and notify the person concerned of its findings. It offered no clinical details, nor should it have.

Joren turned his pen between his fingers.

“Some readers are going to mistake an empty box for an experiment.”

“Then we'd better work hard to stop them.”

Before submitting the article, Inara met the visitor again. She showed her only the passages based on her testimony and requested explicit permission to publish an anonymous quotation without treatment details. The woman corrected one word: her brother had not “disappeared from the system.” His medication had arrived late, and he was alive.

Inara amended the draft.

The following morning, the article appeared in a short edition. It called for data protection, accessible complaint procedures, and a firm date for withdrawing old forms. It did not imply that the family's case was identical to the separate issue of third-party medical custody.

That afternoon, the first formal response from the health authority arrived. The examination had found a notation referring to consent recorded in a different department. The authorization had not yet been verified against its source; both records were to be preserved and the family notified before any decision on whether the disclosure had been legitimate.

Inara read the letter over the pounding of the press.

The box remained empty.

Now there was a verifiable obligation to explain who had acted in its place.

***End of Episode 13.***
