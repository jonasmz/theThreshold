# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 04: The Name Between Two Invoices

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel, limited third person.

***

The printing press stopped again before the first sheet was finished.

Inara Vel looked up from her notes as the pounding machinery fell abruptly silent. From the back of the shop, an apprentice announced that the power window had ended earlier than expected.

“Fewer pages tomorrow,” Joren Mal said.

“Fewer people willing to pay for them if we don't explain why,” Inara replied.

On her desk lay three complaints from residents about low water pressure and a notice from Services. The municipality had temporarily reduced the load on a pump while awaiting a replacement suitable for installation. The measure was public. The precise technical reason was absent from the notice.

Joren spotted Sera Nhal's name in her notes.

“We already reported that engineer's rejection of a part.”

“And now another delivery couldn't be installed. I'm not going to say it's the same part when it has a different serial number.”

“Readers don't care about those distinctions.”

“The pump does.”

Inara went out with a clean notebook and a copy of her earlier article. She needed to establish whether the second incident belonged to the same commercial chain or whether the resemblance was simply the result of a supply crisis affecting the entire sector.

In the street, a woman argued with a water delivery worker. She demanded an arrival time, not an explanation of who was to blame.

Inara wrote down the question. She would print it if she could obtain a verifiable answer.

***

At the oversight office, they handed her the public extract from the quarantine receipt.

The new part had arrived, but no certificate had yet been attached. The document recorded its serial number, the warranty order, and the decision to prevent installation until the required test was obtained. It also identified the company that had prepared the packaging: Pelder Warehouses.

Inara recognized the name.

It appeared in a payment schedule examined during her investigation into Arven. Not as the principal supplier, but as a subcontractor handling and transporting materials. The schedule was part of a public extract; it did not reveal every job performed under the contract.

“Is it the same order?” she asked the official.

“We don't know. One concerns a supply invoice reviewed in the earlier investigation. This is a warranty replacement. A company appearing in both doesn't prove it handled the same batch.”

“Can I obtain the dispatch dates?”

“The ones that are public, yes. Workers' names and internal routes aren't included.”

Inara paid the fees, waited for the certified copies, and compared the pages on a bench in the corridor.

They were two separate transactions. Both named Pelder. One was linked to Arven's disputed invoice; the other to the replacement Sera had placed in quarantine. What was missing between them was the document that would establish whether the company had manufactured, tested, or approved either plate.

The journalist drew one column for facts and another for questions.

It was a habit that had saved her from publishing mistakes. It also cost her headlines.

Before leaving, she requested the public version of the quarantine record and asked about complaints from affected neighborhoods. She was told there was a service schedule and that an urgent assistance request had been registered. No name was provided.

Inara closed her notebook.

A story about metal parts could become a story about people if she bothered to look at who was waiting at the other end of the pipe.

***

Sera met her outside the workshop.

“I'm not showing you a sealed box,” she said before Inara could greet her.

“I wasn't going to ask. I have the public extract. I want to check which parts you can personally confirm.”

Inara read out the points without showing figures from unrelated cases.

Sera confirmed that she had not installed the replacement because the required test was missing. She had taken operational measurements, reduced the load under municipal authorization, and filed a conditional receipt. Her bench could not certify the load-bearing performance required by the standard.

“Do you know Pelder?” Inara asked.

“The dispatch note bears its identification. I don't know who made the part or who was supposed to test it. A packaging stamp answers neither question.”

“Pelder appears as Arven's subcontractor in the other case.”

“Then prove that with your documents. I'm not going to join two numbers just because it suits your story.”

Inara smiled without amusement.

“You wouldn't be the first to tell me that.”

The engineer leaned against the wall.

“If you write that the workshop never received a part, I'll contradict you. We received one. If you write that we approved it for installation, I'll contradict that too.”

“I'll write the distinction.”

“Then add something else. Every day we wait means double shifts and less water for part of the city. A headline about who is guilty won't solve the problem.”

Inara asked permission to quote those words and use Sera's name. Sera agreed after reviewing the exact wording. No other workshop employee was identified.

***

Arven's commercial office had a small room for handling complaints.

A representative read Inara's questions and requested that his response be entered in writing. He maintained that the replacement had been processed under warranty, that conformity testing belonged to the supplier chain, and that a missing document in the packaging did not necessarily mean the test had never been performed.

“Who can produce the report?” Inara asked.

“The subcontractor responsible for preparation.”

“Pelder?”

“That's the name on the dispatch order.”

Inara requested permission to quote the reply. The representative granted it in writing, adding that Arven rejected any suggestion of criminal responsibility while the review of the earlier invoice remained open.

At Pelder Warehouses, she was received through a loading window. A supervisor agreed to take her questions but would not discuss clients without permission. That afternoon an official reply arrived: Pelder confirmed receiving and forwarding packages, but did not perform technical tests or issue fitness certificates for regulating equipment.

Inara read the two statements side by side.

Arven directed the question of testing toward the subcontractor's chain.

The subcontractor said it did not test parts.

There might be a third-party laboratory. Its identity might simply have been omitted from the replies. Or there might be a certificate that no one had ever issued.

The contradiction was specific enough to demand an answer.

***

Joren had to choose between a short front page and a long accusation.

Inara showed him the sources: the quarantine extract, two public commercial records, an authorized interview with Sera, and written responses from both companies. She marked in red the claims that could not be printed as facts: that the two parts were identical, that Pelder had falsified a test, or that Arven had ordered the installation of an unsafe replacement.

“Then what are we publishing?” Joren asked.

“That the replacement still has no certification submitted to the workshop, that the same subcontractor appears in two different transactions, and that the companies direct responsibility for the test to different places. We can publish the substance of both responses in full.”

“And the water?”

Inara placed the municipal schedule in front of him.

“This too. A woman needs to fill a tank tonight. She isn't going to wait for someone to win a commercial argument.”

Joren authorized the shortened edition.

As the apprentice set the type, Inara received another note from the oversight office. A formal request had been opened to identify the issuer of the missing certificate. The companies would have to answer within the case record, where their responses could be compared.

It was not a conviction.

It was an obligation to provide a verifiable answer.

When the press began pounding again, Inara looked at the column of her article reserved for the laboratory's name.

It was still blank.

***End of Episode 04.***
