# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 26: The Simulator Without a Door

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Sera Nhal, limited third person.

***

The test bench was so far removed from the Network that it didn't even have a connection anyone could activate by accident.

Sera Nhal checked that before accepting the laboratory key. The power feeder had been removed from the old calibration module, leaving the empty terminals exposed, and two measurement bridges had been installed that could carry only low-intensity signals. No Threshold, no Anchor, no line to a destination. The apparatus could imitate a sequence of responses, but it couldn't open a route.

“We specified it that way so nobody would mistake a simulation for an authorization,” Talia Mer explained.

The laboratory lay beneath a maintenance hall. The low ceiling threw back every thump of the ventilation fans. A work lamp flickered because the building shared its accumulators with the annex's night watch.

Sera set her bag on a table.

“Who's paying for the hours we're using?”

“The Commission approved a technical review budget. Two operators per shift; everyone else stays on monitoring duty. If we use up today's allocated reserve, we stop.”

There was a reason they'd called Sera. She could tell the difference between a cooling failure, a return-line fluctuation, and a reading contaminated by the instruments themselves. She hadn't been part of the team that developed the bench, and she had no access to the files of the people the historical procedures had tried to use.

Three indicators on the panel were covered.

“Biological, mnemonic, and technical,” she read from the test labels.

“Those describe functions,” Talia said. “We're testing circuits only. No person will be connected as authentication material.”

Sera studied the cover over the first input.

The machine had been designed to ask a question that a piece of metal should never answer as though it were alive.

***

The first test used an artificial signal generated by the bench itself.

The response reached the level required to detect circuit integrity, but it failed to reproduce the variability of a living organism. When the control program tried to advance to the second phase, an interlock disconnected the output.

“That's not a failure,” Sera said. “It's the limit we declared before we started.”

A young technician, Nel Or, had expected the panel to accept more steps.

“If we fine-tune the curve, we might fool the detector.”

“We might fool this laboratory detector,” Talia replied. “That doesn't mean we know every challenge the original regulator might issue, or how to keep it running under a real load.”

Nel didn't argue. He looked down at the power consumption.

The second test replaced recorded signals with variable challenges directed at a trained response program. The imitation got some associations right, then failed when the order changed. Sera saw on the paper strip that power wasn't the problem. The response had become repetition.

“Would Imprinting have solved that?” Nel asked.

“An Imprint isn't an entire person stored away,” Sera replied. “And a recorded memory can't improvise experiences that never happened.”

The third input required a sequenced technical maneuver. Talia left it disabled. The simulator didn't have all the parameters needed to verify it; blindly testing combinations would only establish how much the circuit could tolerate.

A vibration ran across the table.

The auxiliary accumulator was draining faster than the model predicted.

Sera switched off the load and checked the dissipator's temperature. One of the measurement-line connectors had developed resistance and was consuming power as heat.

“Electronics have a price even when they aren't doing anything useful,” she said.

Talia ordered the defect documented before anyone attributed the power drop to some mysterious response from the system.

***

Outside in the yard, Sera found a maintenance crew waiting for the backup accumulator to be returned.

A woman held a case of instruments.

“They told us it would be free by noon. We need to inspect the lower district's return line before the flow drops.”

Sera checked the time. Preparing the test and documenting the faulty connector had taken two hours longer than planned.

“You're getting the reserve back,” she said. “Today's test ends here. They have a real installation to maintain.”

Nel objected from the doorway.

“With one more hour, we could repeat the second sequence.”

“With one more hour, we could lose our safety margin for a water emergency,” Sera answered. “Request another test window and put it in the budget.”

The young man closed his notebook. He didn't look convinced, but he helped disconnect the bench from the power supply.

The laboratory manager explained that there was pressure to produce a result for the ethics review. Some officials wanted proof that people couldn't be replaced; others wanted proof that a machine was enough and nobody ever needed to be questioned.

“Both conclusions would be premature,” Sera said. “All we've established is what this model can do under these conditions.”

The crew took the accumulator for its service inspection. The laboratory retained a power record showing the final reading and the thermal incident; Talia's copy went into a log separate from the technical results.

Later, Sera opened the report she was supposed to sign.

One sentence read: “Simulation of the ancient sequence not viable.”

She crossed out *not viable* and wrote: “This isolated bench does not reproduce all the required factors and cannot establish whether an operational-scale replacement would be viable.”

“That's less definitive,” Nel said.

“It's true,” she replied.

And sometimes saying less kept someone from treating everything else as permission.

***

Mira Desen came to collect the report without crossing the laboratory's safety boundary.

The physician was reviewing human-protection procedures under the limited mandate of an independent commission. She had no need to touch the simulator to understand the difference between a measured result and the decision some people hoped to justify with it.

“Would you need a person to complete the test?” she asked.

“This apparatus can't reproduce all the authentication factors,” Sera replied. “But I'm not going to declare that a person is necessary just because our design is incomplete.”

Mira nodded.

“That's what I need to report.”

Talia handed her the day's inventory: power used, hours worked, interlocks, physically isolated terminals, uncertainties, and the reason testing had stopped. The annex listed the cost of the testing window the return-line crew had been unable to use.

“We haven't found a solution,” Talia said.

Sera looked at the dark panel.

“We found an honest limit. And we know where we're wasting power.”

The report went to the technical custodian and the ethics reviewers through separate channels. Neither office received medical histories or the names of protected individuals.

Before leaving, Nel asked whether they would test the bench again.

“After the connector's repaired and we've reserved power another installation won't need,” Sera answered. “And only to answer a question we can actually measure.”

Above them in the maintenance hall, the lower district return line resumed its uneven rumble.

There was a water system in need of repair, and a damaged Network demanding solutions. Beneath the floor sat a machine unable to answer all the ancient questions on its own.

Sera locked the laboratory door, certain that—for once—opening no other door was a complete technical decision.

***End of Episode 26.***
