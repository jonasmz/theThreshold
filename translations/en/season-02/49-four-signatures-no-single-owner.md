# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 49: Four Signatures, No Single Owner

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Maelor Thane, limited third person.

***

The first proposed agreement fit on one page.

Maelor Thane read it standing beside the Licensing representative. The heading stated that the Continuity Commission would coordinate all safety reviews and testing authorizations for as long as Network restrictions remained in force.

That was precisely what he couldn't accept.

“The Commission cannot become the judge of its own decisions,” he said.

The representative closed his folder.

“If every department retains a veto, we won't even be able to approve a bench test.”

“If an authorization lets one office bypass another's jurisdiction, it won't be safe either.”

Since Maelor had rejected a real-load reopening, Licensing had requested administrative review. The dispute wasn't only technical. The municipality was paying for additional ground transport, workshops were losing hours, and operators at the Western Annex worked extended monitoring shifts. The Commission held historical documents it couldn't disclose indiscriminately, while the civilian safeguards office insisted that experiments involving people must not be disguised as routine maintenance.

A joint committee seemed reasonable. The draft would have made it a body empowered to control everything.

Talia Mer had prepared three sheets: one for facility permits, another for isolated instrument testing, and a third for the rights of any human participant. They could not be confused with one another.

“Sera's bench doesn't open nodes,” she explained. “Right now it hasn't even validated its safety shutdown. It doesn't need Transit authorization to inspect wiring without power, but it does need laboratory oversight and a budget.”

“And a test on the Annex?” the delegate asked.

“That would require technical safety review, Licensing authority, and whatever safeguards apply,” Maelor replied. “No single signature would be sufficient.”

The delegate pointed to the heading.

“Then we'll have to rewrite it.”

Maelor picked up his pen.

For once, delay seemed like a useful form of work.

***

The negotiation took place in a municipal department's dining room.

It wasn't a solemn setting. A boiler dripped near the window, and an assistant had to empty the container beneath it from time to time. Arel Vin, the safeguards representative, was the first to lay down conditions.

“There will be no shared database of people considered potentially suitable,” she said. “Individual assessments will stay with their custodians. We don't need people's identities to study instrumental methods.”

Maelor had no objection. Restricted records had told him of Project Mirror's existence and purpose, but he did not possess the personal reports of those it might have affected. Technical knowledge of a system did not make him anyone's guardian.

The municipality requested an agreement about costs.

“Every time a test is suspended, shipments move onto our roads,” its representative said. “We need estimates, funding, and enough notice to rearrange shifts.”

Licensing wanted test permits to take effect automatically if no office answered within a short period.

Talia shook her head.

“An expert's silence doesn't prove an installation can dissipate a load. For a test that changes the containment regime, we need affirmative safety approval.”

Arel added a second distinction.

“A person's silence cannot be interpreted as consent, either.”

The table fell quiet.

A transport worker invited to describe the civilian costs asked to speak.

“You're arguing about how to authorize things,” he said. “We need to know who's listening when you prohibit them and we pay for it in lost work.”

Maelor proposed a public impact annex and a claims channel. The municipality would evaluate assistance within its own jurisdiction; the Commission would contribute nonsensitive information about schedules and safety needs. Nobody would promise subsidies using another office's funds.

The boiler dripped again.

Maelor considered how much time institutions could spend disputing who had the right to command, and how little they spent recording who was affected by their commands.

***

The second draft ran to eight pages.

Oversight was divided by type of activity. Tests on physically isolated benches would require an instrument protocol, a verified cutoff, and independent observation, without Network access or personal data. Any real-load maneuver on strategic infrastructure would require affirmative authorization from Safety and Licensing, with technical justification and a right of review. Proposals involving human beings would additionally require competent ethical assessment, specific consent, and withdrawal protections, with no contractual shortcuts.

“And if the new method proves a person isn't needed?” the Licensing delegate asked.

“Then you may request an instrumental test within those limits,” Maelor replied. “That wouldn't make it permission to open a node.”

The Commission would retain its historical originals. Civil archives and courts would retain theirs. An independent reviewer could request specific materials through reasoned procedures and receive redacted versions when those were sufficient. No secretariat would gain authority to centralize citizens' files simply because their records used related codes.

Arel underlined the personal-data clause.

“I want an explicit ban on using clinical results obtained for identification as authorization to operate.”

Maelor added the clause without asking which particular person might benefit.

The Licensing delegate requested a procedure for appealing technical vetoes. They agreed to review by the competent authority, with reasons available to the parties and no test proceeding during the appeal if an unmitigated risk remained.

Talia checked the final page.

“We haven't specified who pays for outside examination when a test is suspended because of a design defect.”

It would come from the test sponsor's budget, subject to oversight, rather than being automatically deducted from a technician's wages or the civilian service whose energy reserve had been reclaimed.

Maelor watched the agreement grow heavier and less ambiguous at the same time.

Every new sentence prevented someone from assuming a power merely because another department had left a box blank.

***

The signatures did not arrive together.

First the municipality signed the costs annex. Then Licensing accepted the review procedure, reserving its position on administrative deadlines. The safeguards authority ratified the privacy measures within its jurisdiction, and the Commission approved technical custody rules. The judicial body overseeing other cases did not sign: it retained its independence and could cooperate only through its own orders.

Maelor accepted that absence as a proper limit.

The agreement would take effect for new proposals. It did not retroactively validate earlier acts or automatically lift the Annex restrictions. The ethics commission established weeks before would keep its independent mandate; it would not become subordinate to a meeting of engineers.

A public copy summarized the procedures, appeal routes, and civilian information obligations. The strategic annexes remained confidential, subject to review. No list of people was attached.

Vessa brought the final version to Maelor's office.

“Someone asked whether we can now announce an alternative solution,” she said.

“We can announce a procedure for testing one without concentrating power or drafting anyone into it by default. The results are still partial.”

“That's not very encouraging news.”

Maelor studied the four signatures.

“It may be to someone who could otherwise be forced to participate.”

Talia called from the laboratory. Sera's proposal for an independent safety shutdown was ready for unpowered review. The new agreement provided a technical process and funding for outside expertise; it did not yet authorize applying power.

Maelor signed the referral acknowledgment.

The Network remained fatigued. Warnings issued before the Fracture were confirmed as received by historical offices, not by identifiable individual decision-makers. Equipment failures, civilian harm, and unanswered questions persisted.

But there was a tangible difference: no official could single-handedly turn the need for a repair into a mandate over a facility, somebody else's budget, or a person's body.

Maelor switched off his lamp.

The agreement needed several signatures because no participant owned everything they were trying to protect.

***End of Episode 49.***
