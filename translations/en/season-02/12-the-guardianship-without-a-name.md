# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 12: The Guardianship Without a Name

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, limited third person.

***

There was a box on the form he had never noticed before.

Kael Veyran discovered it at a desk in the civil registry office while waiting for a certificate identifying who had requested the registration of his birth. He wasn't trying to replace his birth certificate with an older document. The data reviewer had asked him to trace the missing transmission record between his employment file and the restricted series. To distinguish an identity reference from a mere archival convention, Kael needed to understand how his civil record had been assembled.

The clerk showed him the bottom margin of his own entry.

“This isn't your parents' names,” she explained. “It's a historical documentary guardianship field. The office retained it when the old registers were transferred.”

A short code stood beside the seal. Kael had seen it for years without paying attention, because the main portion of his birth certificate was valid without that line.

“Does it mean I was under court-appointed guardianship?” he asked.

“I can't say that. Some forms of guardianship concerned the child directly. Others identified the office responsible for verifying or keeping a minor's documents. This format was used for a long time.”

“Can I examine the original entry?”

“You have the right to request a breakdown of information concerning you. If the register contains other people's data, we'll have to separate what can be disclosed. And a complete copy might not exist.”

Kael paid the fee, received the list of requirements, and left with an acknowledgment of his request.

The city had learned to wait behind service counters. He thought he had learned it, too.

Only when he reached the street did he realize he had gripped the folder so tightly that his fingers had left marks along its edge.

***

Dalia Ferren asked him to say aloud exactly what he wanted to prove.

“That my birth record contains a guardianship code,” Kael answered.

“You already know that.”

“I want to know who might have used it to verify my employment details.”

“That's a different question. A better one.”

The lawyer placed the migration manual received during the previous episode on the table beside the new civil records request. There was no certified documentary connection between them. The guardianship code appeared on the birth record; a reference to an external series appeared in his Personnel file. Nothing established that they belonged to the same chain.

“If you join them now,” Dalia warned, “it will look as though you're asking to correct one thing on the basis of the other.”

Kael stared at the two sheets.

In his kitchen, he still kept the letter he had written to the woman who raised him after he lost the memory of that rainy afternoon. The letter belonged to no case file. Sometimes it angered him that a form seemed more likely to survive than a human voice.

“I had a childhood,” he said.

Dalia looked at him without hurrying him.

“You don't have to prove that to me.”

“I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm saying that whatever this mark means, I won't let an office treat the years of my life as a deduction from a box on a form.”

“Then let's separate two rights: the right to learn who entered the code, and the right to decide what you do with that information.”

Kael drafted a narrowly scoped request. He sought its origin, the administrative definition in force when his record was created, and evidence of later amendments. He did not ask for other children's records or presume documentary guardianship meant physical custody of a person.

Dalia read it.

“I can accompany you to the comparison. But there's another question that doesn't depend on me.”

“Which is?”

“Are you prepared to accept that some members of your family may know something they never put on paper—and may not want to tell you?”

Kael thought for a moment.

“I'm prepared to ask without treating their silence as a confession.”

***

The civil registry's response came in two parts.

The first reproduced the general explanation of the codes. The second offered an in-person consultation of an old register, with entries concerning other minors concealed behind a screen and a clerk responsible for ensuring that only the relevant entry was displayed.

Dalia accompanied Kael. The clerk opened the volume on a wooden cradle to avoid straining its spine.

The ink in his entry had partly faded. The name of the woman who raised him appeared in the section identifying the person responsible for his care when his childhood residence was registered. Kael knew that relationship from his life and from the papers kept at home. Seeing it in a book didn't make it more true, but it protected it against an administrative interpretation that might erase it through ignorance.

The guardianship code was repeated in the next column.

The clerk pointed to a note.

“At that time, this code indicated that evidence supporting the origin of certain details had not been entered into the general register. Verification was referred to a supplementary protection register. It doesn't mean every child with this code was adopted, orphaned, or subject to the same measure.”

“Where is that supplementary register?”

“Who currently holds it is under review. We have the register reference and the transfer entry, but it isn't here.”

Kael felt the answer moving away just as he seemed to be approaching it.

“Who signed the transfer?”

The clerk searched the movement log. She found a transaction number and the legible signature of the person who handed the book to another office. The receipt box held an abbreviation the current manual did not explain.

Dalia requested that the document's physical condition be recorded before a reproduction was made.

There was no revelation about Kael's birth. Only two facts: a woman had formally assumed responsibility for his care, and part of the verification of his details had been kept outside the ordinary civil register.

Even so, Kael took a long time to stand.

For months, he had felt that documents were trying to take something away from him. For the first time, one offered simple evidence of a real responsibility someone had accepted for his sake.

***

On his way back, Kael stopped outside the house where he had lived as a child.

The facade had been painted a different color. The metal gutter still slanted toward the first-floor window. It had rained that morning, and a late drop fell onto the wooden frame.

Kael recognized the materials. The afternoon he had lost crossing the Threshold did not return.

It was an absence he knew, not a door he could open.

He took out the authorized copy of the entry. It contained the record of care he had just seen, with other minors' information excluded. The office had recorded that the supplementary reference had yet to be located.

On the back, Dalia had written the only next request she considered proportionate: establish which authority now oversaw the old protection register before asking for any of its pages.

Kael put the paper away without placing it alongside the personal letter in his kitchen.

When he arrived home, he found a reply from the reviewer of his employment history. The transmission record connecting Personnel and the Mirror Index was still missing; no relationship had been established between that search and the civil guardianship record.

There was, however, one procedural detail attached: one of the offices named in the employment migration had historically used the same family of supplementary codes for protected files.

The note warned that a family of codes identified neither an individual nor a program.

Kael read it twice.

He had no answer about his origins.

He had another place to ask someone to explain why a child had needed a second way to record his story.

***End of Episode 12.***
