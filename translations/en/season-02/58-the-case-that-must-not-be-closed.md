# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 58: The Case That Must Not Be Closed

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Daren Holt, limited third person.

***

The prosecutor arrived with a proposal to provisionally close part of the case.

Daren Holt read it without sitting down. The document acknowledged that the corpse presented at the execution wasn't the condemned man's, that a clinical report pointed toward failed stabilization, and that a medical instrument had been preserved. But it argued that, without fully identifying the dead person or conclusively connecting the recording strip to the session, continuing every criminal investigative step would be disproportionate.

“I'm not asking to close the investigation into the substitution,” the prosecutor said. “Only the experimentation line until more evidence appears.”

Daren looked at the folder.

“What happens to the preservation orders and to the people who've testified about the training procedures?”

“We could keep copies.”

“The originals are held by other custodians. A copy doesn't guarantee preservation when the territorial order expires.”

The prosecutor drew a long breath.

“The case takes time and money. There are current cases involving identified people still waiting for hearings.”

Daren knew the argument. It wasn't false. Officials worked overtime to preserve evidence, while judges had to determine which investigations merited urgent resources.

Mara Ellin came in carrying the medical summary. She had compared the body's injuries with stabilization models, but still couldn't establish which specific authorization the person had signed or who had ordered the procedure to continue after the safety margin was lost.

“There's another problem,” she said. “The training agency located a sessions index, but it requires an order distinguishing patients from apprentices. If we suspend this line, we may lose the chance to request it while the records still exist.”

The prosecutor did not withdraw her proposal.

“Then you'll have to show why that search is proportionate.”

Daren looked again at the document.

It was a question they could answer without inventing an identity.

***

The hearing brought together the prosecution, defense, and data-protection representatives.

Defense counsel did not ask for evidence to be destroyed. She asked that each new investigative measure have a precisely defined purpose and that nobody be investigated merely for having worked at a center whose administration had come under scrutiny.

“We can't declare someone guilty for signing a maintenance sheet,” she said. “Nor can we use every apprentice's records to search for someone whose name we don't know.”

The judge agreed.

Daren proposed restricting the search to a period identified by three sources: the medical movement entered in a transport register, the treatment window in the clinical report, and the series of trials referred to in the general index. Authorized custodians would make the comparison without giving the court complete lists of unrelated patients or students.

“What do you expect to obtain?” the judge asked.

“A session-assignment receipt and, if there is one, a reference to specific consent or to its documented absence. I'm not asking you to treat a signature as valid without knowing what it authorized.”

The defense requested a role in determining the method of selection. The prosecutor asked that every negative response and every repository lacking records be documented, so an absence could not be presented as deliberate concealment.

The judge accepted both conditions.

Elia Ren, the former training assistant, had already testified under protective measures. Her shift notes confirmed certain days of work, not her presence in the room where the fatal procedure took place. Nobody asked to question her again to extract a certainty she didn't possess.

For the first time, Daren felt that part of the case was moving forward precisely because they had stopped asking every witness to solve it all.

***

The order was limited to preservation, inventory, and comparison.

An official from the medical repository called that afternoon. A cooling unit holding a group of recording strips had failed. It wasn't the same sealed unit containing the drum already preserved, but another set of mixed records, some sensitive to heat and humidity.

Daren asked the custodian to apply its ordinary conservation protocol and document the incident. He had no jurisdiction to order the materials transferred to another court.

The territorial judge authorized moving the materials to another shelf under supervision, without opening envelopes or photographing personal data outside the scope of the request. Two technicians performed the transfer wearing dry gloves, recorded the temperature, and preserved the old seals inside the new packaging.

“If we don't do this, there may be nothing legible tomorrow,” one said.

“And if we open everything today,” the examiner replied, “we could destroy the chain that establishes where each piece came from.”

The defense observed the recustody process by communicator and objected to the date on one seal. The examiner incorporated the objection into the record without questioning the need to move the materials.

By the time they finished, the repository had preserved records that might be relevant and others that nobody yet had the right to read. The cost was an additional day of cooling and two workers temporarily diverted from an urgent repair.

Daren made sure the expense was recorded.

A criminal investigation couldn't feed on invisible resources.

***

At the end of the week, the judge refused to close that line of inquiry in its present state.

She did not find that criminal experimentation on identified people had been proved. She ordered the investigation to remain under judicial oversight, with a review deadline, proportionate preservation, and the right of both sides to challenge findings. The prosecutor could reconsider her hypotheses; the defense could contest new measures; custodians would retain their originals.

The ruling distinguished two questions: whether consent and safety had been violated during a human procedure, and how a different person's corpse had ended up at a public execution. It didn't assume that everyone involved in one chain knew about the other.

The prosecutor read the decision in silence.

“We'll continue,” she said. “But I need your next request to be for something less than another entire repository.”

“It will be,” Daren answered. “One precisely defined question.”

Mara waited in the corridor.

“Is this justice for the dead person?”

Daren thought of the body whose name still couldn't be verified and the condemned man whose whereabouts were still unknown.

“It's keeping the file from abandoning its search for an answer before we've exhausted the relevant sources,” he said.

It didn't sound heroic.

Back at his office, he found an unsigned letter, delivered by the same ordinary channel that carried hundreds of communications. It claimed to contain a list of former participants, but included neither an original nor evidence of provenance.

Daren logged it as unverified correspondence and sent it to the judge to determine whether protective measures were appropriate. He did not use it to identify or track people himself.

He closed the case folder and placed it on the shelf for active investigations.

He hadn't finished the investigation.

He'd kept it on a path where a future answer could still count as evidence.

***End of Episode 58.***
