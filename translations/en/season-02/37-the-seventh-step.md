# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 37: The Seventh Step

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, limited third person.

***

The manual had six steps. The inventory said it should have seven.

Kael Veyran discovered the difference during a cataloging assessment, not while searching for his own past. Tessa Ord had offered him an interview to supervise the conservation workshop. The assessment asked him to explain how he would handle a collection of safety guides returned by different technical schools.

The examiner placed two publicly available booklets on the table, along with an edition record and a library incident sheet.

“You have twenty minutes,” he said. “We don't need you to repair the device. We want to know what you'd put in the catalog.”

Kael opened the newer guide. The page entitled “Checks Before Commissioning” listed six steps and left a blank line before the operator's signature. In the earlier edition, there had been a seventh check: wait for a second rest window and verify the return response without introducing a new load.

No strategic parameters were given. Only the existence of the test and its place on the list.

“The change might have been deliberate,” Kael said. “I'd need the revision notice and the scope of both editions before marking it as an error.”

The examiner pointed to the equipment-family codes. They were identical.

“So it's been omitted?”

“In this edition's shortened text, yes. I don't know whether a separate supplement replaces it.”

Kael recorded the exact wording. He didn't write “a safety measure was removed” or “a defect was covered up.”

In the margin of the catalog record was a reference to the workshop-copy repository. Its batch number matched the guide on the table.

There was a way to investigate further without opening any restricted archive.

He requested authorization to consult the public supplement index.

***

The training repository lay behind a frosted-glass door.

The assistant, Rena Tor, would not allow Kael to remove booklets from the shelf. She showed him the catalog and lending register, where maintenance entries and supplements intended for students were kept in separate series.

“Sometimes instructors print loose sheets so they don't have to carry entire books,” she explained. “If an abridged edition leaves something out, they might have the sheet in the classroom.”

“Can you check the index without opening students' personal records?”

“Yes. What you're looking for is an editorial table.”

They located a list of published annexes. The newer guide had been distributed with a notice moving two checks to a practice workbook. One corresponded to the sixth entry; the other wasn't the rest-period test Kael had seen.

“And that seventh step?” he asked.

Rena checked the dispatch register.

The guide's code indicated a complete revision rather than a simple abridgment. The workshop catalog listed no supplement restoring the missing check. Unfiled materials might exist, but the published collection there did not identify them.

Kael held the record beneath the lamp. A small ink stain obscured part of the original title. Rena switched on another light, and they verified the number without rubbing the page.

“An ink stain isn't censorship,” she said.

“And an incomplete sentence isn't a safe instruction.”

Rena gave the faintest smile.

Kael requested certified reproductions of the two index pages and the editorial notice, excluding handwritten notes containing students' names. He didn't need an installation blueprint to document that a check published in one version of a procedure had disappeared from a public list.

The assistant warned that correcting a guide would require intervention from the technical authority.

“That's their responsibility,” Kael replied. “Ours is not to pretend the two versions are identical.”

***

Tessa reviewed his report at the end of the interview.

“You could've marked the newer edition as incomplete and moved on to the next book,” she said.

“That would've been faster.”

“And saved you half your time.”

Kael looked at her. He had learned to distrust any question that seemed to conceal a question about identity, but Tessa wasn't asking for personal information. She expected a professional explanation.

“A reader might treat our catalog entry as confirmation that the manual is complete. If we know a check is missing, the entry should warn them and refer the question to the authority that can resolve it.”

“Would you deny access to the collection?”

“No. It's published. I'd add a note about the editions without presenting the guide as authorization to perform maneuvers. And I'd recommend that instructors consult Safety before using it as an operational checklist.”

Tessa marked something on her form.

“How would you tell an editorial omission from a valid technical change?”

“By finding the decision authorizing the revision. If it exists and applies to the same equipment, there'll be a reason. If nobody can locate it, I wouldn't invent one.”

For the first time in weeks, Kael said the word *authorized* without feeling that someone was talking about his body.

Tessa didn't know what the safeguards authority had established about his origins. She didn't need to know it to evaluate his ability to organize documents.

Leaving the workshop, Kael noticed two apprentices folding conservation boxes. One had placed adhesive over an original inscription; the other stopped him before he could press it down. Kael offered to show them how to use a separating strip.

This was the work he wanted: correcting a mistake without erasing the mark that made it possible to understand.

***

Kael's note reached the civilian technical standards office through an ordinary channel.

It described the difference between the public editions, the catalog sources, and the absence of a published supplement in the collection examined. It requested an editorial integrity review, not operating instructions or access to blueprints. A warning was attached: Kael had not established that any station had used the guide instead of its complete manual.

Dalia read a copy Kael brought her later.

“Are you doing this as part of your job?” she asked.

“As an Archive employee. None of it requires my personal report.”

“Then the answer should go back to the Archive, not into your protected file.”

Kael nodded.

That evening, at home, the woman who had raised him prepared tea while he tried to repair a hinge. The part resisted him. He had to set down the screwdriver and look for a smaller washer.

“Didn't you know how to fix it?” she asked with amusement.

“I knew how it was supposed to work. Not what this door was missing.”

She handed him the washer.

Kael finished the repair. The hinge closed smoothly, and the frame stopped creaking.

The next morning, an acknowledgment arrived from Standards. The office recognized the documentary discrepancy and was opening a review of the editions, without yet ruling on its technical effects.

It wasn't a victory over a conspiracy or an answer about the Network's origins.

It was confirmation that someone would examine an omitted check before allowing a guide to go on being used as though nothing were missing.

Kael filed the acknowledgment in the public collection.

His private history remained where he had chosen to keep it.

***End of Episode 37.***
