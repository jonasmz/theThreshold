# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 36: The Recorder That Was About to Disappear

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Daren Holt, limited third person.

***

The notice said the drum would be destroyed because it was defective.

Daren Holt read it twice. It had come from the medical training department that was still searching for consent records from a stabilization trial. It listed equipment scheduled for disposal due to wear, including a mechanical recorder of load pulses. Its inventory number partially matched a reference in the clinical report of the dead person whose body had reached the scaffold.

The notice did not claim that the drum had recorded that particular trial. It merely allowed forty-eight hours for preservation objections.

Mara Ellin examined the copy.

“A recorder could have served ten facilities. A matching prefix doesn't prove it was used in the fatal procedure.”

“And the absence of proof doesn't mean we should let it disappear without asking,” Daren replied.

The court clerk searched the cooperation file. The equipment belonged to an institution in another jurisdiction; the court overseeing the investigation of the body couldn't seize it with a telephone call. Daren drafted an urgent request asking the judge to seek provisional preservation through the competent territorial custodian, with an opportunity for the prosecution, defense, and representatives of protected interests to participate.

The defense responded before nightfall.

“We agree to preserve it,” said its attorney, “provided the examination is adversarial. We won't allow a preliminary reading to be presented as an authentic record of an event that hasn't even been identified.”

Daren had the objection entered into the record. The judge authorized the urgent request to proceed.

In the street, a cart dragged rusted components toward the municipal scrap yard. Nobody stopped every repair to ask whether the waste was evidence of a crime.

But this drum bore a number worth checking before it became scrap metal.

***

The preservation order reached the depot the following day.

Daren attended as an authorized liaison, not as the owner of the originals. The document examiner, prosecutor, and defense counsel entered with him. A territorial inspector verified that the institution had received the order and suspended destruction of the drum without halting disposal of the other inventoried equipment.

The drum rested on a shelf, disconnected for years. Beside it sat a box of paper recording strips. Some had buckled from damp.

“Don't unwind them without support,” the examiner warned. “They could tear along the edge, and we'd lose the positions of the marks.”

The custodian produced a lending register. According to the date at the top of the entry, the machine had been assigned to a technical classroom and returned before the medical intervention under investigation. A later notation said “extraordinary dispatch,” but the line lacked the usual signature.

Daren felt the urge to treat the missing signature as an answer.

“What can we conclude from that?” he asked.

“That a piece of information is missing from the chain,” the examiner said. “Not that someone concealed it. There may be another delivery sheet.”

They separated three sets of materials: the drum, the strips listed under its number, and the lending register. They photographed their positions, documented signs of use, and sealed each set without confusing its provenance. Copies of the photographs stayed with the custodian; the court received only a certified inventory.

The examiner requested permission to inspect the cylinder's exterior and its printed scale. She wanted to determine whether the types of measurements matched those mentioned in the clinical annex.

The inspector allowed it within the scope of the order.

The device was not connected to any power source.

***

In a conservation room, Mara compared the recorder's model with the description of the trial.

The drum measured load intervals, voltage drops, and the triggering of a safety interlock. If the corresponding recording strip could be located, it might help reconstruct when a maneuver had been interrupted. It couldn't identify the person connected to the apparatus or establish whether that person had consented.

“The general forms won't answer that, either,” Daren reminded her.

“The training department says it's locating the specific-consent index,” Mara replied. “We can't replace that with a curve.”

A depot assistant offered to look for the missing delivery sheet. She had worked on the archive transfer and knew that receipts were sometimes kept with toolboxes. Daren didn't ask her to open the entire building.

The judge authorized a limited search of movement indexes for that recorder and the relevant dates. The defense proposed documenting the search through two independent records to prevent selective reconstruction after the fact. The inspector agreed.

They found a dispatch acknowledgment bearing a box number and a receiving stamp from a testing department. The signature was legible, but the recipient's role wasn't. The acknowledgment didn't identify which recording strip had come back.

“We have a documented transfer,” the examiner said. “We still need to connect it to a particular session.”

Mara pointed to a worn section along the drum's edge. It might be the result of an old impact or recent transport. The inventory's damage log did not mention it.

Daren imagined how easy it would have been to write a complete sequence: the instrument in a room, a trial, a death, and criminal intent behind every stamp.

The evidence available was smaller than that. It would also stand up better to a defense entitled to challenge it.

***

The judge received the record at the end of the afternoon.

She ordered the drum and strips preserved for a reviewable period under territorial custody, with access restricted to experts and authorized parties. The medical department would retain its original clinical records. Nobody would receive unrelated patients' histories or be called guilty for appearing in a lending register.

The order carried a cost: dry storage, security, a reading support that wouldn't tear the paper, and hours from a calibration technician. The budget could not cover everything immediately.

“What do we examine first?” the clerk asked.

“The correspondence of the numbers and the integrity of the recording medium,” Daren answered. “If we can't establish those, even a readable curve will be nothing but a curve.”

The prosecutor asked that the historical calibration procedures also be preserved. The defense joined the request; without them, an apparent difference in load might simply be a scaling error.

Mara added a reservation about consent: even if the strip belonged to the fatal trial, it would not establish what information had been given to the person subjected to it.

The judge ordered notice to the equipment custodian and to the agency holding the authorization index. Neither office lost its jurisdiction merely by receiving the court's letter.

Outside, Daren found the worker assigned to scrap removal waiting beside his cart, which had been standing idle for hours.

“Are they taking the whole machine?” he asked.

“Only this unit will be preserved,” Daren said. “The rest of your work order can continue.”

The man sighed and went to find help loading the released parts.

Daren watched him leave. Justice delayed people who had done nothing wrong, too. That was a cost he had to explain, not deny.

He entered one last line in the case file: “Recording medium preserved; connection to the session not yet established.”

He had not uncovered the truth about the trial.

He had prevented a possible piece of it from being destroyed before anyone could question it.

***End of Episode 36.***
