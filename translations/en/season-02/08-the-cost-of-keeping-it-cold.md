# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 08: The Cost of Keeping It Cold

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel, limited third person.

***

The woman who had complained about the water supply returned to the newsroom with an invoice.

She did not ask to have her picture taken. She set it on Inara Vel's desk and waited for the journalist to read the heading.

“My mother needed a refrigerated medication delivered,” she explained. “The shipment was delayed because the transport routes changed. We paid for another night of storage. I want to know who's responsible for that cost.”

The invoice came from an authorized medical storage facility. It listed days of storage, the rate, and a delivery code. It said nothing about the recipient's treatment or illness.

“May I ask the provider about the service without publishing your name?” Inara asked.

“You can ask about the fee. Not about my mother.”

“That's what I'll do.”

Joren Mal appeared in the doorway.

“Another supply problem?”

“The same problem reaches different doorsteps,” Inara replied.

The reader left with a copy of her invoice. Inara retained only the information the woman had consented to share. Then she requested the storage facility's public rate schedule and the municipal regulations for reimbursing delays in essential deliveries.

The rates matched. There was no obvious fraud in the woman's bill.

But the regulations showed her an expense category she had not noticed during the spare-parts investigation: third-party medical transport and storage, partly covered by contingency funds. Some of those payments passed through the same general compensation system now absorbing the costs of closed strategic routes.

The expense category was not secret.

That was precisely why Inara could demand an answer without implying a crime.

***

At the oversight office, a clerk handed her the public statement of medical expenditures.

Patients' names were not listed. Nor should they have been. The table contained amounts, service periods, commercial operators, and destination categories: dispensaries, pharmacies, storage facilities, and organizations that contracted their own preservation services.

Inara followed the column of additional charges.

Some invoices had risen after the Needles branch was suspended, as expected when vehicles had to take longer roads. Others charged for *extended cold storage*: a fee for keeping containers at a controlled temperature while no one authorized their release.

One detail made her stop.

The same dispatch company appeared in a mechanical-parts transport statement and on the list of medical shipments: Pelder Warehouses.

There was nothing impossible about that. A carrier could prepare packages for different kinds of clients. A warehouse stamp did not prove what was inside a box.

“Is Pelder charging the municipality for refrigeration?” Inara asked.

The clerk shook her head.

“On these invoices, Pelder is the transport intermediary. A medical storage facility charges for preservation. Those are different services. If you combine the lines, you'll count the expense twice.”

Inara pointed to another entry.

“Here are two extended cold-storage charges for a service unit that doesn't appear on the public delivery list.”

“The public list excludes protected recipients. It might also be storage commissioned by an outside organization, without direct delivery to the municipality.”

“Is there a record explaining the charge?”

“There is a compensation file. You can request the general purpose of the service and the authority that approved it. Not the beneficiary's clinical identity.”

Inara wrote down the exact procedure.

An invoice without a name did not mean a person had disappeared. It was an invoice whose financial justification could be investigated without exposing anyone's private life.

***

The medical storage facility had a yard where vehicles waited beneath awnings.

Inara arrived as workers unloaded small containers into an isolated corridor. The manager told her not to approach the storage area. Temperatures were recorded in a restricted-access room; opening the doors to satisfy a journalist would endanger materials that did not belong to her.

“I don't need to see the containers,” Inara said. “I need to know who's responsible for storage costs when the recipient can't collect them.”

The manager showed her a rate notice and the complaints procedure.

Containers arrived with service orders. The facility certified storage times, temperatures, and the person or office authorized to retrieve them. Extended custody consumed energy cells, refrigerants, and labor shifts. The facility could not preserve material forever unless someone paid for the work.

“And if nobody acknowledges being the recipient?” she asked.

“We don't hand it to the first person who turns up. We notify the contracting party and, if there's a medical risk, the competent authority.”

“Does the municipality pay in those cases?”

“Only when the order falls under an agreement that permits it. I can't tell you which client corresponds to a protected invoice.”

Inara asked for a public example of the procedure. The manager pointed to an anonymized training file. A shipment had been delayed by a bridge closure. The container had remained under observation for three days, and the facility billed the logistics operator, who later sought compensation.

The case explained why there could be two legitimate invoices for one shipment.

It also showed what documentation should support them: an order, a continuous log, and either a release receipt or confirmation of ongoing custody.

Inara asked whether the facility could issue redacted confirmations of that kind.

“To the authority with jurisdiction, yes. To you, only the public portion. And a refusal is recorded as well.”

He gave her a form.

Inara put it away without attempting to photograph the inner door.

***

The compensation office's answer arrived in writing.

The two extended cold-storage charges Inara had identified were authorized under an agreement for preserving medical materials. The office identified the general class of service and the paying organization but did not disclose the final recipient, whose identity was protected by contractual and medical confidentiality.

There was one more useful detail: the second charge did not concern a failed municipal delivery. It concerned custody that remained active when the billing period ended.

“So the shipment isn't lost,” Joren said when Inara showed him the response.

“We can't say that. The document says someone paid to keep it in storage. It doesn't prove what's being stored or that the service was performed exactly as billed.”

“Can we publish the facility's name?”

“As a supplier under a public agreement, yes, provided we don't use that to suggest it's storing something illegal. It has a right to respond, and the financial audit isn't finished.”

Joren looked at the woman's invoice, from which Inara had removed unnecessary personal information.

“And her mother?”

“The rate was correct. The municipality accepts reimbursement applications for delays. I'll send her the form and office hours.”

The editor looked disappointed.

“Not much of an ending.”

“It's an answer for someone who came here asking.”

They prepared an article on preservation costs during the transport crisis. Inara distinguished what she could demonstrate from what the oversight office still needed to examine. She said nothing about patients, trials, or mysterious boxes.

As the newspaper went to press, she received acknowledgment of a new public request. It sought the procedure for auditing extended custody services paid for with contingency funds.

A warning appeared at the bottom of the provisional response: one of the storage periods had been authorized not by the municipality, but by a preservation department operating under its own agreement. Before closing the review, the oversight office would have to request the original supporting record from that department.

Inara folded the page.

The money had left a trail she could follow.

The container and its contents remained beyond it.

***End of Episode 08.***
