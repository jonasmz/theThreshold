# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 2 · Episode 03: The Untested Part

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Sera Nhal, limited third person.

***

The pump hadn't stopped. That was the problem.

Sera Nhal listened to the uneven knocking from the workshop doorway. Every time the valve shifted, a brief vibration traveled along the pipe and shook the workbench. The distribution station was still supplying water to the lower district, but its operating margin was dangerously narrow.

Her colleague had spent the night there.

“The part arrived,” she said, pointing to a box on the table. “They brought it by road after the Needles were closed.”

Sera checked the outer seal. The shipment belonged to the replacement order for a regulating plate she had rejected weeks earlier because it failed to meet tolerance. The order read *compliant replacement*. The box carried a transport receipt and a delivery invoice.

It carried no test certificate.

“Is it inside?” she asked.

“The part is. I didn't see any certification.”

Sera lifted the lid with her colleague watching. A new plate, wrapped in waxed paper, lay in the center. Its product-family number matched the request. Its individual serial number differed from the one on her previous rejection.

That was to be expected of a replacement.

What was not acceptable was for someone to write “compliant” without documenting what test had been performed, by whom, and for which configuration.

The shift supervisor appeared, still wearing his coat.

“I need that pump running until the next supply window,” he said.

“It is running. Badly, but it's running.”

“The new part might fix the vibration.”

“It might. If the tolerance is right.”

“Can't you measure it here?”

“I can measure some dimensions. Certification requires a load test and material traceability that this bench can't reproduce.”

The supervisor laid a sheet on the table.

It was a municipal priority instruction to keep the water flowing. It was not an order to install unapproved parts.

Sera placed a finger on the heading.

“The priority is getting water to the neighborhood. Not turning a box into proof just because it has a stamp.”

Another jolt came from the pipe.

They both looked toward the door.

The pump could fail before their argument was over.

***

Sera went down to the regulation room with two technicians and authorization for routine maintenance. The supply section was isolated for inspection; the remaining pumps continued carrying their distributed loads. There was no reserve of energy capable of making the malfunction simply disappear.

The old valve showed wear, but no open fracture. Sera examined the accessible supports, compared the vibration with the previous week's readings, and requested a second instrument to rule out a calibration fault in the first.

The readings agreed within their margin of error.

“If we reduce the flow through this branch and divert part of it to the reserve tank, we can maintain minimum service,” she said.

The younger technician studied the gauge.

“For how long?”

“Until the window in the calculation, provided demand doesn't rise. Then we'll have to reassess. And the tank won't refill just because we want it to.”

The maneuver required approval from the municipal operations manager. Sera requested it by cable, specifying how much water would stop circulating and which area would receive priority. Permission arrived with one condition: notify affected users and stop the reduction if the safety indicators moved outside their permitted range.

The shift supervisor came downstairs again.

“One neighborhood will have lower pressure,” he said.

“Yes. Our immediate alternatives are installing an untested part or forcing this valve to bear a load we no longer have the margin for.”

“What if people protest?”

“They'll be right to demand a replacement that comes with documentation and tests. Not to ask us to hide the risk.”

Sera waited for the execution order. At the operator's signal, they adjusted the load in stages, checking temperature and vibration after every change. The knocking slowed. It did not disappear.

A minute later, pressure in the reserve tank fell less than predicted.

“That doesn't mean we should use the extra margin to turn everything back up,” Sera warned when the supervisor breathed a sigh of relief. “First we need to see whether it holds.”

They stayed and watched.

Eventually, the curve remained steady long enough to hand over the monitoring shift.

Sera signed the reading and recorded the operational cost: reduced flow, extra staff, and reserve capacity committed. She also wrote down the observation period. No one could use that sheet to claim the pump had been repaired.

***

The receiving office opened after noon.

Sera brought the unused replacement box, accompanied by her colleague as witness to its opening and resealing. She would not allow the part to be left loose on a purchasing desk. She requested that its serial number, packaging condition, and missing document be entered into the record.

The warehouse manager read the order.

“It's listed as a warranty replacement. If I reject delivery, the company will say we received it late and we'll lose the next transport slot.”

“I'm not denying that the part exists,” Sera replied. “I'm refusing to approve its installation as a certified component.”

“That's a difficult distinction for Purchasing.”

“Not for a pump.”

The manager called the oversight office. It replied that the part could be accepted *under technical quarantine*, without approval for use, while the supplier was asked to provide the test report and material traceability. The arrangement required the serial number to be frozen in the inventory so a new workshop order could not withdraw it as an available spare.

That was the only receipt Sera signed.

The box was sealed. Her colleague received one copy of the record, and the manager another. The earlier rejection remained in her file with its original date; nobody replaced it with the new entry.

Outside, she found a line of residents at the Services counter. A woman wanted to know why the water pressure on her street had dropped.

Sera explained that the equipment's load had been reduced to prevent a failure, that basic supply continued, and that the priority schedule and service hours were available there. She did not describe strategic operations she knew nothing about. Nor did she promise a repair time that nobody had authorized.

The woman listened without thanking her.

“My mother needs to fill a tank before nightfall.”

Sera helped her find the relevant supply window and filed a request for assistance. It was less than a solution. It was something they could try to verify.

When she returned to the warehouse, the manager had found a sheet tucked into a fold of the packaging.

It was not a certificate.

It was a dispatch note identifying the company that had prepared the replacement for the main contractor. Its stamp did not match that of the company that had invoiced the delivery.

“So they cheated us?” the manager asked.

Sera shook her head.

“It means we know whom to ask for the test. And we can ask why Purchasing only showed us the intermediary's invoice.”

She requested that the dispatch note be added to the quarantine record. The part would not leave storage because of an argument in the hallway.

Behind the door, the pump knocked again.

As long as that sound continued, someone would be paying for every day the certification was missing.

***End of Episode 03.***
