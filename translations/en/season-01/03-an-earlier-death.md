# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 03: An Earlier Death

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Perspective:** Daren Holt and Kael Veyran, in scenes separated by explicit breaks.

***

Daren Holt hated the court's basements for one simple reason: down there, nobody seemed to believe the air needed replacing.

The medical repository occupied an old stone wing beside the river. In summer it smelled of dampness and preservatives. In winter, of dampness and colder preservatives. Officials spoke in low voices, as though respect for the dead could be measured by the volume of a conversation.

That morning, the forensic physician did not look up when Daren entered.

“You're late.”

“You didn't give me a time.”

“Then you're late for an appointment that didn't exist. An impressive administrative achievement.”

Dr. Mara Ellin wore a dark apron and held an observation board. A covered body rested on the main table. Beyond it stood three assistants and an examination lamp whose hum seemed far too loud for the room.

“Do you have the report?” Daren asked.

“I have observations. The report is waiting for someone to stop ordering me to sign premature conclusions.”

Daren glanced toward the assistants.

Mara understood.

“They can stay. They're not part of the problem.”

One of the young assistants raised an eyebrow, but none of them spoke.

Daren approached the table. He had seen bodies before. Far too many, unfortunately. What troubled him was not death itself, but how a body could become a political argument without ever having the chance to contradict anyone.

“Is it Taren Oss?” he asked.

“That's what I'd like someone to prove.”

The doctor uncovered the face.

The man on the table had dark hair streaked with gray and a beard thicker than the one Daren remembered from the condemned man's portraits. His features were neither monstrous nor supernatural. They belonged to a middle-aged man who had suffered a difficult death.

“His estimated age is higher than the age recorded for Oss,” Mara said. “That alone doesn't tell us much. Illness and living conditions can mislead us.”

“And the marks?”

“Some features don't change just because someone ages ten years. The scar recorded on the condemned man isn't here. We found another, an old one, near the collarbone. His dental structure doesn't match the prison record either.”

Daren wrote that down.

“Then it's someone else.”

“The evidence supports that conclusion. I don't need to imagine an arcane alteration to explain it. If someone claims magic changed his teeth and erased a scar, they can provide a mechanism and earlier samples. I won't certify a hypothesis nobody can test.”

Daren nodded.

He liked the fact that Mara was unpleasantly precise.

“Cause of death?”

The doctor partly covered the body again.

“It doesn't match what we'd expect from the procedure described.”

“Explain it without words I'll have to look up in a manual.”

“There are injuries compatible with mechanical intervention, but they don't explain the internal damage on their own. And the tissues show changes suggesting death occurred before the ceremony.”

“How much earlier?”

“I'm not giving you an exact figure when the body was preserved and transported in ways we still don't understand. We're talking about a significant interval, not the minutes between the chamber doors closing and opening.”

Daren looked at her observation board.

“Could the body have been preserved for days?”

“Yes. But I need to know what methods were used. Storage changes the indicators we rely on to estimate time of death.”

“So, in short, the man was already dead when the city heard the execution.”

Mara gave him a stern look.

“In short, that is a hypothesis consistent with the findings. In court, we'll also need preservation records, samples, and an independent investigation.”

Daren sighed.

“Every time I say something simple, you people make it complicated.”

“Because simple things have a habit of getting innocent people killed when they're used as evidence.”

Silence followed.

Mara indicated a sheet beside the lamp.

“And there's something else.”

The document carried the court's letterhead, but the space reserved for the certifying signature was covered by a provisional stamp.

“What am I looking at?”

“An order to prepare the body, issued before the ceremony.”

Daren moved the sheet closer to the light.

“That's normal. They have to prepare the apparatus.”

“Read the description.”

Beneath the case number was a printed line: **receipt of remains for subsequent certification**.

Daren read it again.

“Whose remains?”

“That's what I want you to tell me.”

***

By midmorning, Daren had submitted a preliminary report to the magistracy.

The judicial secretary listened without interrupting. His face was so narrow that his glasses looked as though they were holding it together rather than resting upon it.

“Are you claiming the condemned man didn't die?” he finally asked.

“I'm saying the body presented isn't the condemned man's, and there are indications of an earlier death.”

“The execution certificate has been signed.”

“The certificate may describe a procedure that did not occur as declared.”

“Inspector, consider the implications very carefully.”

“I've been doing that since yesterday.”

The secretary set his glasses on the desk.

“The Council maintains that an anomaly of an arcane nature may have occurred.”

“Dr. Ellin found nothing that supports that conclusion.”

“She isn't a channeler.”

“Neither am I. But neither of us needs to be one to see that an identification record doesn't match a body.”

The secretary picked up his glasses.

“The Delegation has requested that certain findings remain restricted.”

“Under what authority?”

“Arcane security.”

“The same phrase they use whenever they don't want to cite an actual regulation.”

“Don't turn this into an institutional confrontation.”

Daren set both hands on the desk.

“We have a person whose death was publicly announced and a corpse we cannot identify. If someone manipulated the procedure, we could be looking at forgery, unlawful confinement, concealment of human remains, and obstruction of justice. All of that falls within our jurisdiction until a valid ruling says otherwise.”

The secretary glanced at the closed door.

“Getting an order to inspect the chamber will be difficult.”

“Why?”

“Because they're already dismantling it.”

It took Daren a second to understand.

“Who authorized that?”

“Municipal Maintenance. They say the contract expires today.”

Daren stood so quickly his chair struck the wall.

“I need an immediate suspension order.”

“I'll process it.”

“Not when you get around to it. Now.”

He left without waiting for an answer.

In the corridor, his footsteps seemed to multiply between the stone walls. Daren realized that for the last twenty-four hours he had been trying to solve an identity problem while someone else was busy taking apart the physical scene of the incident.

***

Kael Veyran had barely slept.

The hearing notice he had received the previous evening had allowed him to establish two things: the Archive maintained an authorization process absent from recent manuals, and that process could summon an employee without identifying who was responsible for it.

Neither observation was enough to support a formal complaint.

On his desk he had arranged copies of the case file in three columns: court documents, Archive documents, and Delegation documents. To the right lay a blank sheet headed **verified facts**.

He had not written much on it yet.

The first line read: *An identifier is shared by systems that are officially unrelated.*

The second: *A query using that identifier triggers a notification with no identified recipient.*

The third was blank.

An internal message announced a meeting at eleven in the Administrative Continuity Room.

Kael checked the sender.

No name appeared.

He decided to attend with certified copies of his requests, leaving the original case documents behind. If someone intended to challenge his access, he wanted a precise record of what he had consulted and why.

At ten forty, Daren called.

“I need to know if your Archive can certify a receipt time.”

“That depends on the medium and the issuing institution.”

“An order to prepare human remains, dated before the execution.”

Kael put down his pen.

“Whose remains?”

“That's what we're investigating. Can you check whether the form was issued at the time it claims?”

“If an originating record exists, yes. But I can't alter the chain of custody. Send a judicial request with the complete identifiers.”

“I will. Could you take a look at a copy in the meantime?”

“I can check whether its format is consistent with the period and the institution. I won't issue an official conclusion without the original.”

“I can live with that.”

Daren paused.

“There's something else. They're dismantling the execution chamber.”

Kael looked at the clock.

“With judicial authorization?”

“I'm trying to stop them.”

“If the dismantling contract was issued before the ceremony, there should be a work order.”

“And if it was issued afterward?”

“There should still be one.”

“For once, I like your answers.”

The call ended.

Kael looked at the empty third line on his sheet.

He wrote: *Documentation for the preparation of remains may predate the event whose consequences it purports to record.*

Underneath, he added a single word: *Verify.*

***

The Administrative Continuity Room was on a floor Kael had never visited.

Not because it was forbidden, but because it belonged to a wing used for meetings between senior Archive officials, and his work rarely required him to go up there.

The corridor was narrow, with high windows and old lamps casting a yellowish light. The floor was clean, but the doorframes showed signs of repeated repairs.

The meeting room was empty.

On the table stood a glass of water, a folder, and a clock.

Kael checked the time.

Ten fifty-eight.

He sat facing the door and left his notebook closed.

At eleven five, a woman of indeterminate age entered, her hair pulled back, a gray suit on her shoulders. She wore an identification badge with no visible emblem.

“Mr. Veyran.”

“Good morning.”

She did not offer her name.

She sat opposite him, opened a folder, and consulted a sheet.

“You attempted to access a withdrawn collection.”

“I submitted a formal request through my supervisor.”

“Before that, you made a direct query.”

“Yes. As part of a document verification assigned by my department.”

The woman looked up.

“What were you expecting to find?”

“The origin of an identifier appearing in a court file.”

“Do you know what administrative continuity means?”

“It's a term for records, procedures, and jurisdictions that survive institutional reorganizations.”

“A correct definition.”

Kael waited.

“Some collections,” she continued, “contain references to agencies that no longer exist. Reading them out of context can lead to serious errors.”

“That's why I want the index of origins.”

“Not every index is available to ordinary analysts.”

“Then I need to know the authorization procedure.”

The woman slid a sheet across the table.

“This document certifies that your query did not result in access to restricted information.”

Kael read it.

It was a declaration closing the incident. By signing it, he would agree that his request had been invalid and that there were no grounds to pursue it.

“I can't sign this,” he said.

The woman's expression did not change.

“Why not?”

“Because it states that my query had no documentary basis. It did. The identifier is printed in a court file legally received by the Archive.”

“It could be an error.”

“That's exactly what I'm trying to determine.”

The woman closed her folder.

“Mr. Veyran, a career in public service can be damaged by unnecessary persistence.”

Kael looked at her.

It was the first sentence of the meeting that could not be mistaken for a procedural statement.

“Is that a professional warning?”

“It's a recommendation.”

“Then I'd appreciate having it in writing.”

For several seconds, the woman remained perfectly still.

Then she collected the sheet.

“Your request will remain under review.”

“Before which authority?”

“You'll receive a response.”

“When?”

“When appropriate.”

She rose and left.

Kael was alone with the glass of water and the clock.

He had learned nothing about the collection.

But he now knew someone had arranged a meeting specifically to persuade him to stop looking for it.

That did not prove a conspiracy.

At the very least, it proved deliberate resistance to answering a legitimate question.

***

Daren reached the square too late to prevent the removal of part of the outer structure, but in time to halt the dismantling of the chamber.

The court order had come through barely twenty minutes after his phone call. Holding it in one hand, the inspector found four workers unloading tools and a supervisor claiming he had received no instructions.

“You have now,” Daren said, handing him the copy.

The man read it with difficulty.

“If we leave this standing, they'll charge us for another day's rental.”

“Submit the claim to the court.”

“For how long?”

“Until the inspection is complete.”

The supervisor looked toward the closed chamber.

“We've already started taking panels off the north side.”

Daren felt an unpleasant chill.

“Nobody touches anything else.”

He called two municipal officers and ordered them to record the names of every worker present, the tools used, and the pieces already removed. He accused no one. They might simply have been following a routine order.

But the structure was no longer intact.

Inside the chamber, he saw a metal mechanism, a restraint seat, and a floor made of narrow plates. Nothing visible suggested that anyone had escaped through it.

One plate bore marks from recently turned screws.

Daren knelt.

He had learned that a mechanism did not have to be secret to go unnoticed. It only had to sit among other components that appeared to serve ordinary purposes.

He called the supervisor over.

“Is this plate removed for maintenance?”

“It shouldn't be.”

“Who knows this apparatus?”

“Our equipment manager.”

“I need to speak with him.”

“He isn't here today.”

“Why not?”

The man shrugged.

“Said he was sick.”

Daren wrote down the name.

Then he ordered that the plate remain untouched until the mechanical examiner arrived.

On his way out, he found Mara Ellin beside a transport stretcher.

“I have the second report,” she said.

“Please tell me it won't make my day worse.”

“It will.”

She handed him a sheet.

The doctor had examined internal marks on the body and compared them with the declared conditions of the procedure.

Her conclusion was cautious but clear within its limits: there were indications of an earlier death, and the mechanism described did not adequately explain the injuries.

“Can you sign it?” Daren asked.

“Already have.”

“Then perhaps we still have one solid thing in this case.”

Mara indicated a second page.

“There's a problem with the receiving paperwork.”

Daren read it.

The hospital had received a request for funeral preparation before the execution began.

The form included a batch number, a place of origin, and medical authorization.

The authorization bore a perfectly legible signature.

Daren recognized the name. It belonged to the physician who had certified an extraordinary inspection at a research facility months earlier.

But the signature was dated in the early hours before the ceremony.

“Could it be a mistake?” he asked.

“It could. That's why we need the original.”

Daren felt the list of documents requiring investigation growing faster than his ability to examine them.

The doctor continued.

“There's something that wasn't in the preliminary report. The ink on the receipt stamp is consistent with a form that had been prepared for several hours by the time the body was presented.”

Daren looked toward the chamber.

The possibility was so simple that it was disturbing.

Someone had prepared the paperwork for a corpse before the city witnessed the execution of a living man.

***

At the end of the day, Kael returned to his office and found an urgent judicial request.

Daren needed the Archive to verify the authenticity of a medical authorization dated before the execution.

The sheet carried a name, a time, and a batch number.

Kael compared its format with the court's standard forms.

Then he checked the issuing institution's code.

The catalog recognized the organization but not its department.

He checked the numbering again.

A short sequence appeared beside the signature, as though it identified an internal unit.

It did not match any known medical division.

Kael brought the paper closer to the lamp.

The sequence began with the same characters he had seen on the corpse's identification sheet.

He went still.

He thought of the woman in the continuity room, of the document she had tried to make him sign, of the order to prepare remains, and of the body that did not belong to the condemned man.

None of those pieces was enough to explain the execution.

But there was now a concrete connection between the corpse's identity and the documentation issued before his official death.

Kael took a fresh sheet of paper.

He drew two columns: **what we know** and **what someone wants us to accept**.

Under the first heading, he wrote three verifiable facts.

Under the second, a single sentence:

*That the body died when the bell rang.*

He paused.

Then he added another line beneath the first heading.

*Medical documentation of receipt exists from before the execution.*

He opened the judicial form to prepare his response.

Before writing anything, he called the number Daren had left him.

“Inspector, it's Veyran.”

“Tell me you found something.”

Kael looked at the signature.

“I don't think the question is who died yesterday anymore.”

“Then what is it?”

“The question is who was already dead before the ceremony began.”

***End of Episode 03.***
