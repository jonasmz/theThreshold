# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 16: Witnesses to the Mechanism

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Daren Holt, third-person limited.

***

The first statement did not begin with what the witness had seen.

It began with what she was afraid of losing.

“They told me that if I say the shift wasn't carried out the way the report says, they'll review all the overtime in my section,” Nadia Varel said.

Daren Holt set down his pen.

The woman had her municipal guard uniform folded across her knees. She was not under arrest. She had come in response to a witness summons concerning the ceremony in the square, accompanied by a representative of her union. In the courthouse corridor, other officials' footsteps were muffled by an old carpet.

“Who told you that?” Daren asked.

“My superior said statements can have administrative consequences. He didn't use the word *threat*.”

“I'm not going to interpret it for you.”

Nadia looked at him for the first time since entering.

“But you think it was a threat.”

“I think I need to know whether anyone put conditions on your testimony. Then we'll record it in your own words.”

The union representative gave the slightest nod.

Daren asked the clerk to record the warning in a separate statement, without attributing intentions to the superior until he had been heard. The woman looked too tired to thank him.

“Let's begin with what you did that morning,” he said.

Nadia had guarded the front door of the execution chamber. Her duties were to check the identities of officers delivering the condemned man, prevent unauthorized entry, and remain at the entrance throughout the procedure.

“Did you see Taren Oss enter?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“How close were you?”

“Less than two steps away. His sleeves were fastened. They'd removed his chains before he reached the platform.”

That matched the Archive's observations and the attendance record.

“Did you identify him yourself?”

“I checked that the delivery sheet and prison credentials matched the order. The transfer guards had established his personal identity.”

Daren recorded the distinction.

“Did you see him again after the doors closed?”

“No.”

“Did anyone else enter?”

Nadia raised her head.

“The people responsible for the apparatus entered. They were authorized.”

“How many?”

“Two through my door. According to the preparation report, another person had already gone in through the technical section.”

“Did you personally see that third person?”

“No. That part of the shift is recorded at another entrance.”

The clerk checked a box.

Daren remembered the chamber. From the gallery where Kael had watched the event, people could see the doors, the exterior light, and the stretcher that appeared afterward. Neither the crowd nor the guards positioned in front of it had seen the interior during the decisive interval.

“What did the sound you heard around nine twelve mean to you?” he asked.

“That the apparatus had completed its cycle.”

“Did you see the condemned man die?”

“No. My post doesn't allow me to.”

It was an answer without drama. Nadia had spent years repeating security procedures in municipal buildings and had probably never imagined she would one day have to distinguish a sound from a death.

Daren closed the entry register.

“Then when you signed the guard report, you certified that the condemned man entered alive and the door remained closed.”

“That's what my report certifies,” she replied. “Not his death.”

The woman straightened her back.

“Inspector, if someone wrote that I saw what happened inside, that person didn't read the description of my post.”

***

The second witness was named Ilda Rehn and worked for the company that rented out the apparatus. Her job was to monitor the exterior lighting panel, warn of electrical faults, and notify the magistrate when the cycle had finished.

She arrived carrying a lunchbox wrapped in a cloth.

Daren offered to wait until she had eaten. She refused.

“If I get back to the factory late, they'll deduct the entire afternoon.”

“We can certify that you appeared here.”

“Paper doesn't pay last week's debt.”

Daren asked the clerk to arrange the witness compensation available for missed work. He did not promise that the application would be processed that day.

Ilda opened her lunch, put away half a loaf of bread, and sat down.

“What could you see from your console?” Daren asked.

“The signal-system power supply, two closure indicators, and the notification control. I couldn't see the platform inside.”

“Could the equipment operate without completing the execution?”

Ilda stopped moving her fingers.

“The panel doesn't measure anyone's death.”

“Explain that.”

“It shows that a sequence received the command to begin, that the reported closures responded, and that the notification circuit reached its final position. To know whether the actuating mechanism completed its entire movement, someone has to inspect the equipment from inside.”

“Did you inspect it?”

“I wasn't authorized to.”

“What did you hear that morning?”

“The relay clicking, an impact, and then the completion signal. Same as everyone else.”

“Was there any abnormal behavior?”

“A delay before the interior-closure notice. I thought the operator had asked someone to check a restraint.”

“Did you report it?”

“I wrote it down. I couldn't leave my post.”

The clerk showed her a certified reproduction of the event log. The witness pointed to a line written in the margin.

*Interior confirmation delayed; external circuit available.*

“How long was the delay?” he asked.

“I don't have a separate measurement. The panel clock records exterior notifications, not what they did inside. Anyone who tells you it was an exact number of seconds is guessing.”

Daren felt the familiar relief of finding someone who preferred a limited answer to an elegant explanation.

“Who could order the sequence to continue?”

“The authorized operator of the apparatus.”

“Which of the names on this list belonged to that person?”

Ilda reviewed the document he slid across the table.

“Eron Bale was listed as the technical assembly supervisor. The execution operator was assigned under a different roster. I don't know whether they were the same person or which one was inside at the end.”

Daren let the clerk record her answer.

They had established their first precise separation: by design, the external execution signal was not a certificate that someone had died inside.

The court had treated the two facts as though they were one.

***

The next day they returned to the square.

The chamber remained under judicial custody. Its exterior structure was incomplete because the contractor had begun dismantling it before the preservation order arrived, but the interior section and the mechanism they had discovered were still protected by seals identified in the initial inspection records.

Odel Mar was waiting with two experts and a municipal architect.

“I'm not putting anyone underneath that platform until we know what's holding up the roof,” he said before greeting them.

Daren showed him the supplementary authorization. It allowed inspection of the space beneath the floor and removal of an already documented closure panel. It did not authorize entry into old galleries or removal of components without an inventory.

“Then let's do what we are allowed to do,” he said.

The architect checked the supports. Two temporary columns carried loads redistributed by reinforcements installed after the ceremony. The team fitted mechanical displacement indicators and a work barrier. Only when the structural examination was complete did Odel ask for the side panel to be lifted.

There was no monumental doorway behind it.

There was a dark recess, a loading tray, and a length of short rails whose surfaces bore friction scratches. The rails reached a service cavity between the raised platform and the stone of the square. Its side exit could not be seen from above.

“We already found this,” Daren reminded him.

“We found part of it,” Odel replied. “Now we know how the tray lines up.”

Using a rigid ruler, he measured the usable width and clearance along one section. He did not send an assistant through to demonstrate anything. He prepared a test frame to the recorded dimensions, slid it as far as the permitted section, and marked where it rubbed against the edge.

The test was not a reconstruction of what had happened. It was a check of geometric possibility.

“What could pass through here?” Daren asked.

“An elongated load. With the person lying down, there's room for someone approximately the condemned man's size. But that doesn't tell me whether someone was moved alive, dead, or whether they transported equipment instead.”

“Would it require arcane power?”

“Not to move the tray. An ordinary mechanical drive would be enough. We haven't found a Transit device or anything that forces us to imagine one.”

Daren looked into the recess.

A simple mechanism could be concealed behind a ceremony designed not to show what happened inside. The hard part was not inventing an impossible machine. It was ensuring that nobody was responsible for watching when it mattered.

Odel pointed out a broken guide.

“The piece that would transmit movement from the side is missing. We noted that in the first inspection.”

“Could someone have removed it afterward?”

“Yes. Or it may never have been installed. We can look for fastening marks and check inventories. I'm not filling that gap with a story.”

An assistant called from the barrier. Beneath the surface layer of dust, a small strip of cloth was caught on the rail's edge.

Daren approached but did not touch it.

The expert first photographed its position, took a measurement, and then removed the strip with clean tools. He placed it in an identified envelope; two people signed the seal.

“Cloth from the condemned man's clothing?” Daren asked.

“Cloth,” Odel corrected him. “Its origin depends on comparison. It could come from a uniform, packaging, or anyone who worked here.”

Daren accepted the correction.

The scene was beginning to provide answers, but none justified neglecting the questions.

***

Mara Ellin had requested a conversation before signing the update to the forensic report.

Daren found her at the medical store, where two assistants were cataloging sample containers.

“You're not going to ask me to work out how someone escaped through a hole,” she said.

“No.”

“Good. Last time you already wanted me to deduce a mechanism from a scar.”

The inspector sat down.

Mara laid the report on the table.

The corpse's dental records remained incompatible with those of the condemned man. A preserved tissue sample had been compared at the municipal laboratory under authorization, and the result reinforced one conclusion: the body presented after the ceremony was not Taren Oss.

The exact interval between death and presentation remained difficult to estimate because of the preservation procedures, but there were sufficient findings to establish that the body had undergone treatment before its public appearance.

“Could Taren have died inside the chamber and someone then displayed this other corpse?” Daren asked.

“Yes. The fact that the displayed body isn't Taren doesn't prove he's alive.”

“Can you say the displayed body died during the apparatus's cycle?”

“Not from what we've observed. The injuries and treatments don't support that chronology.”

“Then we have an announced death, but no identified body proving it.”

Mara studied him for a long moment.

“That sentence I can sign within my field of expertise. If you take it to court, make clear that it isn't a certification of survival.”

Daren nodded.

Before leaving, he showed her the remains-receipt form dated before the ceremony.

“Were you able to verify its origin?”

“The issuing office recognizes the format and the number. It hasn't identified the individual that preparation concerned. The samples from the body don't yet allow us to give that person a name.”

Daren imagined someone who had died far from the square, then been made into the outcome of somebody else's sentence.

“We can't treat that person as an unidentified object,” he said.

“That's what I've been trying to tell you since day one,” Mara replied, closing the folder.

***

The third statement was taken in a different room.

Tomás Brin had worked as an assembly assistant during the week before the execution. The main company hired him by the day and sent him to inspect wooden joints. He was not part of the team that certified the apparatus's operation.

He arrived accompanied by a legal-aid attorney specializing in labor matters.

“I didn't bring any company documents,” he said. “If I take a sheet, they'll accuse me of stealing it.”

Daren assured him that the summons did not require him to produce material held in someone else's custody.

“Tell us what you remember about the lower access.”

Tomás rubbed his hands together.

“The plate was smaller on the work plan they gave me. When I arrived to install it, they'd already changed the frame.”

“Who made the change?”

“I didn't see. There was a night-shift supervisor who signed for the work, but I came in the next day.”

“Did you know him?”

“By sight. I couldn't tell you his name without looking at the list.”

Daren showed him the official workers' roster. Tomás recognized two coworkers. When he reached Eron Bale, he frowned.

“That name was on the work orders,” he said. “I can't be certain the man giving me instructions was Bale.”

“What did he tell you?”

“Not to close the platform's side until they'd finished the load tests.”

“What tests?”

“I didn't ask. My job was carpentry.”

Daren let a few seconds pass.

“Did you see the tray move?”

“Once. From the side. They pushed a covered load through to check it wouldn't hit the frame. I don't know what was under the tarpaulin.”

Odel, attending as a technical adviser, requested permission to ask a question through the inspector.

“Could the tray reach the side recess when the panels were shut?”

Tomás considered it.

“If the little panel was left out of place, yes. But when I worked there, it was open. I never saw it again afterward.”

Daren wrote down exactly what he had said. It was evidence that the mechanism had been used during preparations, not during the ceremony itself.

“Why didn't you mention this when the investigation began?” he asked.

Tomás lowered his eyes.

“Nobody asked about trays. They asked who'd signed the contract and whether I knew the condemned man.”

The answer left Daren feeling something different from anger. They had not failed to hear a great confession. They had asked the wrong questions of someone who did not know what mattered.

“Are you afraid you'll lose work because of your testimony?” he asked.

“I already lost two shifts. I don't know whether that counts.”

The attorney explained that the company had been reviewing temporary contracts since dismantling was halted. Daren arranged for a record of the witness's court appearance to be sent to the labor department and for Tomás to receive information on reporting retaliation, without promising protection the court had not yet ordered.

Tomás agreed to read his statement. He pointed out two corrections in wording and signed only after they were made.

***

That afternoon, Daren visited the municipal guardroom.

He had no authorization to inspect every guard rotation in the city, and did not want one. He had requested only the chamber's duty ledger and shift instructions. The officer in charge received him with a mixture of courtesy and annoyance.

“The agents did what they were supposed to,” he said.

“That's exactly what I need to establish.”

The shift ledger contained two signatures for the main entrance, one for perimeter control, and a field for the technical section. The last carried a post number but no individual's name.

“Is that an error?” Daren asked.

“A security post can be staffed by company personnel when there's an authorized service contract.”

“Then there should be a roster identifying those people.”

The officer pointed to a folder.

“We requested it from the contractor. They still haven't answered in full.”

Daren reviewed the page for the morning of the execution. There was a marginal note written after the apparatus was closed.

*Perimeter stable. Support personnel out of sight.*

“What does ‘out of sight’ mean?” he asked.

“That staff on the technical side were not visible from the main post. It's a security designation, not an absence.”

“Who wrote the note?”

“The shift supervisor.”

“I want to interview him.”

The officer did not object.

The supervisor's statement was less useful than Daren had hoped. The man had directed the perimeter and had not entered the chamber. He confirmed that the front guards remained at their positions until the stretcher appeared; he acknowledged that surveillance of the lower areas was the technical contractor's responsibility and that the municipal ledger could not identify every worker in that zone.

“So nobody from your corps guarded the side exit?” Daren asked.

“I didn't know there was an authorized side exit.”

Daren felt the weight of that sentence.

“Was there a prior inspection of all entrances?”

“We reviewed the security plan supplied with the installation.”

“That plan didn't include the access we found.”

The supervisor lowered his gaze.

“Then the plan we approved was incomplete.”

Daren saw neither relief nor guilt in his expression. He saw someone understanding, too late, the implications of an omission.

“That doesn't mean you authorized the modification,” he said.

“But my signature says the entrances were controlled.”

“It says the entrances listed in the plan were controlled.”

“To someone's family, the distinction might not matter.”

Daren let the man take a moment.

The idea of a crime committed through badly designed form fields was beginning to seem more terrible than a conspiracy of masked figures.

***

The final stage of the inspection required a new order.

Odel had discovered that the platform's side panel lined up with an old service opening beneath a stone staircase. The access was covered by municipal construction predating the installation of the chamber. Opening it without a stability assessment could affect a drain and a wall that was already showing damp damage.

Daren submitted a narrowly framed request to the magistrate for structural examination and preventive preservation. He did not ask for permission to explore galleries or enter facilities associated with the Delegation. The request described which parts of the square belonged to the municipality and which were in judicial custody.

The judge authorized measurement, photography, and protection of the opening. Any later excavation or opening would require a technical report and a new order.

The next morning, while workers installed a cover to keep the rain from flooding the recess, Daren studied the alignment.

The tray could reach the side cavity.

From there, a worker familiar with the space could have moved a load through a maintenance passage without appearing before the guards at the main entrance. It remained to be established what lay beyond.

The expert traced a line on a working copy of the plan. He stopped at the old staircase.

“This is as far as I can establish geometric continuity,” he said. “The rest remains closed.”

“Could a person have reached this point during the minutes of the ceremony?”

“The distance and width don't rule it out. The mechanism's resistance, the actual conditions, and who was operating it are separate questions. You can't derive an exact time without a controlled reconstruction.”

Daren looked at the place where, weeks earlier, the crowd had demanded to see a body.

The problem was no longer merely that the wrong person had been displayed.

There had been a phase of the procedure beyond the sight of those certifying entry and death, a phase connected to a physical route absent from the security plan.

***

When Daren returned to the courthouse, the judicial clerk had gathered the new proceedings into four folders.

The first contained Nadia's account: Taren entered alive, and she did not witness his death.

The second held the apparatus's external log and Ilda's testimony: the public signal did not verify the physical outcome.

The third contained Odel's report and Tomás's statement: a mechanical transport system existed beneath the platform, had been tested during preparations, and was geometrically compatible with moving a person lying down.

The fourth held Mara's forensic evidence: the body shown to the crowd did not belong to the condemned man and had received earlier treatment.

Daren opened them one by one.

He did not need all of them to describe the same event for them to form a legitimate question. Each corrected a certainty the execution record had presented as final.

The clerk came over with a request from Taren Oss's defense counsel.

“They're asking for the status of the body's identification,” she said. “If it isn't their client, they need to know whether they can continue acting on his behalf.”

Daren read the document.

The defense had maintained even before sentencing that there were irregularities in the proceedings. Their new request was not an admission that they knew of an escape. It was an attempt to prevent the state from treating as dead a man whose death it could not prove.

“We'll answer through the court, using only what has been established,” he said.

“What do we put in the report's conclusion?”

Daren took a clean sheet.

He first wrote what could no longer be maintained: there was no reliable physical proof that the displayed corpse was Taren Oss. Then he recorded the mechanical observations and the limitations of the testimony.

When he reached the word *escape*, he paused.

A person might have exited through the lower access and died afterward. They might also have been moved under duress. Nothing established where Taren was now.

But the apparatus, the silences in the records, and the substitution of a body made a hypothesis that had initially seemed impossible now reasonable.

Daren wrote:

*The evidence gathered supports investigating, as a substantiated hypothesis, whether Taren Oss left the execution section alive through an undeclared service route. His destination and current condition have not been established.*

He read the sentence twice.

It was not a verdict.

It was enough to request an investigation into his whereabouts.

***

The magistrate received the report without interrupting.

Other cases lay on his desk: a dispute over ownership of a workshop, a complaint against a market inspector, and a file concerning flood damage. Daren was grateful that the court remained an ordinary court, even when faced with a case threatening to consume everything.

“Are you asking for a public declaration that he's alive?” the judge asked.

“No. I'm asking that any definitive death certification based on this corpse be suspended and that we investigate the condemned man's possible whereabouts, with the appropriate identity safeguards.”

“And the workers?”

“I want their testimony preserved and labor protection requested where applicable. Not all of them know the same part of the mechanism. Some only performed assembly work.”

The magistrate leafed through the conclusion.

“The word ‘alive’ appears once.”

“Because I don't have the evidence to use it twice.”

The judge set the sheet down.

“I'll authorize the search for his whereabouts and preservation of the service opening. If the experts establish stability, we can inspect the next section under a specific order. And you'll need to notify the prosecution service of any possible irregularities in the procedure.”

Daren nodded.

“I won't let an official use this order to enter unrelated Council collections,” the judge added.

“Neither will I.”

Leaving the office, Daren found Nadia Varel seated on a corridor bench. She had returned to collect a copy of her statement. She was wearing her uniform.

“Are they going to arrest all of us?” she asked.

“There is no arrest warrant against you.”

“The supervisor says that if the condemned man's alive, someone on guard must have let him out.”

Daren glanced toward the room where the clerk was working.

“Security plans can be incomplete,” he said. “That alone neither clears nor incriminates anyone.”

“Then what am I supposed to say when they ask whether they killed him?”

He thought of the crowded square, the closed doors, and the five reports claiming to know a moment nobody outside had witnessed.

“What you saw,” he answered. “You saw him go in alive. And you didn't see what happened afterward.”

Nadia held the copy against her chest.

“That's so little.”

“It's more than we knew.”

***

Before the day ended, Daren received another report from Odel.

It was not the stability assessment. The architect would need more time to sign that.

It was a brief note about the platform's side panel, written after comparing its measurements with photographs from the first inspection. The expert had noticed a difference: the opening beneath the staircase did not look like an improvised addition made the previous night. Its edges belonged to an older structure, while the chamber's supports had been positioned to align with it.

Daren did not assume that alignment proved a clandestine destination.

It did confirm that someone would have had to know the opening existed before assembling the platform.

The note included a recommendation: locate the staircase's historical plans and the municipal closure records for that section, without excavating or opening it yet.

Daren signed the receipt.

As he lifted the last sheet, he found a copy of a photograph taken before the ceremony. The contractor had submitted it in support of its defense concerning assembly work. The image did not allow anyone to be identified with certainty.

Beside the stairs, a small service door could be made out.

Daren looked again at the photograph taken after the execution.

The door was covered by a temporary partition, the same one the experts had treated as part of the exterior finishing.

The access had not vanished during the ceremony.

It had been concealed from view.

The preservation order already covered that area. He did not need to force his way inside that night.

He called the judicial clerk.

“Add both photographs and request the names of the crew who closed off that partition. Have the assembly supervisor answer in writing.”

“What exactly are we looking for?”

Daren rested his hand on the report.

“The people who knew there was a door when everyone else thought there was only a wall.”

The city's bells sounded through the window.

In the square, for a few minutes, nobody had seen Taren Oss die.

For the first time, the case file made it possible to investigate, with evidence rather than mere suspicion, whether those very minutes had been used to get him out.

***End of Episode 16.***
