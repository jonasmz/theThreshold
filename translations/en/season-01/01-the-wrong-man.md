# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 01: The Wrong Man

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1, pending global editorial and audio review. **Format:** third-person limited prose, focused on Kael Veyran. **Production note:** Do not disclose narrative content to the creator before the full season has been completed and reviewed.

***

The first bell rang before the rain had stopped.

Kael Veyran had counted six drops sliding down the windowpane when he heard it. Not because he had any interest in counting raindrops, but because the man sitting across from him had spent almost fifteen minutes repeating that the ceremony would begin at precisely nine, and someone needed to prove, if only in silence, that time still followed rules of its own.

“Eight fifty-seven,” Kael murmured.

“Pardon?” the man asked.

“The bell. It's three minutes early.”

The court clerk looked up from his notebook.

“That's the summons bell, not the execution bell.”

“The program says, ‘First bell, nine o'clock.’”

“The program was written by a government official.”

“Exactly.”

The clerk chose not to reply. He snapped his notebook shut harder than necessary and turned toward the square.

From the observation gallery, Kael could see the execution apparatus: a temporary structure of dark timber and metal plates erected on the old stone paving. Its front doors stood open. Nothing was visible inside except a folding partition painted the same matte black as the exterior walls. Above it hung the court's emblem: a set of scales crossed by a vertical line.

Three platforms had been reserved for civilian witnesses to the left of the stage. To the right, a line of Custody officers kept the crowd back from the barriers. There were laborers still wearing clothes stained by the foundries, vendors selling hot drinks, students holding rolled-up banners, and people watching without expression, as though they had come to fulfill an obligation no one had bothered to explain.

Kael did not want to be there.

His presence was an administrative requirement. The Central Archive had to receive certified documentation of exceptional executions and later compare its integrity against the copy filed with the municipality. As an analyst, Kael had nothing to do during the ceremony itself. But his section director had decided it would be useful to send someone who could recognize a valid signature without consulting three manuals.

“I don't need you to serve as a witness,” the director had told him. “I need you to come back with a case file that won't cost us a week.”

The next morning, Kael had left his apartment before dawn for the sole purpose of following that instruction.

He had left an unwashed cup in the kitchen. Beside it lay the orange he had not finished peeling at breakfast. Seeing the fruit had brought back, for a moment, a distant afternoon: an open window, rain falling onto a wooden table, and a pair of hands removing the peel in one continuous spiral. His caregiver used to say that breaking the peel was a sign of impatience, then invariably break it herself to prove the point. Kael had smiled at the memory of her mock indignation.

The second bell brought him back to the square.

The clerk stood.

“Here they come.”

Taren Oss appeared between four Custody officers.

He was younger than the newspapers had made him seem. He had a narrow face, an unkempt beard, and a gait too stiff for someone who had spent his entire working life repairing heavy machinery. His shirt was gray and clean, its sleeves buttoned at the wrists. There were no visible shackles, but two of the officers kept their hands close to his arms.

A murmur rose from the crowd.

“Murderer!” someone shouted.

Another voice answered, “They sentenced him for speaking out!”

The officers quickened their pace. Taren did not look toward the witness platforms. He stopped for a second at the chamber entrance, tilted his head toward one of his escorts, and seemed to ask a question. The man did not answer.

Kael noticed the clerk making a mark beside the condemned man's name.

“What does that mark certify?” he asked.

“Entry into the facility.”

“He hasn't entered yet.”

The clerk pressed his lips together. Taren took one step and disappeared through the doors.

“Now he has,” Kael said.

The man crossed out the mark and made it again.

Kael wondered how many differences that small eventually became official facts. It was a familiar thought, not a suspicion. He spent his working days looking for miscopied dates, seals stamped onto documents before the ink had dried, and identical names belonging to different people. Most contradictions were born of exhaustion, vanity, or haste. They did not need a conspiracy to exist.

The magistrate appeared at a side lectern and read the confirmation of the sentence. His voice, amplified by a small resonator, trembled beneath the sound of the wind.

Taren Oss had been convicted of sabotaging an arcane regulation station. The resulting failure had killed five workers and left more than forty people permanently injured. The court maintained that the accused had known the risks of his interference. His defense argued that he had tried to prevent an unsafe operation ordered by his superiors.

Kael knew the case only through public records. Neither account seemed complete to him.

When the magistrate finished, the chamber doors closed.

The square fell almost silent.

The execution apparatus belonged to a class of facilities Kael preferred not to study. He knew that exceptional protocol required the procedure to remain hidden from those attending. The witnesses would examine the body afterward and sign the relevant certificate. The method had been contested in court for years. Some considered it a minimal concession to the condemned person's dignity; others saw it as a convenient way to conceal state violence.

A white light came on above the chamber.

Several seconds passed.

Kael heard a metallic click, followed by a dull thud that made the gallery's floorboards vibrate. It was not loud. He was surprised that something so final could announce itself so quietly.

The light went out.

The clerk glanced at his watch.

“Nine twelve.”

He recorded the time.

“Does the signal certify death?” Kael asked.

“It certifies that the procedure was carried out.”

“Those aren't the same thing.”

The man looked at him with an almost genuine weariness.

“Mr. Veyran, could you save your observations for the Archive?”

Kael inclined his head. It was not an unreasonable request.

A side door opened. A medical officer emerged carrying a folder, spoke briefly to the magistrate, and went back inside. There was a delay. The spectators began murmuring again.

Kael checked his watch.

Nine seventeen.

Five minutes to confirm something the program said should be certified in one.

The clerk turned a page, turned it back, then searched beneath his seat.

“Have you lost something?”

“The receipt form.”

“It's in your notebook.”

“No, the second copy.”

“Under the attendance sheet.”

The man found it and gave Kael a look that contained no gratitude.

In the square, the Custody officers received an order Kael could not hear. Two moved toward the doors; another told the spectators to step back. The inner curtain had shifted, but no one came out.

At last, the main door opened.

A stretcher covered by a gray sheet rolled to the edge of the stage. The magistrate recited the certification formula and called the designated witnesses forward.

Kael was not among them. For the first time, he was grateful.

One of the witnesses lifted the sheet.

The reaction was so slight that Kael almost missed it. The man took half a step backward. Then he looked at the doctor, looked back at the body, and said something the wind carried away.

The doctor answered without moving his lips.

The second witness bent closer. The third let out an exclamation.

The crowd sensed that something was wrong. Guards moved in front of the stretcher, but too late: some spectators had seen the face and begun pushing one another for a better look.

“What's happening?” the clerk asked.

Kael did not answer.

From where he stood, he could barely make out the dead man's profile. The beard seemed thicker than Taren's, although the lighting was different. The hair plastered against his forehead was streaked with gray. There was a dark mark near one ear. None of that, by itself, justified the commotion. The witnesses might simply have been shaken by the sight of death.

Then one of them raised his voice.

“That's not the condemned man!”

The doctor ordered the stretcher removed.

The magistrate demanded silence.

The crowd answered with a roar.

Kael rose to his feet.

“I need the identification form,” he said.

The clerk caught his arm.

“You're not authorized to intervene.”

“I'm not intervening. I want to know what document you're going to send to the Archive.”

The man released his sleeve.

Onstage, two Custody officers covered the body. The magistrate was speaking with the doctor and a municipal official. None of them appeared to agree on what should happen next.

The third bell began to ring.

It was not on the program.

***

An hour later, Kael sat in a courtroom office, his hands folded on a table that was far too small. Across from him waited an inspector with a broad face, a worn jacket, and a patient gaze.

“Daren Holt,” the man introduced himself. “Municipal Investigations.”

“Kael Veyran. Central Archive.”

“I know. I checked the observers' list.”

Daren placed a sealed envelope on the table.

“I need a copy of every document your institution receives.”

“We haven't received any yet.”

“Which is precisely why we're talking.”

Kael examined the envelope without touching it. It bore the magistracy's provisional seal, stamped across a paper strip carrying two signatures.

“Is that a formal request?”

“It will be when the magistrate finishes arguing with the Council Delegation.”

“Then I can't give you a formal answer yet.”

Daren let out a short laugh.

“You archivists must be wonderful in an emergency.”

“Emergencies tend to produce defective paperwork.”

“Today they produced a defective corpse.”

Kael looked up.

“A corpse can't be defective, Inspector.”

“It can if it's the wrong one.”

Neither man smiled.

Daren opened a folder and displayed an identification sheet. It held a case number, a receipt stamp, and a simplified sketch of the condemned man's face. Beneath them were biometric measurements recorded when he entered the prison system.

“The doctor won't sign the final certificate,” Daren said. “The body doesn't match this record.”

“In which fields?”

“Approximate age. A scar. Some measurements.”

“Complete biometric identity?”

“We don't have the final report.”

Kael examined the lower margin of the page.

A sequence of printed characters appeared beside the certification system's mark. It was not a prison number. Nor did it match the courts' usual format.

“Where did this copy come from?”

“The execution register.”

“Was it issued by the court?”

“That's what the record says.”

“The identifier doesn't belong to their system.”

Daren leaned forward.

“What does that mean?”

“I don't know.”

Kael hated giving that answer, but the alternative would have been a lie.

The inspector studied him for several seconds.

“Can you find out?”

“If the document legally enters the Archive, yes.”

“I'll work on that.”

Daren put the folder away.

“One more thing, Veyran. If you find an anomaly, don't settle for calling it a transcription error.”

“I never settle for that explanation until I've checked it.”

“Glad to hear it.”

After Daren left, Kael remained seated, looking at the sealed envelope.

He thought of the stretcher, the witness's exclamation, the delay between the apparatus's signal and the opening of the door. None of those facts proved anything on its own. Together, however, they formed a sequence worth examining.

He disliked discovering how much he wanted to examine it.

***

By nightfall, Vael-Tir had recovered its familiar noise.

Trams crossed the bridges with a low hum. In the Foundries, smokestacks painted the sky a muted red. Vendors dismantled their stalls in the execution square, as though an uncomfortable festival had finally ended.

Kael returned to the Central Archive to submit his observation report.

The building looked even older beneath the rain. Its towers rose between the Resonance lights and the illuminated windows of offices still working into the night. Inside, the air smelled of damp paper, warm metal, and floor soap.

A box of documents waited at the reception desk.

“It arrived twenty minutes ago,” the receptionist said. “Exceptional case file. It's been assigned to you.”

“By whom?”

“Integrity Directorate. It came with a priority order.”

Kael picked up the form.

His name appeared at the top.

Below it was an instruction: **verification of authenticity and interinstitutional consistency**.

At least someone understood that an investigation ought to begin with a question that could actually be answered.

He climbed the stairs to his office, switched on the lamp, arranged the documents across his desk, and checked the receipt signatures first.

Both were valid.

Then he checked the numbering.

The court's copy used the current format. The Custody Corps copy carried a suffix reserved for arcane investigations. A third identification sheet, with no visible issuing authority, bore the same unfamiliar code he had seen in the inspector's office.

Kael copied it onto a separate sheet of paper.

He searched the general index.

**No matches found.**

He tried the catalog of old administrative archives.

**Record does not exist.**

The system was not simply reporting an absence. It indicated that the identifier fell outside the permitted ranges.

That was strange.

An invented number might return no results. But a number rejected as invalid usually suggested that a reference table had been altered.

He stood to find a classification manual from before the latest reform. On the shelf was a blue-bound volume so worn that the lettering on its spine was barely legible.

He opened it to the section on discontinued records.

For several minutes, he heard nothing but the tapping of the rain.

He found prefixes used by defunct ministries, merged courts, and offices whose responsibilities had passed to other agencies. None matched.

He was about to close the book when he noticed a footnote printed in tiny characters.

**Series withdrawn by order of continuity.**

It did not list the numbers. It referred him to another index.

Kael looked for the reference.

The index was not on the shelf.

Nor did it appear in the electronic inventory.

He returned to his desk and examined the unidentified sheet beneath the lamp. In one corner was a tiny mark, almost invisible, that he had mistaken for a flaw in the paper.

A vertical line passed through three concentric arcs.

Unlike the signatures, it was not an administrative seal.

It looked like the insignia of an institution.

Kael searched the catalog of historical emblems.

No results.

Then he noticed something he had overlooked until now: beneath the code was a registration date written in an archaic notation.

It predated the official founding of the Secretariat of Archives.

He went still.

He checked the format. Verified the characters. Repeated the conversion.

The date did not change.

According to its own metadata, the document had been issued by an office absent from known administrative history.

And someone had inserted it into a contemporary court file that very morning.

Kael drew a slow breath.

He had spent years learning to distrust documents that seemed to say too much. This sheet, by contrast, said almost nothing.

It held only a number the Archive refused to recognize.

He switched off his desk lamp.

For a moment, the mark of the three arcs remained visible in the glow from the window.

Before leaving, he turned the lamp back on and submitted a request for access to the withdrawn index.

The system responded at once:

**The requested record has never existed.**

A second line appeared beneath the response.

**Request logged. Notification sent to the competent authority.**

Kael stared at the screen.

He had not chosen to notify anyone.

And the system did not say who that authority was.

***End of Episode 01.***
