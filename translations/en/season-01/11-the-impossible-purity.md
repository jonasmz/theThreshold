# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 11: The Impossible Purity

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel, third-person limited.

***

The crate broke open before it hit the ground.

First, one of its iron bands gave way. Then the wood struck the edge of the platform, and a row of gray plates slid onto the pavement. There was no flash, no explosion, none of the humming noise the newspapers attributed to every arcane substance. Only the sound of an improperly secured heavy object and the cry of a dockworker who pulled his hand away too late.

Inara Vel stood beside the scales at the river warehouse. She had come with Helis Rann to observe the unloading of supplies destined for municipal workshops. Their authorization allowed them to watch from a strip painted on the floor, well away from the carts. It did not permit them to touch the cargo or examine internal documents.

A foreman ordered the operation stopped.

The injured young man sat on an empty sack. Two of his fingers were bleeding, but he could move them. A coworker wrapped his hand in a clean cloth.

“Fetch the medic,” the foreman said. “And nobody go near those plates.”

Inara remained where she stood. Her first impulse had been to help; her second was to recognize that two people were already doing what was needed, and that she would only get in their way.

Helis was studying the crate's markings.

“That packaging shouldn't have come in like that,” she murmured.

“Because of the bands?”

“Because of the declaration.”

Inara followed her gaze. On one side, beneath a recent transport seal, was a label darkened by moisture. It read: *Stabilized Asterite. High purity. Origin: Kharven.*

It was not a substance capable of powering a machine by itself. Inara knew at least that much: Asterite was used to stabilize precision regulators. The energy came from elsewhere. Loose plates could break, become contaminated, or render an installation unusable if fitted without proper checks, but they were not going to set the street alight.

“The declared weight is for a lot of finished plates,” Helis said. “This is bulk-transport packaging. We need to know whether the manufacturer delivered what the buyer paid for.”

“Who's receiving the lot?”

Helis pointed to a booth painted onto the wall. A municipal Purchasing clerk waited there with an open folder. When he saw the accident, he began arguing with the foreman over who had to write the incident report.

“That man,” the accountant replied. “And now he has a problem: he can't sign for an undamaged delivery.”

Inara watched the clerk trying to separate the papers he had brought with him from those someone had just handed him. The foreman returned one sheet.

“I'm not writing that it arrived intact,” he said.

Spoken in front of six workers, the refusal did not seem heroic. It was simply the only description the man was willing to sign.

Inara recorded the time and the visible crate number. Then she put away her pencil.

She had come looking for a commercial anomaly. The first thing she found was something more ordinary: someone could get hurt because a shipment arrived under a classification nobody wanted to verify.

***

At the newsroom, Joren Mal considered the accident good news.

“Dangerous material being unloaded at a municipal warehouse,” he said. “Now that's a front page.”

“I didn't say it was dangerous material.”

“It injured a worker.”

“A badly secured crate injured him.”

Joren turned in his chair. The wall behind him was covered in advertisements; for weeks the newspaper had been negotiating discounts with two factories that had stopped buying ad space.

“And what were you investigating there?”

Inara laid three sheets on his desk.

“High-purity Asterite supplies. Arven Assembly Services appears as an intermediary in a municipal purchase. We also found movements associated with a warehouse formerly operated by Rhal Equipment.”

“You had that already.”

“I had names and an address. Now I have deliveries I can count.”

Joren picked up the first sheet.

“Fifty-seven crates. So?”

“All were declared as new or reconditioned material of Kharven mining origin during the same ninety-day period.”

“I still don't see the headline.”

“Neither do I. Not yet.”

She did not tell him that Helis had asked her not to identify the warehouse until they knew whether the accident would trigger a labor inspection. Publishing the place and time immediately might make the accountant's role as a source too obvious to those controlling transport shifts.

Joren set down the sheet.

“Last time you said you needed two days to understand Arven.”

“And I used them.”

“Now you want two more.”

“I want a number that can withstand a question.”

“Which question?”

“Where fifty-seven crates came from.”

The editor studied her for a moment.

“You could write that it's too many.”

“Only after establishing too many for what.”

Joren exhaled and pointed toward the door.

“You have until tomorrow's deadline. If you can't find a story, we'll run a piece about unsafe packaging.”

“That we can prove.”

“Then don't come back with fifty-seven questions.”

Inara gathered the sheets. She had kept her job after an incomplete accusation she still regretted. She did not confuse that second chance with infinite patience.

***

The count had begun in the transport records from the incident in the square.

Arven had moved tools, regulators, and components for the execution chamber. The old Rhal company appeared on documents dated after its dissolution, although some transactions might be explained by contract assignments. That lead brought Inara to purchases of regulators by other agencies: not just those connected to the judicial investigation, but also workshops, water-treatment plants, and compensation stations.

The figures were visible to anyone who knew to request them separately. The municipality published contract awards; transporters recorded weights; the Kharven Refiners' Guild issued quarterly bulletins to set insurance premiums. None of those offices answered for the others.

Helis had made the first calculation on a napkin.

During the quarter, fifty-seven standardized crates had entered Vael-Tir with certificates attributing their raw material to authorized Kharven mines. Those were not fifty-seven deliveries: a delivery could occupy several vehicles, and a crate could change warehouses. Helis had removed transshipments and cancellations from the count, keeping only distinct original seal numbers.

Inara checked the totals herself. Two invoices were duplicated in payment schedules, but their seals appeared only once. One shipment had been rejected and later accepted under another receipt number: it was still a single crate.

Fifty-seven remained.

The Kharven bulletin declared forty-two crate-equivalents of high-purity production during the quarter. The registered reserve inventory at the beginning of the period allowed another eight to be released. Even assuming every one of them went to Vael-Tir and none to other customers, at least seven remained unexplained.

Inara circled the result and wrote in large letters: *This is not yet proven fraud.*

There might be private inventory omitted from the bulletin. There might also be recovered material, stock from earlier periods, or an error in the conversion between refined products and shipping crates. In the contracts, “Kharven origin” might refer to a commercial intermediary rather than the mine of extraction. Each explanation required a different document.

The contradiction was not between a number and the truth. It was between a commercial claim and the declared capacity to support it.

She needed someone who understood production before the material received a label.

***

The telegraph link to the Refiners' Guild did not work on the first attempt.

The operator said a mountain line had failed and that messages were being routed through an intermediate station. Inara paid for a short, verifiable inquiry: did the forty-two published units include recovered Asterite, and were the eight reserve crates already included in that total?

The answer arrived three hours later.

*FORTY-TWO CERTIFIED NEW PRODUCTION. EIGHT RELEASED OPENING STOCK. RECOVERY ACCOUNTED FOR SEPARATELY. REQUEST DETAILS AT GUILD OFFICE.*

It was not a complete answer. Nor did it change the sum.

Inara requested the public recovery register.

While she waited, she went to Belor Sen's boardinghouse.

The former Rhal inventory clerk had changed rooms. The landlady sent her up another floor and warned her not to smoke in the hallway. Belor opened the door in a clean shirt, looking less trusting than he had during their first interview.

“They told me you've been asking about the warehouse materials.”

“I attended a public unloading.”

“In this neighborhood, that means they'll talk as if you climbed in through a window.”

“I can show you my authorization.”

“No need. Come in.”

He had found temporary work repairing tools for a bookbinding workshop. Two gears, a small wrench, and a bag of bread lay on the table.

Inara showed him a transcription of the Kharven bulletin. She had brought no third-party originals.

“You used to keep inventories. What happens when a company sells recovered Asterite as new?”

“First they'd have to find a way to certify it. If it's pure enough, some can be used again, but it doesn't leave the workshop with the same number identifying its mining origin. Someone has to declare the recovery.”

“What if they blend materials?”

“A laboratory can certify properties. It can't change where a stone was mined.”

The statement was so simple that Inara felt compelled to distrust it.

“Is that a commercial rule or a technical impossibility?”

“A rule. The technical side only tells you whether it works. Contracts say what the customer bought.”

Belor checked the figure.

“Seven crates aren't something a warehouse keeper forgets in a corner.”

“They could be private stock.”

“They could. Ask what it costs to keep them in storage, how long the packaging lasts, and who's paying for inspections. If they were stored, they left accounts behind.”

Inara wrote down all three questions.

Before she left, Belor pointed to her notebook.

“Don't put me in the article.”

“I promised you I wouldn't. That still stands.”

“I know. But sometimes journalists keep their promises and then write something that identifies the person anyway.”

Inara closed the notebook.

It was a painfully accurate observation.

“I won't mention your trade or this place,” she said. “And I won't turn your experience into proof you haven't given me.”

Belor nodded, though he did not seem entirely reassured.

***

The second telegram arrived near nightfall.

The recovery register added two more certified crate-equivalents during the quarter. That did not resolve the difference. Moreover, the municipal purchasing documents distinguished material “mined in Kharven” from recovered material. The fifty-seven crates in question had been invoiced under the first category.

Inara could not prove there were no earlier private reserves. She could demonstrate that public declarations of production, registered reserves, and recovery were insufficient to support the quantity attributed to a particular origin.

She went to see Neris Daal at the Civic Council building.

The municipal representative received her after a hearing on utility tariffs. Residents were still waiting for someone to explain why an industrial station could reduce its load and an entire neighborhood had to adjust its schedule.

“If you've come to ask me to announce fraud, you'll leave disappointed,” Neris said.

“I've come to ask for documents.”

“That sentence nearly always ends with the first one.”

Inara spread out her calculations.

Neris examined them with the attention of someone accustomed to spotting the missing figure on a budget sheet.

“This schedule includes shared-services contracts,” she observed. “Not every purchase went through the Council.”

“But some of them were paid for by the municipality.”

“Those I can audit. I can't force a Kharven mine to open its books under Vael-Tir authority.”

“I'm not asking you to. I want to know what material the municipality received and under what certificate.”

Neris placed a finger on an invoice.

“If this is wrong, someone might have paid for a grade of purity they didn't receive.”

“Or received good material with a false origin.”

“Which matters more?”

Inara thought of the dockworker's fingers and of stations dependent on precision regulators.

“To the worker opening the crate, it matters that the packaging isn't misrepresented. To the engineer installing the part, that it works and can be traced. To whoever paid for it, both.”

Neris was silent. Then she picked up a request form.

“The municipal contract requires the supplier to deposit a retained reference sample for every accepted lot. There's a sample from the shipment you mentioned. The Basin Testing Laboratory holds it.”

“May I see it?”

“No. You're not a municipal expert, and that sample doesn't belong to the newspaper. I can request a quality comparison through the audit process. The result will be added to the administrative file, subject to any applicable commercial restrictions.”

“Can it be compared with a certified Kharven sample?”

“If the laboratory has valid reference standards and the request is justified, technical management will decide.”

Inara waited while Neris finished writing.

“Why help me?”

“I'm not helping you. I'm exercising a power the voters entrusted to me. If the report contradicts your figures, you'll learn that too.”

Neris handed her a filing receipt.

“And don't publish that I've ordered a criminal investigation. I don't have that authority.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“I wish everyone else were that careful.”

***

The laboratory occupied two brick floors beside the Foundries canal. Inara arrived the next morning as the night shift was leaving. One sign forbade entry with heating equipment without authorization; another explained how to request results from tests paid for with public funds.

Its director, Ada Fenn, was not someone easily impressed by headlines.

“The municipal request concerns two different things,” she said. “One is verifying the material's quality. The other is establishing whether the samples could have the same declared provenance. Neither authorizes us to invent a quarry of origin.”

“Can you do both?”

“We have a retained sample from the municipal lot and three reference standards from certified Kharven batches. We can compare physical properties and the distribution of trace impurities. That doesn't mean we can identify every possible deposit.”

She showed Inara the seals from behind a partition. Inara did not touch the containers.

The municipal sample retained its receipt date, lot number, and the signature of the employee who had collected it. The standards came from earlier inspections, accompanied by their certificates. Ada warned that a label could be genuine yet describe its contents incorrectly; that was why the provenance of each standard needed to be documented separately.

On a bench stood a Resonance instrument powered by a workshop accumulator. A technician checked the available charge and calibrated the reader before inserting a reference sheet. The machine compared the crystalline structure's responses to low-power stimuli. It did not create energy or examine a hidden past: it recorded differences present in the samples.

“How long will it take?” Inara asked.

“Long enough to repeat anything that looks strange. I'm not issuing a report at the speed of a printing press.”

Inara smiled.

Ada did not.

Two compensation-station employees and a woman in an industrial safety uniform waited in the corridor. They were discussing a regulator taken out of service after overheating. Inara heard the name Sera Nhal when an assistant passed with a folder, but found no reason to interrupt someone else's business.

Those people's work did not stop because she needed an article. That was precisely the difference between a wrong number on a page and a defective part inside a facility.

She returned to the newsroom without results.

***

By midafternoon Joren had written a provisional headline.

**THE MATERIAL KHARVEN CANNOT PRODUCE.**

Inara tore it from the planning board.

“I'm not putting my name on that.”

“The numbers are yours.”

“The numbers say declared production isn't enough to support the listed origin. They don't say Kharven is incapable of producing it.”

“Will that distinction sell more copies?”

“That distinction keeps us from publishing a false accusation.”

Joren took the crumpled sheet.

“Do you know how many times you've told me that this week?”

“I haven't counted.”

“I have. And I'm still paying for your archive visits, telegrams, and cabs.”

Inara looked at the board. There was a note about the price of bread, another about a tram accident, and a photograph of two teachers demanding heating for their school. The newspaper could not devote itself exclusively to chasing an invisible story.

“Publish the unloading accident,” she said. “The failed restraints, the worker who received treatment, and the questions for the contractor. There are witnesses willing to speak about what they saw. I'm not going to suggest Asterite caused an explosion that never happened.”

“And your investigation?”

“I'll take responsibility for the fact that it isn't ready.”

Joren held her gaze.

Then he laid the headline on his desk.

“One more edition. Not another week.”

“One edition.”

Inara went back to her desk. She wrote the account of the accident without including Helis's name or details from which someone could infer who had brought her to the warehouse. She identified the facility because unloading procedures were public and the site already appeared in the workplace incident report. She kept responsibility for the packaging separate from any suspicion about the supply chain.

The article went to typesetting before nightfall.

For the first time in days, she had submitted something she knew she could defend line by line.

***

The laboratory report arrived when the newsroom was nearly empty.

It did not come from an anonymous source. Ada Fenn had registered it as an appendix to a municipal audit and authorized Inara to consult the unrestricted summary. The copy bore a verifiable reference and a warning that provenance tests were no substitute for investigating transport records.

Inara read the quality section first.

The Asterite in the disputed lot met the contracted purity requirements. She had not discovered worthless material being sold as good.

Then she turned to the comparative results.

The three reference samples varied among themselves, in ways consistent with the different extraction and refining sites listed on their certificates. The municipal sample was also high purity, but its trace-element distribution and crystalline response fell outside the ranges recorded for the batches named as its source.

Technical management had repeated the measurements with a different calibration and a second operator. The differences persisted.

Inara called the laboratory. Ada answered on the third attempt.

“I need you to tell me exactly what you're claiming,” Inara said.

“That the municipal sample is not compatible with the Kharven batches identified in its certificate of provenance.”

“Does that prove it came from no mine in Kharven?”

“No. There might be another source, a mixture, or a process we don't know. To attribute it to a deposit, we'd need more standards and a reconstruction of its transport. My report does not make that claim.”

“And the certificate?”

“The certificate attributes the lot to specific batches. On that claim, we do have a result: the material we received does not correspond to them.”

Inara rested her free hand on the desk.

The accounts had shown a quantity impossible to justify with the available records. The test had now added something different: a verified material discrepancy between the declared source and the delivered lot.

“Are you willing to stand by it?” she asked.

“In writing, before the competent authority. I've already signed it.”

The call ended.

Inara arranged three sheets on her desk. The transport register showed how much had arrived. The refiners' bulletin showed how much could be traced to declared production. The test showed that, for at least one batch, the certificate did not describe the physical material it purported to cover.

Separately, each document allowed incomplete explanations. Together, they required someone to demonstrate a real source.

She looked at tomorrow's front page, already closed. There was still time to call Joren, ask him to change it, and turn her investigation into the biggest story she had published since her mistake two years before.

She did not.

Instead, she dialed the laboratory and formally requested the list of tests the contract allowed the public to consult. Then she drafted two questions for the supplier: who had issued the certificates, and who had received the samples before they entered municipal custody.

As she put away the papers, she noticed a sentence in the technical summary she had overlooked.

*The observed difference cannot be attributed to an ordinary variation in purity.*

Until that afternoon, she had been investigating whether someone was selling more Asterite than Kharven claimed to produce.

Now she knew something narrower, and more solid.

Someone had certified material as originating from specific batches that the laboratory could distinguish it from.

And for days, the city had been treating it as material of verified origin.

***End of Episode 11.***
