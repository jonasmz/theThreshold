# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 10: The Dead Facility

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, third-person limited.

***

Inspector Daren Holt had secured a court order to examine three categories of documents and authorization to enter a property.

Kael Veyran considered the second less useful than the first.

“If the station is closed,” he said, “all we'll see is ruins.”

“Ruins have owners, too.”

“And owners usually have archives.”

“That's why I brought both.”

Daren tapped the folder against his palm. They stood before an iron gate in the old eastern freight district. Workshops, a tram depot, and a timber warehouse surrounded them. The station shown on the plans had been absorbed into other buildings. Its original entrance was now a side wall with a small door and a municipal number plate.

The order did not allow them to open every room. It identified the historical parcel, authorized inspection of facilities linked to the execution-chamber investigation, and required a representative of the owner to be present.

The representative arrived twenty minutes late.

His name was Farlen Odris. He managed storage buildings for a real-estate company and carried a ring of keys and the expression of a man whose breakfast had been interrupted.

“We don't operate machinery here,” he said as he unlocked the gate. “We store spare parts for municipal buildings.”

“That's what we're trying to verify,” Daren replied.

“The building was inspected six months ago.”

“By whom?”

“Maintenance.”

Kael looked up.

“What kind of maintenance?”

Farlen pointed at the lock.

“Doors, damp, that sort of thing.”

It was not a suspicious answer. It was the kind of work any abandoned building required if its owners did not want it to collapse.

They went inside.

The entrance hall smelled of dust and damp timber. Recent boards had reinforced the floor, and one wall still bore the outline of a clock someone had removed years before. Daren recorded the visible changes as they walked.

A corridor ended in a room containing four metal cabinets and a water pump no longer in service.

“This is the area we use,” Farlen explained. “Other rooms have been walled up.”

“For safety?” Kael asked.

“Because they used to flood.”

Daren showed him the closure plan.

“The old regulation room should be here.”

Farlen came closer.

“That's behind the south wall. They sealed it before I started working here.”

“Can we get in?”

“With authorization for construction work. We'd have to open the wall.”

Daren checked the court order. It did not permit demolition.

“We won't break anything today,” he said.

Kael was relieved. The possibility of finding a sealed room did not justify destroying a wall whose stability they had not established.

While Daren inspected the cabinets, Kael examined the inventory labels in plain view.

There were spare parts belonging to different agencies. Most bore new numbers over older inscriptions. One cabinet held low-voltage network accessories. Another contained unpackaged mechanical components.

On a shelf, several boxes bore a mark of three strokes around an open dot.

The figure no longer surprised him.

“Who cataloged these?” he asked.

Farlen shrugged.

“They came like that. We received them from another depot.”

“When?”

“Before I took over management.”

“Do you have the delivery notes?”

“If they exist, they'll be at the central office.”

Kael noted the cabinet number and asked Daren to include the labels in the inspection record. He did not touch the boxes.

The station might be nothing more than a storage room. The same recovered material had circulated through several addresses, as the contracts Inara had investigated suggested. By itself, this finding brought them no closer to the mechanism in the square.

Then Daren called from the end of the corridor.

“I want you to see something.”

On the floor beside the walled-up section was a drain covered by a grate. Next to it, a rectangular patch of paint was cleaner than the rest.

“Someone removed a plate,” Daren said.

“Or replaced one.”

“Look at the screws.”

All four were new. The wall supports were old.

Kael crouched.

“It could be damp-prevention maintenance.”

“It could. But the manager says no work was done in this section.”

Farlen hurried over, alarmed.

“I said they sealed it before I arrived. I don't know what the previous owner did.”

Daren nodded and corrected the record. It was an important distinction.

***

The inspection did not open the wall.

Daren requested a structural assessment and supplementary authorization. Farlen signed the attendance record and took a copy. The parcel was placed under a limited preservation order: no components could be removed from the designated section without notifying the court.

On the way back, Kael reviewed his notes.

“The documents prove that Subunit E-0 has recent maintenance movements,” he said. “The property contains old parts, but we don't know whether those operations took place here.”

“The wall is a good start.”

“It's a circumstance, not a destination.”

“I'll offer you a reward,” Daren said. “The day you say ‘we have proof’ without adding five qualifications, I'll buy you dinner.”

“Without counting the qualifications on the bill?”

“Of course.”

Kael put away his notebook.

“The first one will be expensive.”

Daren smiled and turned toward the courthouse street.

Kael continued to the Archive. There he found a message from Tessa Ord: the technical documents section had located a record of equipment inspections corresponding to the E-0 cost center.

The record carried an old digital signature.

The modern office reproducing it did not recognize its algorithm.

***

Tessa had laid the record out on a consultation table.

“It's not a handwritten signature,” she explained. “It's a verification sequence printed by an old archival system. It survived the migrations.”

“What does it guarantee?”

“Only that the document bears a mark compatible with the system that issued it. It doesn't guarantee the work was performed.”

Kael examined the record.

There were two sheets. The first was a recent regulator inspection order. The second contained a source reference and a validation chain.

“The maintenance date isn't the signature date,” he said.

“Exactly. The order was logged now using an inherited procedure.”

“How long has that procedure been in use?”

Tessa looked concerned.

“That's what's strange. It's not in the current catalog.”

“A withdrawn standard?”

“Maybe. I expected to find it in the table of obsolete algorithms. It isn't there.”

Kael requested permission to copy the system reference. Tessa gave him a certified reproduction of the heading, warning that the complete document required a specific application.

The prefix was familiar: not identical to that of the port supplement, but part of the same family of designations.

“We need to establish when it stopped being used,” he said.

“We can consult the old guides. But some of the earliest migration tables were stored outside this building.”

“In Continuity?”

Tessa nodded.

Kael set down the copy.

He could not go from one office to another indefinitely, confirming a code without knowing who had actually produced the document. He needed evidence independent of the Archive.

“The equipment manufacturer?” he asked.

“Disappeared generations ago.”

“Who maintains the signature verifiers?”

“The Technical Authentication Service. Its laboratories work for several agencies. If the protocol preserves a mathematically verifiable pattern, they can test it without knowing the case file's contents.”

It was a different approach. The Archive could certify a document's history. A laboratory could examine the technique used to sign it.

Kael submitted the request that same day.

***

Engineer Sera Nhal greeted Kael with her shirt sleeves rolled up and a small burn covered by a bandage.

He had gone to the Technical Authentication Service looking for a specialist. The office referred him to a maintenance station where Sera worked, an engineer accredited to examine old equipment. She was not responsible for the entire network and had no authority to consult any restricted project she pleased.

“They tell me you need to validate a historical protocol,” she said.

“I need to identify it. Whether the document is authentic is a separate question.”

“Good answer.”

Sera indicated a workbench with an opened regulator on it. The equipment was disconnected from the line, with a red tag hanging from its switch.

“What do you know about the signature's origin?”

Kael showed her the copy of the heading.

Sera read the prefix and fell silent for several seconds.

“I don't know of this protocol being used in service,” she said.

“Could it belong to a retired industrial system?”

“Possibly. If you authorize a comparison with the public catalog of historical signatures, I can check whether it belongs to a known family. I don't need access to the restricted file.”

Kael agreed.

The engineer switched on a tabletop reader powered by a small accumulator. The device read the sequence and compared its structure with patterns stored locally. There was no extraordinary display, just a list of mismatches and two partial matches.

“It has a valid header for a dual-origin validation system,” Sera said. “But the catalog doesn't identify the issuing authority.”

“Can it be forged?”

“Any system can be forged with sufficient resources. The question is whether this pattern was generated by a tool that existed on the stated date.”

“And was it?”

Sera switched off the reader.

“I don't know yet. I'd need an authentic historical signature for comparison. One of the two partial matches points to a family retired before the current phase regulators were installed.”

“How much earlier?”

“I don't have the precise chronology here.”

Kael looked at the sheet.

“What does ‘dual-origin’ mean?”

“In old systems, certain operations required approval from two separate circuits. The mark combined both verifications. They weren't necessarily two people; they could be two independent devices.”

“Like a maintenance order?”

“Perhaps. But don't turn the shape of a signature into a complete story.”

Kael smiled despite himself.

“I've heard something similar.”

Sera leaned against the workbench.

“I can issue a report on technical compatibility without claiming provenance. For historical identification, you'd need a pattern archive dating from before the Great Silence.”

Kael did not respond.

He had heard the term in institutional history classes: the Great Silence referred to a long interruption in records, shifting jurisdictions, and the closure of technical centers. It was not taught as a single event. It was the name given to a period that caused trouble for any documentary researcher.

But Sera had just placed the document's signature family before that period.

“Where might an authentic pattern survive?” he asked.

“In a depot of instruments that were never migrated. If any remain.”

***

Daren called that night with news from the station.

The structural expert had found a ventilation duct behind the wall that remained connected to an extraction system. The equipment could operate on an external power supply, but there was still no authorization to open the section and determine where it led.

“Then the wall isn't simply an old closure,” Kael said.

“At least there's an installation someone kept operational. We still need to know who and why.”

“The marked boxes?”

“The owner provided a copy of a delivery note. They came from a company that bought materials from a municipal depot twelve years ago.”

“Which company?”

Daren gave him the name.

Kael wrote it down. It did not match the companies Inara had identified, but that did not rule out an indirect commercial connection.

“We need to trace the delivery note back to its origin,” he said.

“We've already requested that.”

“When can we open the wall?”

“When the judge receives the safety report. I'm not putting two workers in front of a pressurized duct just to save a day.”

Kael agreed.

After the call, he looked at the copy of the heading. A dual-origin system, a recent maintenance record, and a signature from a retired family. The pieces still did not form one explanation.

And for the first time he was no longer sure the right question was which building remained in operation.

Perhaps what mattered was who had kept alive a method of authorizing work that current institutions could no longer identify.

***

The next morning he found Tessa waiting beside his desk.

She was carrying a small volume wrapped in protective paper.

“It didn't come from a restricted collection,” she said before he could ask. “It's a technical manual published for infrastructure inspectors more than a century ago.”

Kael opened it.

The book described the migration of authentication protocols after a reorganization of the networks. One page contained a table of withdrawn seals. The same prefix as the E-0 order was listed among families declared obsolete.

“See the date?” Tessa asked.

Kael read it.

The protocol's last recognized issuance dated back decades before the Great Silence.

“The maintenance document could be using a copied signature,” he said. “That doesn't mean the original system is still active.”

“Correct.”

Tessa turned the page.

There was a note: *Validators in this family require a response sequence generated during each operation. A static reproduction of the seal is insufficient to pass a full verification.*

Kael read the sentence again.

“Does the modern record include that sequence?”

“The certified copy says it does.”

“Was it verified or merely stored?”

“That's what we need to ask.”

They requested a technical check from Sera through the Technical Authentication Service's formal channels.

The response arrived at noon, signed and limited to what the laboratory could support.

The heading of the recent record contained a variable response consistent with a new operation. This was not definitive proof that the historical issuing equipment still existed: a modern system with sufficient documentation might emulate the protocol. But it was not merely a passive reproduction of an old seal, either.

Kael reread the date.

The document had been entered eleven days earlier.

The procedure said to have signed it had been absent from official catalogs for generations.

Tessa sat opposite him.

“What are you going to do?”

Kael took a blank sheet.

“Request an audit of the issuing authority.”

“And if there isn't one?”

“Then someone will have to explain who's generating valid signatures in the name of an authority that ceased to exist.”

He wrote the request heading. This time he was not looking for an incomplete figure or a service connection without an address.

He was looking for the origin of a signature that should no longer be possible to issue.

***End of Episode 10.***
