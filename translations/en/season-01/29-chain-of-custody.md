# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 29: Chain of Custody

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Daren Holt and Inara Vel, third-person limited, alternating only at scene breaks.

***

The envelope arrived at the courthouse bearing seals from two different offices.

Daren Holt received it in the presence of the case clerk and did not open it immediately. The first inscription belonged to the shared-infrastructure authority; the second, to the judicial registry that had requested the information following the inspection of the galleries under the square. It had taken weeks to obtain this response.

“The compensation facility acknowledges receipt of our request,” the clerk said. “It doesn't grant blanket permission to search its premises. It supplies three certified documents and offers a joint inspection of the service vestibule.”

“The vestibule, not the inner chamber?”

“The stability report prohibits opening the technical enclosure. No order permits endangering its containment.”

Daren agreed.

The envelope contained a reproduced entry ledger certified by folio, a shift personnel list, and a medical incident record. The originals remained in their existing custody. The issuing office had supplied copies with a register identifying who obtained them, when, and under what authority.

The medical record bore the ceremony's date.

In the field for the person's description it said: *Male, conscious, received from service transfer; no entry through public door recorded.*

Daren read the line twice.

“Identity?” the clerk asked.

“It isn't given. We have to establish what that transfer meant and who witnessed it.”

A marginal note referred to the shift of a medical assistant who no longer worked at the facility. Her name appeared on the personnel list: Nela Ordan. The transfer record preserved an address for professional notifications, not a private address the police could use to find her without permission.

Daren closed the folder.

He had expected a passage with an answer at its far end. The first answer had arrived, like most in this case, on paper that needed to be tested against another source.

“We'll request her statement through the cooperation procedure,” he said. “And ensure the defense receives the material it's entitled to before we draw conclusions.”

The clerk prepared the notice.

On the desk still lay a photograph of the body shown to the city after the execution.

Daren turned it facedown.

Not because he wanted to stop looking.

Because the dead person still had no name, and the man who entered the chamber alive might still have one.

***

The municipal purchasing office had a window that would not close properly.

Inara Vel held her folder against the frame while a draft stirred the receipts. She had requested only public entries for a supply intervention: contracted quantities, the batch number of regulation components, certificates of delivery, and payments actually authorized.

The Audit officer supplied three extracts.

“The review report becomes public today,” he said. “The commercial investigation remains open. We don't have a fraud conviction.”

“I don't intend to write that you do.”

“Then look at the difference between what was billed and what was received.”

Inara turned a page.

Arven had been paid for two certified assemblies intended for network maintenance. The receiving office recorded one as compliant and another as subject to a rejection notice. The second identifier was later transferred to a replacement order without a record of a new compliance test. The payment ledger, however, described two accepted deliveries.

“Was the rejected component actually installed?” she asked.

“We can't establish that from public documents. The review must determine what happened to the batch and who approved payment compliance. A possible offense belongs to another authority.”

Inara recorded the qualification.

The anomaly was not a rumor. Two incompatible figures appeared in separate records, alongside a financial transfer they could both describe. There might also have been people who acted correctly within a defective reporting system.

“Was the company notified?”

“Yes. It has time to respond. The report includes its initial answer.”

Inara read it.

Arven claimed one assembly had been replaced under a commercial warranty and its certification might have been recorded in another office's ledger. It had not yet produced the entry.

The journalist put the copy away.

She did not know whether the company had deceived the city or the city had paid against documents its departments could not reconcile. Each possibility required different questions.

Outside, a line of workers waited for news about reduced shifts.

A woman asked whether the article would bring back the spare parts.

“I can't promise that,” Inara replied.

The woman looked toward the door again.

“Then at least write down who's supposed to answer us.”

***

Odel Mar had asked to build the model in a workshop, not the square.

Daren found him arranging two wooden guides and a testing tray loaded with sacks. The structure reproduced the dimensions measured beneath the execution chamber but was not connected to any judicially protected site or machinery carrying people.

“This won't prove it happened,” Odel said before greeting him. “It'll show whether it could happen and what conditions it required.”

The first condition was a coupling component missing from the preserved chamber. Its identification had appeared in the specific inventory of extraordinary tools requested from the contractor, through the same commercial records that had earlier established a loan but not its final use.

The court had ordered the object located by inventory number. A maintenance warehouse retained it among returned spares. Daren had witnessed its seizure, with an official record, condition photographs, and two signatures. The object was now in expert custody, with no permission to clean it or test it on the original apparatus.

Odel showed a reproduction made to its dimensions.

“The fittings align with the points we photographed beneath the platform,” he said. “The original metal has marks that may indicate use, but we need to analyze them separately. I won't ask the judge to treat an ordinary wear mark as a unique identification.”

“And the ceremony's timing?”

“There's an external signal around nine twelve and a presentation of the body around nine seventeen. The various offices' clocks weren't synchronized to the second. That's an approximate window, not an absolute stopwatch.”

An assistant pushed the loaded testing tray.

The first attempt jammed at a wooden joint.

Odel stopped it.

“On the original, that resistance might have required two people or a better-leveled surface. If we pretend it worked on the first try, we learn nothing.”

They adjusted the model's support and repeated the movement, recording the change and the reason.

The tray covered the section within the possible time window. A second load then came from the service side. No bodies transformed, and there was no Pulse discharge: the operation required doors, operators, and enough space to conceal its intermediate phase.

Daren looked at the empty platform.

“Could they move a conscious person?”

“The space would allow it. The model doesn't prove whether anyone was conscious or moved on that day.”

“That will have to come from elsewhere.”

Odel released the lever.

“And don't forget: even a machine capable of doing it requires someone willing to use it.”

***

Joren Mal wanted a front-page headline with a word Inara still could not write.

“Fraud,” he said, tapping the desk with his pen. “That's what readers will understand when they see two payments and a rejected component.”

“The report establishes a payment inconsistent with receipt and an open review. It doesn't identify who falsified the acceptance.”

“What about the chamber contractor?”

“He appears in another series of orders. We can't add the two together and call them one operation without demonstrating the connection.”

The editor leaned back.

In the next room, the press had stopped for lack of power. Two apprentices argued about continuing by hand or waiting for the next supply window. Inara listened without taking her eyes off the papers.

She had written about cuts, intermediaries, and the cost of supplies failing to arrive on time. She could feel the pressure to bring back something definitive. For weeks the city had heard that restrictions were necessary for safety, yet workshops continued shutting down machines and some carriers had lost routes.

“We have a verifiable fact and an answer still pending,” she said. “That's enough for serious reporting. We also have Arven's right of reply, which I want to publish with the proper qualifications.”

Joren gathered the receipts.

“The court will say something about the execution soon. Every newsroom is waiting for the ruling.”

“I'm not getting it through a side door.”

“I didn't ask you to.”

“You will if someone says another paper has a leak.”

The editor set down his pen.

“Then prepare two versions: one with Audit's public figures, and one we can finish only if the court publishes a ruling. No protected files or sources' names.”

Inara agreed.

At the corner of her desk was a certification from Seris Hal concerning Belor Sen's deposit. The former inventory clerk had left the city; his destination and reasons remained unverified. His envelope remained under its original custody conditions. The journalist could not turn it into an argument merely because its owner was absent to contradict her.

She separated the certificate from her publication papers.

Never had she felt so clearly the difference between the story she wanted to tell and the story she could defend.

***

Nela Ordan came to give her statement accompanied by a labor adviser.

Daren had not sought her at home or sent agents to her current workplace. The summons had gone through the judicial-cooperation authority and offered measures to prevent unnecessary disclosure of her address. Her former duties were identified in the service ledgers, but the woman had the right not to bear alone an investigation she had not initiated.

The room was small. A court clerk attended, along with authorized prosecution and defense representatives. The testimony was recorded in full, without journalists or unrelated officials present.

“What did you see?” Daren asked.

Nela looked at the copy of her shift record.

“We received a stretcher from the municipal corridor. The man was covered up to his waist. His eyes were open, and he asked for water. I was told he couldn't stay there. I checked his pulse because nobody had recorded an earlier assessment.”

“Can you identify him as Taren Oss?”

“At the time, I didn't know the condemned man. I recognized the number on his transfer wristband when I was shown the admission record. Later I saw his image in public announcements. He resembled the man I treated, but I don't want to use a photograph to pretend I have a perfect identification.”

Daren appreciated the correction.

“What did you write?”

“Conscious, answers questions, pulse present. I saw no injuries consistent with a recent execution. I recorded the wristband number and my signature.”

The original ledger preserved the entry. The office had sent a certified reproduction. Its timestamp could not be equated to the minute with the exterior chamber clock, but it belonged to the interval after the ceremony began and before the corpse appeared before the public, according to the shift and available records.

“Do you know where they took him?”

“He left our vestibule through another door with service personnel. I couldn't accompany him. I don't know whether he later died or who ordered the final transfer.”

“Why didn't you testify earlier?”

Nela clasped her hands.

“My report was filed as assistance to a shared-infrastructure user. I didn't know about the court case. When the execution appeared in the news, I consulted my supervisor. She told me the data were restricted and I couldn't hand them to the press.”

“Did she order you to lie?”

“No. She ordered me to follow our workplace procedure. I wasn't summoned until now.”

Her adviser requested that distinction be entered verbatim into the record.

Daren agreed.

The woman had seen a man alive.

They still needed other sources to show he was the same man the square believed had died.

***

Seris Hal did not bring an open envelope to court.

The prosecution had formally requested incorporation of the document Vera Leth deposited before the ceremony. Seris appeared with the package sealed after its voluntary opening in the previous episode, the original deposit ledger available to the authority, two matching receipts, and photographs of the first seal. A specific custody record documented its transfer from her office to the examination room. No page passed through the newsroom.

Daren witnessed receipt alongside the parties' representatives and a document examiner.

There were four sheets and a drawing.

The first referred to the petition for preservation of files on tests involving people. The second described attempts to report wrongdoing. The third detailed a planned intervention involving the execution apparatus. The last assigned roles using initials still requiring attribution.

Daren compared the drawing with Odel's photographs.

The lower opening, tray, and lateral space appeared in positions compatible with the examined scene. Some details differed: the drawing placed a crank where the apparatus had a lever, and omitted a reinforcement installed during later municipal work.

“Does that invalidate the document?” the prosecutor asked.

The examiner held the sheet by its edges.

“It may be an earlier design version. It could also be a plan never carried out. We must record both alternatives. A deposit made before the ceremony makes the plan's anticipation relevant; it doesn't make every instruction true.”

The central text stated that the bodily result shown to witnesses must differ from the person who entered, to force an investigation of identity and medical records.

Daren felt anger unlike what he had felt on seeing the chamber's first images.

The corpse had been used to create a public question.

The question might be legitimate. The use of an unnamed dead person was not.

Vera had testified that she transcribed the plan and objected to carrying it out. Daren considered her neither guilty nor innocent on that basis. Her signature and involvement would be compared with other records, with room for challenge.

The prosecutor requested a technical comparison of the drawing, the located coupling, and the preserved apparatus.

The judge authorized that comparison alone.

He did not authorize opening Council reserves or searching every old building resembling the drawing.

***

Inara arrived at Sera Nhal's workshop with a copy of Audit's public report.

Sera agreed to speak after her shift. She did not allow photographs of the interior or removal of a component still under her employer's technical control. She could speak about her labor complaint and the unrestricted findings of a rejection she had signed, but not certify the entire regional supply system.

“Is the batch number shown here yours?” Inara asked.

Sera studied the sheet.

“It belongs to the family of parts I inspected. My report identifies a specific order. I can't tell you that component ended up on Arven's invoice without checking the chain.”

“What can you confirm?”

“That I refused to accept a plate with the required tolerance, documented the reason, and faced pressure to finish the shift without repeating the test. That's in the labor file. If Audit says a rejected component was recorded as compliant, it must show how it linked the numbers.”

Inara recorded each qualification.

Sera removed her gloves, revealing a thin scar on her wrist.

“My colleague's been working overtime for two weeks because we're still missing the correct part,” she said. “Everyone asks whether the network has been saved. The workshop doesn't work with words like *saved*. It works with parts that break or don't.”

“I want to publish your documented rejection and the fact that Audit is investigating the invoice. May I name you?”

The engineer hesitated.

“My name, yes. Not my colleague's or the technician who took the second sample. And don't write that I discovered a conspiracy. I found a plate that shouldn't have been certified.”

Inara agreed.

Sera returned to her bench.

“If you publish the error without explaining who must pay for a replacement, there'll be another plate like it tomorrow, and a different workshop.”

The journalist put away her notebook.

She had gone looking for evidence.

She left with a responsibility too: not to turn Sera's precision into an accusation she had never made.

***

The inspection of the historical vestibule did not open a Transit route.

Daren descended with Odel, Elia Torset, and a technical representative of the body responsible for the old compensation facility. The judicial authorization permitted examination only of the reception area and its material records. The inner enclosure remained behind a technical barrier, its containment and controls under another jurisdiction.

Cira Venn supervised entry from the municipal gallery. The pump kept working; nobody had requested a drainage shutdown to ease the investigation.

The iron door opened into a short vestibule.

A rolling guide continued through it with the same orientation and width as the municipal section. A lateral support showed fastening points compatible with the coupling examined by Odel. Compatibility was not proof that every component belonged to the same assembly, but the geometric continuity and service route no longer ended at an unknown wall.

“The door could have received ordinary cargo,” the technician warned.

“We'll record that too,” Daren answered.

An old delivery-record compartment was set into the wall. Beneath a transparent panel was a list of historical shifts and wristband numbers for people who had received attention there. The original entry from the day under investigation had been removed under an administrative preservation order and was among the certified documents delivered to the court.

Odel measured the distance between the support and the reception room.

“The tray could stop here and let a stretcher continue along the corridor,” he said. “That makes Nela's record compatible with the route. It doesn't establish who pushed it or why the exterior guards didn't intervene.”

Daren examined the floor.

The place did not look like a secret laboratory. It was a transit room with walls scratched by freight, a protected lamp, and the smell of old dust. Precisely for that reason, it could have concealed something: it had been built for people and goods to move without attracting attention.

The technical representative asked them not to photograph an internal configuration plate.

“It's outside the authorized area, and disclosing it would identify other installations,” she explained.

Daren lowered the expert's camera.

“We need the route we can support in our case. Not a map we're not entitled to take away.”

As they emerged, Cira asked whether they had found what they were looking for.

Daren looked at the measurement record.

“We found a section that connects. And someone who can testify about what she saw at its end.”

Cira nodded.

“Then give me the passage back so I can keep the water running.”

Elia checked the reversible closure and signed the handover.

***

The evidence hearing took an entire morning.

Daren presented a reconstruction separating four findings.

Taren Oss had entered the chamber alive, as Nadia Varel testified. Ilda Rehn's exterior signal did not certify death. A tray, guide, and functionally compatible coupling made it possible to move a person through the concealed section. Inspection of the gallery and vestibule established material continuity as far as the service facility. There, a medical assistant had contemporaneously recorded a conscious man whose transfer identifier matched the one in Taren's case file.

The body displayed after the ceremony was not Taren, according to Mara Ellin's report, and showed evidence of preparation before the event.

The plan deposited before the ceremony deliberately anticipated substitution of a body.

The defense asked whether Nela could swear she had seen the condemned man inside the chamber.

“No,” Daren said. “She saw him in the vestibule. Identification rests on her note, the wristband number, and the transfer chain. No individual item is sufficient.”

“Does Odel's test prove it was done in five minutes?”

“It provides no exact time. It demonstrates feasibility within an approximate window, under specified conditions with operators. We aren't equating unsynchronized clocks.”

“Can you prove Taren is alive today?”

“No.”

The magistrate remained silent.

Then he asked Mara to explain the corpse's chronology. The medical examiner did not offer a name her laboratory could not substantiate. She reaffirmed the incompatible identification and preservation treatment predating public display.

The judge read the documentary list.

The ruling did not acquit anyone or automatically identify those who operated the mechanism. It ordered the scope of the death certificate linked to the ceremony corrected: the death of the displayed body could no longer be attributed to Taren Oss. The search into his whereabouts would continue, and separate proceedings would investigate altered documents, use of human remains, and possible responsibility among those who organized the intervention.

The prosecution would receive access to relevant originals under the case rules. Suspects and potential suspects would have the applicable rights to challenge evidence. The court would publish a redacted factual summary without protected witness information or dangerous technical routes.

Daren listened without triumph.

They had substantiated a physical operation and removal alive from the execution section, through contemporaneous testimony and cross-checked documents.

They had not found Taren.

Nor did they yet know whose body it was.

***

The news reached the newsroom as a public ruling, not a leak.

Inara checked its number, court seal, and the pages actually released. The distributed version omitted technical route details, the witness's address, and protected documents. It contained facts the public had a right to know: the death certificate attributed to Taren was unsustainable, the body's identity was incompatible, and the court was investigating deliberate intervention in the apparatus.

Joren read the summary beside her.

“We can publish this.”

“With these limitations. We can't add who removed him or where he is.”

“People will ask why they believed one witness.”

“We can explain that the conclusion doesn't depend on one person without identifying her.”

The editor set aside an advertising column and told the apprentices to prepare the front page.

Inara worked on two separate articles.

The first explained the court ruling in accessible language, without treating a hypothesis of responsibility as a conviction. The second examined Audit's public report, supply payments, Arven's response, and the technical rejection Sera had confirmed. Repeated commercial names were presented as matters requiring investigation, not proof of one overarching conspiracy.

A short note explained why the newspaper omitted information: certain routes remained unverified, and publishing restricted details could endanger people or installations. Inara did not dress the omission as an enticing mystery. She stated it as a professional limit.

As they prepared to close, Joren asked about Belor.

“Can't we at least say he left?”

“We could prove he departed the city, but not that his absence is connected to this. He never authorized us to identify him as a source.”

Joren looked at the front page again.

“Then leave him out.”

Inara felt strange gratitude for the quiet decision.

At printing time, power returned to the press during the announced window. Sheets began emerging with an uneven rhythm. One apprentice collected them while another counted them for delivery.

At last the city would learn a verifiable part of what happened.

Not all of it.

***

Mara Ellin was waiting for Daren beside the medical storage room.

She held a sample box under two seals and a judicial communication addressed only to authorized members of the investigation. Daren verified its identification before receiving it.

“The issuing office's response arrived through formal cooperation,” Mara said. “It concerns the order to prepare remains we found early in the case. It now identifies the class of procedure from which that body came.”

They entered a room whose door could close without blocking the corridor. The prosecutor and clerk joined them. The documents included a certified administrative section and a medical annex requiring special access control.

Daren read the heading of the original entry.

It was not a criminal-execution record.

It was a death record from a **restricted stabilization trial involving a human participant**. The entry into remains storage predated the public ceremony. The preservation order used the same batch identifier as the documentation for the body received by the court.

Mara laid the sample results on the table.

“A matching batch identifier doesn't establish a person's identity on its own,” she said. “But the biological profile, preservation indicators, and timeline are compatible with the person registered as having died in that trial. The issuing body certifies that a body from the series was transferred into custody. We still need the name and authorization for the remains' release.”

The prosecutor asked them not to say the restricted program code aloud.

Daren looked up.

“Can we say the person displayed before the square had been used in a human experiment?”

Mara chose her words.

“We can say that material evidence and authenticated records establish a substantiated connection to a stabilization trial in which an earlier death was recorded. Definitive identification and responsibility for disposing of the remains require further steps.”

“Does the office identify who authorized the transfer?”

“It identifies a restricted department and has forwarded the request to another authority. No names yet.”

Daren felt the weight of a case opening just as it seemed to be reaching its end.

The group that designed the intervention might have wanted to expose real abuses. That neither explained nor excused how it obtained a corpse, or who chose to use it to deceive a city. There were different victims, with separate duties to identify them.

He requested that the report remain under judicial custody and that the dead person's identity be sought by a limited official request. He also wanted the trial records preserved, with access for the parties under protections for other people's data.

The prosecutor agreed.

Someone called from the corridor to deliver the first newspaper pages.

Daren did not go to receive them.

He remained before the dead man's form.

They were no longer investigating only how an execution had displayed the wrong body.

They now had documentary evidence of where that body might have come from, and an institution obliged to answer why its death had happened before the city knew it needed to ask questions.

***End of Episode 29.***
