# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 04: The Closed Square

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Daren Holt, third-person limited.

***

The first person to try entering the execution chamber that morning was carrying a bucket and a broom.

Daren Holt stopped her two steps from the door.

“The facility is under judicial custody.”

The woman, who looked about fifty, glanced at the tape across the entrance and then at her bucket.

“They told me to come clean.”

“Who did?”

“The maintenance supervisor.”

“Name?”

She took a moment to answer. She did not seem to be hiding anything; she was simply trying to pick out a man whose name she had heard only once from among too many others.

“Bren. I think they call him Bren.”

Daren wrote it down.

“Does he work for the municipality?”

“For the contractor. I clean here when they call me in.”

“Did they give you written instructions?”

The woman took a folded piece of paper from her apron pocket.

It was an ordinary work order with no judicial seal. It was dated the previous day, several hours after the execution.

Daren examined it carefully.

“May I keep a copy?”

“As long as I get paid for the day, you can keep the whole thing.”

He let her leave.

The square was almost empty. The vendors’ stalls were gone, the wooden barriers were stacked beside a cart, and two municipal guards watched over the chamber. From a distance, it looked like the remains of an interrupted celebration.

Daren had requested a full mechanical inspection. His expert would arrive before noon. Until then, the order was simple: no one was to touch the structure.

That included employees sent to clean it.

He had learned that evidence did not always disappear in a fire or a spectacular theft. Often, all it took was someone sweeping up dust, replacing a screw, or moving a plank without recording it.

The inspector looked up at the courthouse windows. A curtain fluttered in one of them.

He thought about the body found after the ceremony.

It was not Taren Oss. That conclusion was beginning to carry documentary weight.

The question now was whether Taren had come out alive—and, if so, how.

***

The mechanical expert arrived with two assistants and a suitcase that seemed to contain enough metal to build another chamber.

His name was Odel Mar. He had enormous hands and spoke so calmly that Daren suspected he had never once run to catch a tram.

“Do you want to know how it works, or how someone could have modified it?” he asked.

“Both.”

“Then we'll start with how it's supposed to work.”

Odel opened the front door and pointed to the interior structure.

The chamber contained a restraint platform, an activation mechanism, and a series of panels shielding spectators from a direct view of the procedure. Beneath the floor lay a space intended for servicing moving parts.

Daren knelt beside the plate he had noticed the day before.

“Is this part of the design?”

Odel examined the screws.

“Not exactly.”

“What does ‘not exactly’ mean?”

“It means the plans include an inspection plate, but the one in front of us doesn't match the dimensions. It's larger.”

The expert took out a folding measuring instrument and checked the edges.

“They also changed the fastening points.”

“When?”

“I can't tell you that just by looking at the metal.”

“Can you determine whether the changes are recent?”

“We can compare wear, paint, and tool marks. But then we'll need to see the contractor's records.”

Daren nodded.

At the back, one assistant began photographing the joints. Another prepared bags for small paint samples.

The chamber stood on a raised platform supported by temporary columns. Between its floor and the stone of the square was a dark space, too low for anyone to walk upright.

Odel lightly rapped one of the plates with his knuckles.

It sounded different from the others.

“There's a hollow space,” he said.

Daren felt something tighten across his back.

“A mechanism?”

“Maybe. Or a support may have been set badly. I'm not opening it until we've finished documenting the structure.”

That caution reassured him.

If there was a concealed passage, the investigation would have a concrete direction.

If not, they would have to keep looking.

Odel straightened.

“I need the approved plan and every modification order.”

“I requested them yesterday.”

“And?”

“They sent an incomplete copy.”

The expert looked at him.

“Then today we'll find out whether something is missing by mistake or by design.”

***

The contractor's supervisor appeared in the middle of the morning.

He was a stocky man whose shirt collar was too tight, with an expression of outrage he seemed to have put on before leaving home.

“You can't halt dismantling indefinitely.”

“It's not indefinite,” Daren replied. “It lasts as long as the court order says it does.”

“Every day costs money.”

“File the appropriate claim.”

“The structure belongs to us.”

“And the scene of a possible crime is under the court's custody.”

The supervisor held out a folder.

“Here are the plans you requested.”

Daren examined them.

There was a general plan, a materials list, and a rental agreement. The chamber drawing showed the inspection platform, but not the lower space they had found.

“A modification is missing.”

“There were no modifications.”

“The expert has just measured a plate that doesn't match this plan.”

The man blinked.

“Perhaps they made an adjustment during assembly.”

“Who authorized it?”

“The technical supervisor.”

“I need his name.”

The supervisor searched the folder.

“Eron Bale.”

Daren wrote it down. It was not the Bren mentioned by the cleaner.

“Where is he?”

“Sick, I'm told.”

“Since when?”

“Yesterday.”

“Have you spoken to him personally?”

“No.”

Daren studied him.

The man seemed more irritated than nervous. He might be lying, or he might simply be discovering that a routine contract had become a judicial problem.

“I need his contact details and a list of every worker involved in assembling the chamber.”

“What for?”

“To identify who modified it.”

“Inspector, a company can't account for every screw that—”

“Yes, it can,” Daren interrupted. “If it rents out a machine to carry out a criminal sentence, it ought to be able to tell us who assembled it.”

The supervisor closed his mouth.

In the silence that followed, Daren realized he had asked a question no one had probably ever asked the man before.

***

Shortly after noon, Mara Ellin called.

“I'm reviewing the preservation records,” the doctor said.

“Did you find anything?”

“An anomaly I want to verify. The body shows signs of treatment intended to delay certain changes that normally follow death.”

“Is that common?”

“For long-distance transport, yes. For a freshly completed execution, it would make no sense.”

Daren leaned back against a column.

“Could there be a medical explanation?”

“Of course. That's why I need the full history. I don't want you repeating a conclusion in court that I haven't signed off on.”

“I won't.”

“You did yesterday.”

“I was tired yesterday.”

“You're tired today, too.”

The connection ended.

Daren put away the device and looked at the chamber.

A corpse preserved before the execution. An altered floor plate. A technical supervisor who had disappeared.

He still could not prove those pieces were related.

But they were beginning to form a sequence.

He walked to the experts’ temporary desk and opened his notebook.

On the first page, he had written three questions:

*Who went in?*

*Who came out?*

*Who certified it?*

He added a fourth:

*Who prepared the chamber?*

***

Odel called him inside at two ten.

The suspicious plate had been photographed, measured, and marked. They had removed the outer screws without disturbing the structural supports.

“We can lift it now,” Odel said.

Daren took his place beside one of the assistants.

The plate was heavier than expected. Underneath, a rectangular cavity appeared, containing a set of small wheels and steel rails. They were not part of the execution apparatus itself.

Odel shone a light into the space.

“This would allow a loading tray to move through here.”

“What would it normally be used for?”

“In a maintenance facility, moving heavy components in or out. But there aren't any parts here that would justify a mechanism like this.”

Daren bent closer.

The rails ended near a side opening, partially covered with boards.

It was not large enough for a person to walk through upright, but it was large enough to drag something through on a low platform.

A stab of unease went through the inspector.

“Could a body fit?”

“With a suitable tray, yes.”

“A living person?”

“That depends on the available clearance, their size, and whether they could cooperate. I'm not claiming anything more until we test the dimensions.”

Daren nodded.

He did not want enthusiasm for an explanation to destroy the value of the evidence.

The expert pointed to a dark spot on the metal.

“There's a recent friction mark here. And this wheel is in better condition than the others.”

“Was it replaced?”

“Possibly.”

“Can the system be operated from below?”

Odel checked a lever.

“Not as it stands. A transmission component is missing.”

Daren straightened.

“Could it have been removed afterward?”

“It's possible. Or it might never have been there.”

“We need the parts lists.”

“And the maintenance orders.”

The inspector looked toward the side opening.

He had spent two days searching for an extraordinary explanation for the wrong corpse.

What lay before him was a loading mechanism.

That was far more disturbing.

Loading mechanisms required operators, tools, and time.

Someone had to have prepared it.

***

The investigation received an unexpected visitor late that afternoon.

A man wearing an Arcane Custody uniform appeared with two agents. He presented valid credentials and an inspection order concerning potentially hazardous facilities.

“The Delegation requests access to the chamber,” he said.

Daren read the order.

It authorized inspection of arcane devices. It did not grant jurisdiction over the corpse or the judicial proceedings.

“You may inspect energy equipment with our experts present,” he replied. “You may not remove any components without court authorization.”

The agent looked at the opening in the floor.

“What have you found?”

“A mechanism absent from the approved plans.”

“It could be connected to an arcane alteration.”

“It could also be a mechanical mechanism.”

“We need to verify that.”

“Go ahead. But the evidence stays in municipal custody.”

The agent put away his credentials.

“You'll receive a formal communication from the Delegation.”

Daren held his gaze.

“I'll add it to the case file.”

For a moment, he thought the man would insist. Instead, the agent merely called over one of his technicians.

The inspection was brief.

They found no clear signs of an arcane device in the plate or the rails. They did identify residual energy in a small lighting panel, but the readings could not establish whether it had been used improperly.

Daren noted the observation.

He was not ruling out magic.

He was only beginning to understand that magic might have been used to distract attention from a physical operation.

***

When the square was empty, Daren returned to the courthouse carrying a box of copies.

A letter from the contractor was waiting for him. It answered his request for additional documentation.

The folder contained invoices, access permits, and a list of tools.

Daren checked the dates first.

Most were from the week before the execution.

One work order, however, had been issued during the preceding night.

Its description was brief:

**Lower access adjustment and platform calibration.**

The document had been signed by the absent technical supervisor.

Daren examined the authorization number.

It did not belong to the main contract.

It belonged to an extraordinary order that should have been approved by another department.

He searched for the name of the requester.

The box was blank.

An internal code appeared in its place.

Daren remembered his conversation with Kael. The archivist would probably have spent ten minutes explaining why a blank box did not, by itself, prove that a crime had occurred.

But this did prove that someone had authorized work the supervisor claimed not to know about.

Daren picked up his communications device and called the contracts office.

“I need to know who approved a modification to the execution chamber the night before the procedure.”

The clerk asked for the code.

He read it slowly.

Silence followed.

Then he heard papers being shuffled.

“Inspector, that code doesn't belong to the chamber contract.”

“What does it belong to?”

“A restricted maintenance allocation.”

“Which institution?”

The woman hesitated.

“I can't see the full entry from this office.”

“Give me what you have.”

Another silence.

“Only a cost center appears.”

Daren picked up his pen.

“Tell me.”

The clerk dictated a sequence of characters.

He wrote it in the margin of the invoice.

He did not know what it meant. It was not a municipal number.

After ending the call, he compared the code with the list of documents sent to the Central Archive.

The same characters appeared in the corner of one of the copies.

Daren felt profoundly tired.

Investigations were usually an attempt to bring scattered facts together until they formed an explanation. This one seemed to do the opposite: each new piece increased the number of offices, contracts, and people he needed to identify.

He put away the invoice.

Then he wrote a note addressed to Kael Veyran.

*I need you to verify which institution this cost center belongs to. Copy of the work order attached.*

He thought for a moment, then added:

*This time, the signature is missing too.*

***End of Episode 04.***
