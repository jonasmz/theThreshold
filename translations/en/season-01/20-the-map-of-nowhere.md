# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 20: The Map of Nowhere

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran and Lyra Saren, third-person limited, changing only between scenes.

***

The largest map in the Public Cartography Room had a mistake nobody had made.

Kael Veyran had been standing beside it for almost an hour, a copy of a historical index open on the reading table. He had checked the scale twice, the changes in the grid, and the margins of the charts predating the reform. The reference he was looking for still pointed nowhere.

It appeared neither in the streets of Vael-Tir nor along the routes of Kharven, nor in the provincial charts available for a fee. That was not strange: *fourteen, eight, three* had never been a modern territorial coordinate.

What was strange was a footnote in the School of Surveying's catalog.

*The second origin reference must be applied before projecting the route endpoint. Where that reference is missing, the local origin must not be substituted.*

Kael read it quietly.

He had found the warning several days before. He understood it as a general rule of older systems: two points could be measured relative to each other without being expressed in the same frame. Now the word *endpoint* seemed more important than the numbers.

A route had a beginning and an end.

A machine had assembly positions.

A navigation chart could describe both, and the catalog did not say which one had been lost.

Lina Ores, the reading-room assistant, approached with a folder tucked under one arm.

“I found the errata you requested,” she said. “It isn't a missing page. The second volume was printed without a concordance table that the first edition had promised to include.”

“Was it published later?”

“Not here. The catalog says it passed to another office. I don't know the conditions for consulting it.”

Kael checked the edition number and noted the omission. He did not ask Lina to search a restricted collection or use another employee's permissions.

“May I compare the two public maps on the tracing table?” he asked.

“Yes. As long as you don't mark the originals.”

She pointed to a broad surface beside the windows. The board still bore wax marks from old work, but its edges were level. Kael spread out a territorial chart and a measuring template used to teach apprentices how to correct orientation errors.

The template could rotate. The territory could not.

He tested the reference in three orientations. Each time, the resulting point fell somewhere different. One transformation placed it in the water; another, in an area where no station existed. The third produced an invalid value for the projection being used.

He recorded the results without crossing them out.

They did not demonstrate that the route ended outside Eryndor. They demonstrated that attempting a conversion without the second origin produced arbitrary answers.

A pair of students beside him were arguing about the course of a rural road. Kael heard them laugh when one placed the template backward. Their mistake was immediately obvious.

He wished he could turn his own sheet over and find an equally simple problem.

***

Lyra Saren found the door to Ema's warehouse tied shut with a red cord.

It was not a judicial seal. It was how carriers indicated that the parcel desk had closed for the day and that any new deliveries would have to wait until morning.

Ema sat on a bench, eating soup from a dented container.

“They've closed the old passage,” she said before Lyra could ask.

“Until when?”

“The Water Service hasn't set a reopening date. The slab at the bend needs another inspection.”

Lyra rested a hand on the edge of the bench.

Last time she had taken Kael along that public path, they had stopped before a closed technical door. She had offered to show him a route, not to use it. Its closure did not turn the path into a persecuted secret or demonstrate that someone wanted to keep them from passing. The water warden had already warned them that the ground was unsafe.

Even so, the closure took away one way of explaining what she knew without asking Kael to rely entirely on her word.

“I'm not going looking for another entrance,” she said.

Ema raised her spoon.

“I didn't ask.”

“I'm saying it so I don't talk myself into it later.”

“Good.”

Lyra sat beside her.

She had spent too long counting routes as though they were possessions. There were few she could still travel. There were many she knew only by hearsay. And some of those in her notes had ceased to be safe before she understood how badly they had deteriorated.

“What happened to Orven's paper?” Ema asked.

“Which one?”

“The one you took out of your coat the day the man from the Archive measured the plate. The one you wouldn't show him.”

Lyra stared at the soup.

It was a sheet folded into quarters, with gray ink and an oil stain along one edge. It did not belong to Orven. She had received it years earlier in a tools transaction, along with a notebook whose provenance was incomplete. The sheet was among her personal belongings and bore no institutional seal.

She had wanted to use it to find someone.

She had failed.

“I put it away,” she said.

“Do you still need it?”

“More than ever.”

“Then why won't you show it to him?”

Lyra took a moment to answer.

“Because some people can turn an incomplete notation into an order to open something that ought to stay closed.”

Ema set down her spoon.

“Kael couldn't even open his own office after they took away his permissions.”

“I'm not just talking about him.”

“Then explain the risk. If you don't trust what he'll do once he understands it, don't ask him to keep walking with you.”

The words reminded Lyra of the argument over the medical shipment, when she had decided for its recipients how much danger they could accept. She had ended up paying for an alternative route and discovered that the hardest part was not the money. It was admitting that other people could reject her decisions.

“There's a difference,” she said. “A shipment can wait in a warehouse. Certain kinds of questions can't be put away again once they're understood.”

Ema tilted her head.

“That doesn't make you the only one who has the right to ask.”

Lyra looked toward the dock entrance.

She had not forgotten what an Anchor could do or the requirements that made a connection safe. What she could not completely remember was a period of her own history around an interrupted test. That gap did not erase her previous experience or give her license to invent what was missing.

She took the sheet from an inside pocket of her coat.

She did not hand it to Ema.

“I'm going to explain it to him,” she said. “But I won't offer him a door I don't know how to keep open.”

***

Kael returned home with graphite staining his hands.

His disciplinary summons lay on the table. Dalia Ferren had confirmed that the preservation order covering the reproductions remained in force, but the proceedings against him would continue. Nothing he found in a public atlas would absolve him of having registered a copy under his responsibility too late.

He separated his papers into two groups.

On one side he placed the records from his own case, which he was entitled to retain as a party to the proceedings. On the other were transcriptions and freely available reproductions bought with his own money. The two categories should not be mixed, even if his questions ultimately became connected.

One sheet came from an old transport chart Orven Dal had allowed him to examine in his presence. Another was a reproduction of the School of Surveying concordance. The third held the measurements of a plate Lyra had kept in her own hands rather than surrendering it to him.

Three sources.

One sequence.

No complete proof of origin.

Kael drew two circles on the paper. In one he wrote *departure reference*. In the other, *arrival reference*. He joined them with a dashed line, because he could not yet draw a route.

Some measurement systems recorded a moving object's position relative to a fixed frame. Others used two active references, like a landing stage and a barge. In either case, the resulting numbers could mean nothing outside the procedure that produced them.

If the old index described a route requiring two frames, the important question was not where the missing point lay. It was what object defined the second frame.

He forced himself to list alternatives.

It could be a workshop standard that no longer existed.

It could be a station dismantled without a reconstruction plan.

It could be a navigational convention the catalog had misclassified.

Or it could be something for which he did not yet have a useful name.

He rose to make tea. On the lower shelf he found the chipped plate he had used during the storm, its rim still stained with orange juice. He washed it while the water heated.

A difficult question had no right to occupy every room in a house.

When he returned, someone knocked twice.

Lyra was waiting on the landing.

She was not carrying the brass plate.

She had a thin folder and an expression that reminded Kael of workers arriving at the Archive knowing the document they were about to request might change their lives.

“I need a table,” she said.

***

Lyra had waited until she reached the house to decide how much of the sheet she would show him.

She did not want Kael to see a document arranged like an irrefutable demonstration. It was nothing of the sort. It was incomplete, and one of its handwritten notes might have been made by someone who had also copied an older standard.

Kael offered her a chair.

“Is it yours?” he asked when he saw the folder.

“It's been among my belongings for years. I can't reconstruct every owner before me.”

“Then I won't call it a certified original.”

“I wouldn't dream of asking you to.”

She unfolded the sheet on the table. It showed two boxes joined by parallel lines, not a map with coastlines or mountains. In the margin was a family of calibration signs Lyra recognized from her work with old equipment. Several fields were incomplete.

Kael did not reach for it.

“May I see it more clearly?”

Lyra held the sheet by its edge and turned it toward the light.

“You can read it. I'm not leaving you a copy today. First I want you to understand what it's supposed to represent.”

The archivist studied the first box.

“A fixed origin.”

“A departure reference.”

“The second has the same structure.”

“An arrival reference.”

“Two installations?”

“Two ends of an operation.”

Kael reached for his notebook.

“What kind of operation?”

Lyra looked down at the page.

The full answer would change her relationship with him, and not for sentimental reasons. She had worked with people who regarded an old route as commercial property, a threat to their jurisdiction, or a chance to recover lost technology. If Kael spoke before verifying anything, he could draw people toward an unusable mechanism who would believe they could force it to work.

“An operation that needs more than a plan,” she said. “There has to be a place through which passage is possible, an installation that keeps its boundaries stable, and a verified reference for the far end. Without all of that, the drawing is no use for taking anyone anywhere.”

Kael set down his pen.

“That describes a cargo transit system.”

“It describes necessary conditions. It doesn't say this sheet is enough to meet them.”

Lyra indicated a column.

“There's an orientation reference here. It isn't an urban coordinate. Here's the type of support. And this space is meant to record destination verification.”

It was blank.

Kael saw it.

“Why is it blank?”

“Because whoever used this form never left a complete verification. Or because the part containing it was lost.”

“Do you know which?”

“No.”

The silence stretched.

Lyra expected frustration to drive him to look for some way to use the document regardless. Instead, Kael picked up a fresh sheet.

“We can record what it contains and what's missing,” he said. “Without pretending the empty field tells us something.”

Lyra realized she had been expecting a worse response.

***

Kael placed the old sheet to one side and his public transcription on the other.

The figures in the manual appeared under *alignment reference*. In Lyra's document, an equivalent field belonged to a two-ended table. They were not identical documents. Their graphic conventions differed, and their dates could not be compared until the dating system of the second document had been identified.

“Would you like me to read this field?” he asked.

“Yes.”

Kael read the family of units, followed by the warning about adjusting the origin. On another line he recognized the three strokes around an open dot that he had seen on the brass plate.

“That mark isn't an address,” he said.

“Not by itself.”

“It could designate a class of calibrator.”

“It could.”

Kael looked at her.

“That isn't a correction.”

“I'm not trying to wear you down until you believe me.”

The archivist returned to the table.

“Then let's run a check that can fail.”

He drew a landing stage and two mooring points. He used sample figures taken from no actual document. He showed how a route between two points in the same city could be described with one local reference and another relative to a vessel.

Then he erased the second origin point.

The line no longer indicated a unique place.

“So far, all we've needed is geometry,” he said. “If I ignore the second origin in your document and use Vael-Tir's, I get a false position. That's already happened to me.”

Lyra nodded.

“Most of the most dangerous mistakes begin with a conversion that seems reasonable.”

“But we still have to demonstrate why the true position wouldn't be in Eryndor. It might be somewhere in another region our atlases don't record.”

Lyra met his gaze.

“That's the part I haven't been able to verify from the material I've shown you.”

“Then we can't conclude it's outside this world.”

“Not by calculation.”

It was a hard distinction to make without sounding as though she were retreating. Lyra knew more than she could demonstrate at that table. Kael seemed to sense that while refusing to guess what he ought to believe.

“What else is on the sheet?” he asked.

“A series of checks I can no longer attribute to a particular operator. And a description of a celestial reference that isn't expressed like the ordinary almanacs.”

Kael leaned forward.

“Celestial?”

“For certain routes, the departure orientation was checked against observations at the other end. It wasn't enough to use the sky above the city where the mechanism stood.”

He went still.

“Why not?”

Lyra did not answer at once.

Outside the window, the last tram of the afternoon rattled past, making the glass tremble. The sound was familiar and safe. She felt an absurd impulse to close the folder and leave, as though she could return to the earlier conversation and leave its most dangerous part behind.

“Because they don't always describe the same sky,” she said.

Kael looked back at the sheet.

“That could mean different periods, different latitudes, or a mistaken observation.”

“I know.”

“It could also mean the word *sky* isn't being used literally.”

“That too.”

Kael lifted his eyes.

“What aren't you telling me?”

***

Lyra drew her hand away from the folder.

She had reached this point through a choice of her own. No interrogation or commercial debt had driven her here. She had watched Kael ask permission even when he badly needed an answer, admit an omission that might cost him his career, and stop before a public door because he didn't know what lay behind it.

That did not mean he was right about everything.

It meant he was capable of choosing.

“I showed you a path to a maintenance platform,” she said. “Not an operational installation. I did it because I needed to know you wouldn't confuse finding a door with having the right to open it.”

“I remember.”

“And I needed to remember that I can tell you something without forcing you to do anything with it.”

Kael did not interrupt.

Lyra breathed slowly.

“The old Transit systems dealt with more than distances. They sought natural connections and stabilized them with Anchors. They weren't corridors anyone could build wherever they wanted. Without a connection, a map was useless.”

Kael had encountered historical references to the term, though many public manuals used it for energy transport or precision maneuvers. He waited.

“Have you seen one operating?” he asked.

“I've worked near installations of that kind, and I know their controls. I can't demonstrate every journey in my notes to you here. There are periods of my life I can't reconstruct without documents I no longer have.”

“Is there an earlier record that could reconstruct that time?” Kael asked.

Lyra looked at the sheet. Until now, she had spoken to him of calibrators and routes, never of the search that had brought her to the harbor.

“I'm looking for an Imprint that might have recorded part of that operation,” she said. “Not a machine that will give me my memories back intact.”

“And what do you know for certain?”

It was a fair question.

“I know there are routes whose arrival references belong to no map of Eryndor. I know the operators didn't treat them as errors. And I know not every attempt to use them ended well.”

“That's what the records say.”

“That, and what I've been able to verify through my work. I won't confuse the two.”

Lyra felt the weight of a scene she remembered only in fragments: the smell of overheated metal, someone ordering a test to stop, the delay between the order and the movement of a hand. She had seen the physical medium that might preserve an Imprint of the procedure. She had not recovered what came afterward. She would not fill that absence with a convenient revelation.

“And the destination?” Kael asked.

“I don't have a complete coordinate we can use safely. Or the energy reserve to attempt it. And the only path I've shown you is closed for ordinary repairs.”

“Then none of this lets us travel anywhere.”

“No.”

“Or prove anyone traveled.”

“Not with this sheet.”

Lyra carefully smoothed a crease worn into the paper over the years.

“There's something else I want to tell you, Kael. But I don't want you to turn it into an explanation for the execution, the companies, or your suspension. I don't know how they're connected, or even whether they're connected at all.”

“I'm listening.”

“What some old indexes call regions aren't always regions of this world.”

Kael did not move.

“What do you mean by *this world*?”

Lyra held his gaze.

“That Eryndor isn't all that exists.”

The words settled between them, smaller than she had imagined and harder to take back.

“There are other Domains,” she said. “Other worlds. Some connections lead to them.”

***

Kael had been expecting an extraordinary answer.

That did not mean he was ready to hear it in a kitchen where the tea water had already gone cold.

He looked at Lyra's sheet, the public notebook, and the graphite mark left along the edge of the table. Everything was exactly what it had been a moment earlier.

A statement could change the meaning of documents without changing the documents themselves.

“You know this,” he said at last. “Or you believe you know it?”

Lyra did not seem offended.

“I know it to the degree of certainty I've achieved through working with those references. I can't ask you to share that certainty on my word alone.”

“Do you know somewhere I can go now to verify it?”

“Nowhere safe. A connection has to be active, an Anchor stabilized, a destination verified, and sufficient energy available. Even if we had permission, I wouldn't activate a deteriorated installation just to settle an argument.”

“And if the connection existed without an Anchor?”

“The natural Threshold would exist. That wouldn't give a person a safe passage.”

Kael ran a hand over his face.

The idea was immense. It was also, for now, a hypothesis supported by one person, incomplete documents, and a technical tradition whose provenance he could not reconstruct.

There could be fraud, error, an ancient metaphor turned into a procedure, or a classification system he did not yet understand.

It was more useful to admit those alternatives than to pretend he had ruled them out.

“I need independent evidence,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Something that doesn't depend on your being there to explain it.”

“Yes.”

“And if it exists, I need to be able to repeat the reasoning without a Council credential.”

Lyra gave the faintest smile.

“That's why I came to your home with a sheet of paper, rather than asking you to enter a locked room.”

Kael looked again at the celestial-orientation field.

“This description might help. If an observation is recorded in sufficient detail, I can test whether it matches the astronomical records we know. Even if your document is false, the comparison should yield an explainable result.”

“Provided the reading is faithful,” Lyra said.

“And provided we know what period it represents.”

“We don't.”

“Then we'll have to narrow down the possibilities.”

Lyra ran a finger along a note.

“There's an observation interval here. It isn't dated according to our present calendar. Converting it requires a table I didn't bring.”

“Where is it?”

“I have a partial working copy. It's no use for certifying anything. I'll look for it and tell you which part you can compare.”

Kael was grateful that she did not produce a perfect match at the very moment one was needed.

“I don't want evidence prepared just for me,” he said.

“Then investigate it yourself, too.”

The archivist wrote down three questions: what temporal system the sheet used, what observational conditions it described, and whether Eryndor's public catalogs could reproduce them.

He did not write *other worlds* as a conclusion.

He wrote it in the margin, beneath the heading *Lyra's statement; verification pending*.

***

Night found Lyra at Pier Three.

Ema was closing a toolbox for the first delivery of the morning. She looked up when Lyra arrived.

“Did you speak to him?”

“Yes.”

“Did you show him the sheet?”

“Enough of it to let him ask questions.”

Ema stopped working.

“Did you tell him why you had it?”

“Not everything. There are things I can't prove, and others that belong to people who never gave me permission to name them.”

“And did that put your mind at ease?”

“No.”

Ema sat on the edge of the table.

“Then it must have been an important conversation.”

Lyra laughed briefly.

The girl handed her a cup of broth.

“Will he come looking for you tomorrow?”

“Maybe. But I told him I wouldn't ask him to activate anything or enter any sealed installation.”

“Did he believe you?”

“He made me promise it more precisely.”

“He learned from you.”

Lyra held the cup between her hands.

She had shared a truth that for years had marked the boundary between people she used and people she was willing to answer to. She had not lost a contact or revealed a name. She still had the choice of withdrawing her help before anyone was put in danger.

But she could no longer hide behind the notion that Kael was investigating an ordinary problem of cartography and that she merely knew shortcuts.

Now he could choose whether to continue, knowing what question he was actually asking.

“If he asks you to take him to the other side, what will you do?” Ema asked.

“Tell him I don't know whether a safe crossing exists today.”

“And if he insists?”

Lyra looked into the darkness of the dock.

“I'll explain what attempting it costs. Then we'll have to decide whether anything justifies that cost.”

Ema nodded.

“That sounds less like an impossible promise.”

“I'm practicing.”

***

Kael did not reopen the folder concerning his suspension that night.

He needed to separate two problems if he was not to make the mistake he had spent his career correcting in other people's files. One was administrative: copies were under custody, a hearing was pending, and he still had to answer for an omission. The other lay on his table: a system of references he could not explain and a person who claimed to know how they were used.

The second question did not cancel the first.

He asked the neighborhood bookshop to obtain a public catalog of celestial observations. The clerk explained that it could be requested from the School of Navigation, where students compared old charts with tables compiled after the reform.

“When do you need it?” she asked.

“Tomorrow, if it's available.”

“That's not up to me.”

“I know.”

He paid for the request and kept the receipt.

At home, he placed his transcription from the Surveying catalog beside his list of questions. The three-stroke mark appeared in several sources; none told him, by itself, what lay at the other end. Lyra's sheet offered a structure for interpreting it, but not yet a certified provenance.

The conversation had made it possible to formulate a different kind of test.

If the description of the sky referred to a period in Eryndor, there ought to be some way to compare it with preserved observations. If it corresponded to none of them, there would still be other hypotheses to eliminate: copying errors, orientation conventions, incompatible dates, instruments calibrated differently.

He could not skip those questions because he wanted the answer to be extraordinary.

Kael thought of Lyra saying *other worlds* without asking him to believe her. It was the most daring thing he had seen her do: not revealing a password or an address, but relinquishing control over the conclusion he might reach.

He took a fresh sheet.

At the top he wrote:

*First: establish what observation the document claims to describe.*

Below it, he left space for the independent comparison.

For a moment, he wished he already knew how it would all end. Then he remembered he was not trying to predict the future. He was trying to read evidence without distorting it.

The city kept making noise outside the window: wheels over wet stone, the voices of the last passengers, the metallic rattle of a gutter.

Lyra's claim had not turned the numbers into a map.

It had turned the absence of a destination into a question he finally knew how to begin testing.

***End of Episode 20.***
