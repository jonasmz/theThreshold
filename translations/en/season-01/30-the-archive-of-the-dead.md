# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 30: The Archive of the Dead

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, third-person limited.

***

The newspaper had printed the correction on its front page.

Kael Veyran read the lines standing beside the tram stop's newsstand. The public version of the court ruling did not say where Taren Oss was. Nor did it claim he had survived everything that happened after the ceremony. It said something smaller and more devastating: the body displayed as the result of his execution could not be certified as his, and the investigation had to continue.

A man behind Kael asked for the next copy.

“Then who did we see die?” he asked the vendor.

“That's what they don't know.”

“If they've printed it, they ought to know.”

Kael paid for the newspaper and stepped aside to let a woman with two children pass. He had heard that demand many times inside the Archive: a sealed document seemed obliged to explain more than its author could substantiate.

The edges of the square had reopened to ordinary traffic. Judicial restrictions remained at its center, and a barrier directed pedestrians away from the drainage works. The city had found a way to keep functioning without answering every question about what had happened beneath its streets.

Kael sat on a bench.

Below the main article was another investigation by Inara Vel into supply payments and discrepancies between receipt and invoicing. The company named had replied in writing. The article distinguished Audit's findings from responsibility still under investigation. It was a way of writing that forced the reader to endure uncertainty.

Kael was surprised to feel grateful for it.

In the newspaper's margin was a municipal notice: pumping shifts would remain under reduced operating conditions for another week, and Services offices would publish changes every morning. The western node remained stable only within limits the city had to keep paying to maintain.

He closed the paper.

He had seen a sky that did not belong to Eryndor. But none of those people needed to know about his journey to demand water, wages, and an explanation for an unnamed corpse.

The world had grown larger.

His obligations had not grown smaller.

***

Dalia Ferren had the disciplinary ruling on her desk.

It had arrived that morning, with the service receipt still tied to it by string. Kael sat before she invited him. His lawyer knew the habit: when he saw a folder containing an answer he had awaited for weeks, he preferred not to give his body the option of turning and leaving.

“There are two separate decisions,” Dalia said. “One concerns your omission. The other concerns what management later tried to attribute to you.”

Kael read the first.

The reviewing body confirmed that he had registered two reproductions late and that the failure must remain on record. Time spent under precautionary suspension would count toward the administrative sanction, without imposing a second removal from duties for the same act. There was no retrospective exoneration: the omission had occurred.

The second decision dismissed, for lack of sufficient support, the additional allegation that he had removed the two reproductions from the Archive. Tessa Ord's records, the corrected inventories, and verifiable seals established that the items had remained in institutional custody.

“That doesn't mean everything you did was correct,” Dalia clarified.

“The ruling doesn't say it does.”

“Good. I wanted to stop you celebrating as though they'd erased the first line.”

Kael read on.

The Archive was required to keep the two identified items under verifiable control until the competent body decided the earlier attempted transfer. An external review was ordered of the procedure permitting movement orders without a defined physical recipient. Kael could request reinstatement to ordinary duties, but access to restricted collections would remain subject to clearance and justified need. No badge would be automatically restored.

“Is that enough?” Dalia asked.

“To establish I didn't remove the documents, yes.”

“That wasn't all I asked.”

Kael thought of Tessa, obliged to sign that she had seen a sealed box and uphold her testimony as the case grew. He thought of offices willing to move records without naming who would receive them.

“I don't want a key back only to use it exactly as before.”

Dalia rested a finger on the ruling.

“That's a professional decision. Yours. You can return to work and keep questioning procedures, but you'll need to separate your position from the resources you submit as a citizen.”

“I know.”

“Not yet.”

Kael looked up.

His lawyer smiled wearily.

“You'll know when another form offers a way to save yourself three weeks of paperwork.”

***

Returning to the Central Archive felt like visiting a house that still remembered the shape of his footsteps.

Kael presented his authorization for ordinary access at the counter, signed the visitors' book, and waited for an employee to escort him to Personnel. He did not try his old blue card. He had surrendered it when his suspension began.

Tessa Ord waited by a window.

She carried neither the sealed box nor the reproductions whose preservation she had confirmed. She held a folder containing copies Personnel could show the data subject: entry records, appointment history, and communications concerning the disciplinary proceedings.

“They let you in,” she said.

“Through the door named in the ruling.”

“That usually helps.”

They embraced briefly, with the care of two people who did not yet know whether they could call it a return.

Tessa drew away first.

“The box is still where it belongs,” she said. “I can't discuss the collection, but I received acknowledgment of a seal inspection. The transfer review isn't finished.”

“That's enough for me for now.”

“That doesn't sound like you.”

Kael looked at the folders.

“I'm learning some new sentences.”

The Personnel officer arrived with the authorized copy of his employment history. Kael had requested it during the appeal to check which entries the suspension had left and which needed correction.

The first pages were straightforward. Employment start date, performance evaluations, training records. A field certifying his identity data when he joined the service referred to the civil birth certificate he had supplied years earlier.

Then another line appeared.

*External provenance verification — linked reference pending comparison.*

There was no insertion date or legible identification of the issuing body. The mark did not appear in his work history. It appeared in the provenance index Personnel used to explain where certain information in the file originated.

“What's this?” Kael asked.

The officer bent over the page.

“I can't interpret it. It's a reference inherited from the previous system. When the files were migrated, links were retained even if the documents themselves weren't transferred.”

“Does it link information about me?”

“It's on your record, but that doesn't establish that the external document describes you. It could be a verification standard, an appointments list, or a collective reference.”

Kael remembered his earliest investigations.

A unit might preserve a name after its function changed. A field could point to an office rather than the person whose file displayed it. He could not turn an inherited reference into an accusation simply because it appeared near his personal details.

“I want to request the origin of the link,” he said.

The officer produced another form.

“You can request a provenance comparison as the data subject. It won't grant automatic access to the external collection, but an independent review must tell you whether the reference exists, what kind of record it is, and what minimum information can be released.”

Kael read the conditions.

He needed no special badge to file the request.

He needed his name, signature, and the willingness to await an answer that might be incomplete.

***

Daren Holt was alone in the café when Kael found him by the window.

He had an empty cup and a newspaper folded in half. Seeing Kael, he pushed out a chair with his foot.

“I don't know whether to congratulate you on the Archive ruling or ask what problem you've found now.”

“I haven't come to report anything.”

“Then sit before you change your mind.”

Kael sat. They had spent months comparing records, but never been so careful about what they could not share.

“I read the court's public ruling,” Kael said. “I don't need the protected proceedings.”

“And I don't need Archive documents that are still restricted. Perhaps we've learned something.”

“That we can't solve everything over the telephone.”

Daren laughed briefly.

A cart passed outside. Its load struck the wood twice, and the driver called to his assistant to tighten a strap. Kael watched without ceasing to listen.

“Do you consider the investigation finished?” he asked.

“One part has an answer I can defend before a judge. Other questions remain open. People deserve to know what happened, even when the answer doesn't suit any institution.”

Kael examined the front page.

“I used to think finding the correct document ended the argument.”

“I thought finding a door was enough to know where it led.”

They fell silent.

Daren ordered another cup and paid for both before Kael could offer.

“Are you going back to the Archive?”

“Maybe. But I want to do it differently.”

“Then don't let anyone convince you a correction is the same as erasing the mistake.”

Kael nodded.

When they parted, Daren clasped his forearm. They made no promises to exchange case files or solve the remaining questions in a week.

As Kael walked away, he thought it was the most truthful form of friendship they had found.

***

The queue outside the Municipal Services Directorate reached the corner.

Kael had come to collect a public supply timetable to help Ema reorganize a delivery to the Highbank shelter. It had nothing to do with his new request. That was precisely why he had chosen to do it: not every task needed to lead to a mysterious file.

A woman held a pump component wrapped in cloth.

“They told me the replacement will come by road,” she was saying to the clerk. “Who's responsible if it's delayed?”

The clerk pointed to a timetable.

“That's the expected date. If it doesn't arrive, you can register a complaint and ask for service priority. I can't promise the vehicle.”

Kael looked at the woman's hands. The component left a dark stain on the cloth, and her fingers were worn from adjusting tools.

A man behind Kael complained nobody had given timely warning about the suspension of a supply route.

“They said they'd prevented an accident,” another replied. “Apparently the price is working half the time.”

Kael wanted to intervene. He knew containment could prevent a rupture without immediately restoring the previous operating level. He had seen an installation subjected to limits even its operators refused to disregard. But he had no direct knowledge of this sector's energy plan and had not come to defend its administrators.

He waited his turn.

The clerk gave him a list of conventional transport windows and freight priorities. It contained no classified routes, power figures, or Anchor locations. It was enough for him to help Ema decide which day to commit to a shipment.

Outside, he saw Neris Daal speaking to two carrier representatives. He had read her name in Civic Council notices but did not know her personally. They were discussing a fuel invoice and the cost of maintaining guards over a longer journey.

Neris asked that the amount be entered in the monitoring report and the answer made available to those affected, not only the agreement's signatories.

Kael continued walking.

An official's decisions could stay within the law and still exact a price others had not chosen.

The city did not need all the Network's secrets to demand that someone keep that account.

***

Ema accepted the timetable with a spoon in her hand.

She was finishing soup at Pier Three and did not set it aside while reading the dates. She compared two road shifts, rejected one because of the expected bridge delay, and marked the other in pencil.

“It's public information,” Kael said.

“That's why I can show it to the shelter. A route I have to conceal from the recipient is no use to me.”

She pointed to a chair.

Kael sat opposite her.

“Lyra's in the storeroom,” Ema added. “She still hasn't accepted the next delivery. She's counting how many days she can work without overstepping her medical review.”

“That seems sensible.”

“A month ago you wouldn't have said it that way.”

Kael took the cup of water Ema offered.

“A month ago I'd have thought every delay was a decision someone could correct with the right signature.”

Ema watched him for a moment.

“Some delays are. Others come from a broken wheel. What matters is knowing which one you've got.”

Lyra appeared in the doorway with an accounts sheet and rolled-up sleeves.

She had met Sena Vard after the technical hearing. Kael knew they had agreed to review obligations in writing rather than treat a promise Lyra could no longer remember making as an irrevocable command.

“Did you get the schedule?” Lyra asked.

Kael handed it to her.

She studied the dates and pointed out a shipment that could leave with a smaller load without displacing priority supplies. Ema made a note.

For several minutes they talked about boxes, payments, and recipients, not another sky or the northern hillside station.

Kael was surprised by the relief he felt.

When they finished, Lyra walked him into the courtyard.

“How did things go with the Archive?” she asked.

“They notified me of a decision. I can apply to return to ordinary duties. There's also a correction pending in the provenance of my personal data.”

“Will you?”

“First I'll ask them to explain a link in my employment history.”

Lyra did not ask which collection he hoped to open.

“If you need someone to accompany you to a counter, I can,” she said.

“Thank you. For now I need to wait for a reply.”

She rested her elbows on a railing.

“I used to hate that phrase.”

“And now?”

“I still hate it. But at least I've learned that waiting isn't the same as letting someone take the evidence away.”

Kael smiled.

They had crossed to another world and returned with losses nobody could replace. It was strange that one of their most useful achievements was knowing when to remain still.

***

Dalia advised him to keep two separate folders.

The first contained his employment defense, the ruling, and the custody receipts for the reproductions. The second held his personal request concerning data provenance, submitted to an independent body entitled to ask the Archive and other offices to identify the inherited reference.

“They mustn't travel together,” she said. “The first concerns your conduct as an employee. The second asks which institution used a source to record your information. If someone tries to answer both with one refusal, we'll demand that they identify their jurisdiction.”

Kael placed the documents in their respective folders.

“What kind of answer might I receive?”

“Certification that the entry exists or doesn't, its basic classification, the authority holding it, and reasons for withholding any protected part. If the collection describes people, they must establish whether it concerns you before releasing third-party data. If they can't, they must say so.”

“And if they claim it doesn't exist?”

“Ask about the search method. Don't assume a denial is false merely because it troubles you.”

Kael touched the second folder's cover.

The reference was in his employment index, not a clandestine page found under a table. There was verifiable documentary provenance for his question. There were also ordinary alternatives: a verification manual, migration table, or old series with reused names.

“When this began, I thought official documents were safer because they were far from people with a stake in the result,” he said.

Dalia removed her glasses.

“And now?”

“I know someone decides which field appears, who may correct it, and which office receives a copy. A document being official means I need to check its provenance. Not believe it without asking.”

The lawyer closed the suspension folder.

“An expensive lesson to learn from a registration error.”

Kael accepted that.

He had made the error.

He had also learned that acknowledging it gave him fewer excuses, not fewer rights.

***

The reply took nine days.

During that interval Kael received two follow-up medical appointments and a Personnel notice about available ordinary posts. He accepted none yet. No prohibition held him back: he wanted to know whether he could return without becoming someone who ignored a doubtful field merely to keep a desk.

Dalia reminded him he did not have to wait for the provenance answer before working. He agreed and marked a deadline on his calendar. He would not live indefinitely on an investigation.

When the first letter arrived, he was cleaning his kitchen table.

The envelope contained no data from the external collection. It confirmed that the review office had found a link identifier retained during migration of his employment file but needed to compare it with a source that had changed departments twice. An inquiry to its current custodian was pending.

The letter did not say he was being monitored.

Nor did it say he wasn't.

Kael filed the response with its case number, date of receipt, and a copy for Dalia.

Then he opened the window.

Outside, rain struck a fabric awning and the metal frame of a shop. The sounds came in two rhythms. He recognized the combination from what he had written in his notebook before traveling to Selivar, and from the fragment of domestic Imprint he had listened to afterward.

He could not enter the childhood afternoon.

He still knew someone had taught him to listen.

The difference continued to hurt. He no longer tried to repair it by playing the cylinder over and over.

He sat and wrote a letter by hand to the woman who had raised him, with no intention of sending it.

He did not ask what secret she might have kept.

He told her he had lost the memory of an afternoon with her and was still capable of choosing the life that afternoon had helped shape.

Folding the paper, he felt a small embarrassment because he knew it proved nothing.

For the first time since his return, he did not need it to.

***

The second communication arrived by certified courier.

It came from a document-review office separate from the Archive. The delivery officer requested a signature and recorded the envelope's condition. Kael checked its exterior number before opening it.

Inside were a partial ruling and a metadata annex.

The provenance authority confirmed that the link in his employment history pointed to an index held by a continuity commission. Full consultation of that index was restricted. An independent reviewer had determined, however, that three fields could be released to the data subject because they did not describe the protected contents: the series title, general record type, and sorting key associated with the reference.

A warning appeared at the bottom:

*The presence of a reference in an employment history does not establish identity with any person or persons mentioned in the source collection. The sorting key may indicate an entry date, verification datum, or series descriptor. Authorized examination of the original entry is required to resolve correspondence.*

Kael read the warning before the annex.

Dalia had asked him to. Limitations were part of evidence, not obstacles erected by someone hiding an inconvenient truth.

The annex's first line said:

*Monitoring index. Restricted series.*

The second contained the title.

*MIRROR.*

Kael pronounced it silently.

He did not remember encountering it in public technical manuals. The word could be a title, an acronym, or a label some office gave a verification table. Nothing on the sheet, by itself, established a biological purpose, let alone identified the people concerned.

The third field was arranged in columns.

*Associated chronological key: day, month, and year of entry.*

Kael read the sequence.

He knew those figures.

He stood to find his civil birth certificate among his household papers. He refused to trust the sensation of familiarity. He had lived too long among dates that seemed to match until someone read them carefully.

He put the sheets side by side.

Day.

Month.

Year.

The match was exact.

Kael reread the warning. The office could not determine whether the key represented someone's birth, an enrollment, an examination, or an earlier register version. The Archive link had not been authenticated as personal correspondence either.

He could not bypass those reservations.

But he could not pretend the coincidence lacked significance.

***

Dalia arrived as the sky darkened.

Kael had asked her to examine the papers before he requested anything beyond his rights. He had not sent a copy by anonymous messenger or read the key aloud to third parties. He kept the documents safe and waited.

She checked the certification, seals, and warning.

“The office establishes that the series exists and your employment record retains a link to it,” she said. “It doesn't establish the contents of the original entry. The date may serve another purpose.”

“It matches my birth.”

“I can see that.”

Dalia put both sheets on the table.

“I won't offer you an answer I can't prove.”

Kael felt relief and frustration at once.

“What's the next procedure?”

“A personal-data comparison request. The reviewer can determine whether the key belongs to a particular person or identifies only the series. If it reveals other people's data, that must be protected. If the link should never have been retained in your file, it must be corrected with a trace of the change.”

“And if they refuse everything?”

“We'll demand a reasoned ruling. We won't steal the file or ask Tessa to risk her job over a question another authority must answer.”

Kael nodded.

He thought of the nameless body displayed in the square, the execution whose public certification had just been corrected, and the way a word could remain in an index for centuries without readers knowing what it had cost to write.

He did not know whether the series was connected to the judicial case.

He did not know whether the title concealed a medical procedure, an administrative inventory, or a catalog of people.

He knew his civil history appeared in other documents and that he had lived his childhood. An unknown index could not erase the woman who raised him or turn his decisions into someone else's acts.

“I want to request it,” he said.

Dalia took a blank sheet.

“Then write exactly what you're asking for.”

Kael put pen to paper.

He requested the scope of the key and the basis on which it appeared linked to his personal history. He did not claim the index described him. He did not request unrestricted access to third-party files. He demanded that any refusal bear a signature and identify the body to which he could appeal.

When he finished, he laid the form beside his birth certificate.

Both documents contained dates.

One recorded that he had been born.

The other did not yet reveal what had begun on that same day.

***

After Dalia left, Kael turned out the hall light.

He kept one lamp lit over the table. Beside the new folder lay the morning newspaper, his Selivar observation notebook, and the domestic Imprint cylinder he had not used again since his first listening.

From the street came voices debating the next water shift. Someone in the neighboring building closed a badly fitted window. The wooden impact reminded him of the incomplete recording he could still recite.

The lost afternoon did not return.

Kael let the sound die away.

He sat and arranged his table without mixing the documents. The disciplinary file went to the left. The visit and journey records, under his personal seal, went to the right. In the center he placed his birth certificate and the review office's limited notification.

They did not resemble a verdict.

They resembled two questions asked in different eras, kept together by someone who had never explained why they should be close.

He opened the notebook in which he recorded established facts.

He wrote that an independent office had certified the existence of a monitoring index with a chronological key. He wrote that the key matched his birth date. Beneath that, he recorded that there was no proof of identity, contents, purpose, or causal connection between them.

It was the hardest line to write.

For months he had waited for a document that would change everything.

Now it lay before him, and his duty was not to let it change more than it actually said.

He closed the notebook.

As he put the papers away, the index sheet slipped slightly out of the folder and remained visible beneath the lamp. The word on its second line was brief. The record number did not contain his name.

Only a date key.

The exact date printed on his birth certificate.

***End of Episode 30.***
