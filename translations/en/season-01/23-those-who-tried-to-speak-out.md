# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 23: Those Who Tried to Speak Out

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel and Kael Veyran, third-person limited, changing only between scenes.

***

The woman outside the newspaper office had not come to buy a copy.

Inara Vel watched her from across the street as the delivery boy loaded the last bundle of newspapers. She wore an old coat buttoned to the neck despite the heat and held a folded sheet without looking at the front page. Each time someone opened the newsroom door, she took a step back.

Inara recognized the hesitation.

She clearly did not want to appear in a story.

She crossed the street.

“Do you need to speak to someone at the paper?”

The woman looked her up and down.

“Did you write about the batches of material nobody could trace?”

“I wrote about a particular batch, and the answers we still hadn't received.”

“And before that, about the execution contractors?”

“Yes.”

The woman tucked the sheet into her coat.

“Then I don't want to go in there. Your editor might see me.”

Inara indicated the vendors' square, where benches stood in full public view.

“We can talk wherever you choose. First, I need to know whether you want to make an attributable statement or you're only looking for advice.”

“I want something already written down not to disappear.”

That was not permission to publish. Inara let her lead the way to a bench, away from the newspaper deliveries.

“My name is Vera Leth,” the woman said. “I kept the minutes for a petitioners' committee dealing with industrial treatments. It wasn't a government office. We were neighbors, workers, and two professionals who helped people file complaints.”

Inara did not recognize the name.

“What does that have to do with the contracts?”

“We submitted a petition before the ceremony. We asked for records of tests on human subjects to be preserved. They answered that we had to address another authority.”

Vera clenched the sheet between her hands.

“Afterward, someone on our committee decided a written request wasn't enough.”

Inara wanted to ask who.

She did not.

“Do you have proof that the petition was filed?”

“The entry number is public. What came afterward isn't.”

“What do you want from me?”

Vera looked toward the newspaper's door.

“I want you to come with me and deliver it somewhere they can't pretend it never existed. Not print it tomorrow.”

***

Kael Veyran's disciplinary hearing began with a question he had not expected.

“Why did you correct the copies register after the deadline rather than before it?”

The question came from an official who did not work for the Archive. In front of her lay the liability file, the provisional order preventing the movement of two reproductions, and a list of people summoned to testify. A Personnel representative sat beside her. Dalia Ferren sat with Kael, without yet touching his documents.

“Because I omitted the entry when I should have made it,” he answered. “I wanted to keep the reproductions under the incident procedure while checking a transfer order, but I should have registered their temporary retention immediately.”

The official waited.

“Do you acknowledge that as a breach of your duties?”

“Yes.”

“And do you consider your suspicions about a later order an excuse?”

“No. I consider them separate facts. The late entry must be reviewed under the applicable rules. Moving material under custody requires an identifiable recipient even if my own conduct was improper.”

Dalia gave the smallest nod.

Kael had practiced that answer. Saying it in front of the Archive's representative was far harder than saying it to his reflection at home.

Tessa Ord testified next.

She did not arrive with a theory about a conspiracy. She confirmed that she had witnessed the sealing of the two reproductions, that the corrected inventory recorded their location, and that she had not seen Kael remove them from storage. Asked whether the registration procedure had been correct from the beginning, she answered that it had not.

Kael felt an uncomfortable gratitude.

Tessa could have turned friendship into an exaggerated defense. Instead, she had spoken about what she witnessed.

The Personnel representative argued that an employee responsible for documentary integrity had to meet deadlines even under pressure. Dalia did not contradict her. She requested that any sanction be assessed according to demonstrated harm, the voluntary correction, and subsequent preservation under controlled measures.

“What ruling are you expecting today?” the official asked Kael.

He looked at the folder.

“One that distinguishes my own failure from any responsibility for transfer orders I didn't issue.”

The hearing ended without a final decision.

The precautionary suspension would remain until the reviewing body received the custody report, and the reproductions would remain traceable in storage. Kael did not get his badge back.

Outside, Dalia gave him a copy of the minutes.

“You said exactly what you could substantiate,” she remarked.

“It doesn't feel like a victory.”

“I didn't promise you one.”

Kael put the paper away.

He had imagined defending his evidence would be easier once he admitted his own mistakes. Instead, he now had to live with those mistakes in writing.

***

Inara met Vera in the entrance hall of a labor-assistance center.

She had company. Levan Oris, a volunteer lawyer for the petitioners' committee, was present and wanted to know what the journalist had offered his former colleague.

“Nothing,” Inara replied. “I don't yet know the contents of what she wants to submit.”

Levan looked at Vera.

“You told me you spoke with her.”

“I told you she understands the difference between an allegation and proof,” Vera replied.

She seemed more tired than that morning. She carried a minute book under one arm and an unfastened folder.

The assistance center occupied rooms in a former school. In one corridor, a man with a bandaged arm waited; in another, a woman tried to persuade a receptionist to accept a statement omitting her employer's name. Inara understood why Vera had chosen the place. An injury here was not necessarily news. It was a reason to ask for help.

Levan led them into a consultation room.

“Before we discuss documents, I need to clarify what kind of committee Vera belonged to,” he said. “It could neither inspect laboratories nor interrupt operations. It collected complaints from affected people and referred them through the appropriate channels.”

Vera took out a copy of the municipal submissions index. The page bore a date before the execution and a petition number.

*Request to preserve records concerning stabilization tests and persons subjected to assessment procedures.*

The heading named no secret technology or patients. Below it were a receipt acknowledgment and a referral to another competent authority.

“Who signed the petition?” Inara asked.

“Several committee members,” Levan answered. “Some names are public. Others were submitted confidentially because the signatories still depended on employers linked to the facilities.”

“Was the substance of the complaint ever addressed?”

“We were told the municipality didn't administer those tests,” Vera said. “The referral took so long we couldn't know whether the records were still intact.”

Inara read the date of the response.

It did not prove illicit experimentation on human subjects had occurred. It proved a group had made the allegation before the event that later captured the whole city's attention.

“What did the committee do after receiving the referral?”

Vera did not look at Levan.

“We discussed how to get an authority to examine the evidence. Some of us wanted to keep pressing through lawyers. Others believed the reports would always be sent back to the same offices.”

“And you?”

The woman folded her hands.

“I kept writing the minutes.”

The statement absolved her of nothing.

Inara waited.

“There was a meeting we didn't enter in the ordinary minute book,” Vera continued. “A separate document was prepared, listing responsibilities and conditions. I transcribed a copy because they told me it would have to be preserved if something went wrong.”

“What responsibilities?”

“I'm not explaining them from memory while the folder is still closed. If I get it wrong, I'll end up changing what the document said.”

Levan leaned toward Inara.

“Ms. Leth intends to submit material that predates the ceremony. Her decision does not authorize the newspaper to reproduce it, and it doesn't replace a statement before the competent authority.”

“I understand,” Inara replied.

In the corridor, someone dropped a box of instruments and several voices rose at once.

Vera flinched.

Inara could see this was not a confident informant certain of the value of her knowledge. She faced someone who had long dreaded opening that folder.

***

Kael went to the public petitions registry after the hearing.

He had not decided to connect his disciplinary case to any outside complaint. He wanted to verify a reference he had noticed in Inara's newspaper article: a municipal office had received older complaints about technical procedures, but the article did not identify which ones could be consulted without affecting private individuals.

The registry clerk showed him a public index of entries.

“You can consult dates, general subjects, and referral destinations,” she said. “Not personal attachments.”

“I don't need names or medical files.”

The clerk searched for *stabilization* and *personal safety*. Several petitions appeared: complaints about occupational exposure, machine noise, and a documentary-preservation request received before the public ceremony.

Kael stopped at the last one.

A petition could have been written by someone sincerely concerned or someone trying to construct an alibi. Its entry date could be verified, but did not establish the contents as true.

“Is the referral record preserved?” he asked.

“The outgoing entry, yes. Not the other administration's response. You'd have to request that from the recipient office under the applicable permissions.”

She printed an unrestricted extract.

Kael checked the seal, paid the fee, and left.

The petition mentioned people in testing. It named no subjects. Nor did it directly mention the execution investigation.

It was coincidence, not yet causation.

He sat on a courtyard bench to take notes.

The index showed that the complaint had been received before the ceremony and referred after an ordinary administrative interval. There was no missing signature or impossible movement. The irregularity, if there was one, might lie in the absence of a substantive response that the municipality could document.

Kael thought of the two copies still sealed inside the Archive.

He did not know whether they bore any relation to the petition. He could not consult them, and had no reason to violate the preservation order to find out.

He wrote a sentence in his notebook:

*An earlier complaint proves someone wanted to leave a record, not that they knew what would happen later.*

He left a space below it.

He did not know what would need to happen before he could complete the thought.

***

Vera took Inara to Seris Hal's office for a specific reason.

The document had passed through neither the newsroom nor a municipal desk. Before the execution, Vera and another person had deposited a sealed envelope at the private certification office, with instructions for preservation and opening at the request of the depositor or a competent authority.

Inara recognized the building. She had accompanied Belor Sen there to preserve his employment settlement. Recognition brought a stab of unease: Belor had left the city, and she still did not know why.

She did not ask about his deposit. Seris had no right to tell her anything more than she had already confirmed.

The certifying officer received them in a room with a table visible from the doorway. Levan remained as Vera's adviser.

“I've located the reference for your deposit,” Seris said. “Before showing you the envelope, we need to verify your identity and the conditions you signed.”

Vera presented her documents. The officer opened the original ledger and read the entry aloud: a date before the ceremony, the exterior description of an envelope holding several sheets, the depositor's signature, and a seal number. A receipt had been issued at the time. A second intake confirmation appeared in its daily closing record.

“Can you prove no one substituted the contents?” Inara asked.

Seris looked at her.

“I can establish the receipt sequence and that the envelope retains the closure marks recorded at the time. When we open it, we can describe any signs of tampering. I can't certify that the statements inside are true. Nor can I certify that the material wasn't prepared to deceive from the outset.”

Vera signed a request for a controlled opening and authorized Seris to draw up an inventory for possible judicial submission. She did not authorize a copy for the newspaper.

“Do you want Inara to remain?” Levan asked.

The woman hesitated.

“She can stay, but not as the recipient of the papers.”

Seris recorded the limits of Inara's presence.

Then she photographed the seal and cut it before the witnesses. She did not carefully peel it away to pretend nothing had changed: the record would state that the envelope was opened at the depositor's request, with the time recorded.

There were four sheets and a folded drawing.

The first summarized the earlier petition. The second listed attempts to submit documentation through ordinary channels. The third described a procedure for intervention in a ceremony already announced by the civil court. The fourth listed responsibilities, with several sets of initials and fields left blank.

Seris read only the headings necessary for the inventory.

Vera stared at the third sheet as though she had never seen it before.

“Did you write this?” Levan asked.

“I transcribed the version they agreed upon,” she said. “I objected to carrying it out. I didn't leave the meeting.”

Inara had no right to question her there about every sentence. This room was not a court, and a journalist's presence could not turn a custody opening into a forced interview.

When they were done, Seris prepared an inventory of pages, seals, visible signatures, condition of the paper, and original numbers. The sheets went into a new sealed envelope against a receipt. The photograph of the old seal was attached to the opening record.

Vera retained a copy of the acknowledgment.

“I want to take it to the prosecution service,” she said.

Levan replied:

“Then we'll request formal receipt and safeguards for your statement. We won't let anyone collect it from a desk without identifying who takes possession.”

Inara felt the air in the room change.

The document had survived the decision to put it away.

Now someone would have to answer for what it said.

***

The newsroom was crowded when Inara returned.

An advertising salesman was arguing with Joren Mal about space in the next edition. Two apprentices were arranging movable type. Fresh ink mingled with soup warming in the kitchen.

Joren noticed the closed folder Inara was carrying and put down his pen.

“Do you have a story?”

“I have a decision to make before writing it.”

“That's the sentence I hear from you more than any other.”

“This time it isn't a commercial provenance error. Someone has decided to submit documents about procedures that may have been criminal. She hasn't authorized me to publish their contents or her identity.”

Joren folded his arms.

“Then why tell me?”

“Because the submission may become visible once the authorities formally receive the document. I want us to know how to cover that without destroying its value.”

The editor looked toward the door.

“I can't build a front page on something you're forbidding me to see.”

“I'm not asking you to. We can investigate the public entries for older petitions. The debate over safeguards for complainants and which offices are responsible is news in its own right. The rest requires judicial documentation and consent.”

Joren took a deep breath.

“If another newspaper breaks it first, we'll look as though we've been asleep.”

“Or as though we had evidence and decided not to burn it.”

He gave a humorless laugh.

“Nobody pays me to look virtuous.”

“I'm not asking for virtue. I'm asking that we can defend what we print when someone takes it before a judge.”

Joren stood and walked to the planning board.

“You can prepare an article about the petitions and their administrative route. No patients' names, no descriptions of the folder I haven't seen, and no announcing a crime that is still under investigation.”

Inara nodded.

“That I can do.”

The editor watched her a moment longer.

“Is the person safe?”

Inara thought of Vera, of her hands when she saw the third sheet, and the doors of the labor-assistance center.

“I don't know what risks she's facing. She has legal advice and has chosen a channel for testifying. I'm not going to promise safety I can't control.”

Joren took a card and wrote down the allotted space.

“Then don't make things worse to win an edition.”

Inara returned to her desk.

It mattered more coming from someone else.

***

At the review office, Dalia Ferren asked Kael to stop sorting his papers by color.

“I have no intention of reading the hearing record for an eighth time,” she said. “I want you to sign the receipt and go eat something.”

Kael stopped.

“There's a public petition from before the ceremony that might relate to the questions of documentary integrity.”

“Did you obtain it through lawful access?”

“Yes. It's a freely available municipal extract.”

Dalia examined it briefly.

“And which act in your disciplinary proceedings does it concern?”

Kael opened his mouth.

He could find no answer that did not require three assumptions.

“None, yet,” he admitted.

“Then don't attach it to your defense to give it a significance it doesn't have.”

“It could be relevant to the public investigation.”

“In that case, use the channels appropriate to that investigation. You can submit public information as a citizen. You can't use my representation to simulate Archive authority.”

“That wasn't my intention.”

“I know. But you start by gathering issues on one table that you don't yet know how to separate.”

The lawyer showed him a copy of the provisional ruling.

“Your own custody history is documented. The originals and institutional reproductions remain where they belong. You have the right to challenge your suspension, not to solve all of Vael-Tir's problems from here.”

Kael studied the petition number.

Part of him longed to find one document that united every anomaly and restored the feeling that dates could explain everything. He had learned something different at the School of Navigation: a description might be incompatible with available records and still require other evidence before an origin could be assigned.

“May I submit the extract to the court's public filing desk as information obtained from a public source, identifying its provenance and making no claim about responsibility?” he asked.

Dalia nodded.

“Yes. But describe it as a notice, not testimony about events you didn't witness. And don't expect them to tell you about someone else's case just because you delivered a sheet.”

“I don't.”

Dalia raised an eyebrow.

“Now that I'd need to see certified.”

***

Vera's voluntary submission was not immediate.

The prosecution service required a written application identifying the person appearing, the confidentiality protection requested, and the relationship between the deposit and the case investigating the ceremony. Levan prepared the request. Seris issued a receipt-and-opening certification so the court could assess the packet's provenance without making the depositor hand it to an employee with no acknowledgment.

Inara stayed out of the legal drafting. She waited on a corridor bench beside a mother trying to persuade her son to stop tapping his shoes against the floor.

Vera came out of the assistance office with Levan.

“I can still change my mind,” she said.

“You can decide what voluntary statement you give,” the lawyer replied. “But once a court receives material, its treatment follows the rules of the case. You need to understand that before submitting it.”

Inara stood.

“I can't decide for you.”

Vera gave her a tired look.

“I know. That's why you asked me not to give you the papers.”

The three of them sat.

“There were people who genuinely wanted to report what was happening,” Vera said. “They'd seen injuries and records they couldn't recognize in the public reports. Some lost their jobs for insisting. Others kept getting paid and kept quiet.”

Inara listened.

“And there were people who wanted proof the courts couldn't simply send back to the same office,” Vera continued. “They thought that if the contradiction appeared in front of everybody, there would be a case someone independent would have to open.”

“Did you know how they intended to do that?” Inara asked.

“I saw a plan. I didn't attend every meeting. I don't know who carried out each part. But what was left in that envelope wasn't a general protest.”

Levan waited.

“Are you willing to say that to the prosecution service?”

Vera took a breath.

“Yes. And explain what I did to preserve it without submitting it earlier.”

It brought no relief.

Inara thought of the people harmed by the tests and the person whose body had ended up as part of someone else's ceremony. A movement trying to force institutions to look might have used other people as though they were components.

That was the moral danger nobody had written about in the reform pamphlets.

***

Kael found Inara in the courthouse's public corridor.

He had not gone looking for her. He recognized her from the byline beneath the municipal-supplies article and an earlier conversation about the difference between public records and restricted files. She stood a few steps from the filing counter, back straight, looking as though she had gone hours without lunch.

Kael held the municipal petition extract.

“Ms. Vel,” he said. “May I ask you something about a publicly accessible document?”

Inara looked at the sheet without touching it.

“That depends on the document.”

“An entry from before the execution requesting preservation of records about tests. It passed through the municipal petitions office. Are you familiar with it?”

“I know petitions of that kind exist,” she replied. “I can't confirm whether any of them are related to a source I've spoken to.”

Kael accepted the limitation.

“I don't want names. I want to know whether the outgoing entry proves the receiving administration agreed to investigate the facts.”

“No. It proves a referral. Not that anyone conducted an investigation.”

“That's what I thought.”

Inara studied him.

“Why are you interested?”

Kael looked at the form in his hand.

“Because institutions can preserve a flawless record of passing a sheet from one desk to another without ever answering the question it contained.”

Inara did not smile.

“That we could publish.”

He lifted the extract.

“I'm submitting it through the public channel, without attributing contents I haven't seen.”

“Do that. And don't request access to anything else in my name.”

“I won't.”

They parted.

Kael left the sheet at the filing desk with a note explaining only its provenance and possible relevance, without mentioning his disciplinary case or his conversation with Lyra.

He received a stamped receipt.

He did not ask which case would receive it or whether the magistrate would read it. He knew the receipt guaranteed neither.

Even so, he had taken an action he could defend.

***

Vera's statement began behind a closed door.

Inara waited outside. She could not hear the questions and had agreed not to try. Beside her, a gray-haired woman folded and unfolded a letter until the crease turned white.

More than an hour passed.

Levan came out first.

“The prosecution service received the custody certification and requested that the office preserve the packet pending an order for formal incorporation,” he said. “Vera has stated what she knows about its preparation and the limits of her involvement.”

“Will the court accept it?” Inara asked.

“It can incorporate it as documentary evidence for investigation and examine its authenticity. No one delivers a verdict in a single morning.”

Vera appeared behind him.

She looked exhausted. She was not carrying the folder; she had left it where Seris would keep it sealed until formal transfer. In her hand was a copy of her statement with sensitive information protected.

“They asked me why I waited,” she said.

Inara had no answer.

“I told them I was afraid. And that I thought if the plan stayed in storage, the people who proposed it couldn't deny writing it. Then I saw what happened and didn't want to admit how much I had known beforehand.”

“Did they believe you?”

“I don't know. I told them where to check the date and who was with me when I deposited the envelope. I didn't ask them to believe me out of pity.”

Inara felt an unexpected closeness to the words.

Levan put away the copies.

“We shouldn't discuss the document's contents in the corridor. Some steps require protecting third parties; others will have to be notified by law.”

Vera nodded.

“I want it understood that some of us tried to report things without hurting anyone. And others agreed to do something we should never have done.”

“That will have to be investigated,” Levan said. “The purpose of a complaint doesn't erase a crime.”

Inara accompanied them to the exit.

As Vera went down the steps, the journalist asked whether she wanted help finding transportation. The woman shook her head.

“Today I need to do just one thing,” she replied. “Go home with my own name.”

Inara watched her disappear among vendors and officials.

She did not know if they would speak again.

This time she did not try to follow.

***

The article Inara wrote that night did not mention the statement.

It was an investigation into unanswered petitions and the responsibilities of referral between agencies. It included three publicly accessible cases from different dates, requests for responses sent to the relevant offices, and authorized testimony from people who had tried to obtain information about working conditions.

It did not claim the Council had ordered a particular operation.

It did not identify Vera.

It published no drawings or instructions that would make dangerous installations easier to access.

Joren read the article and stared at its last page.

“There's no accusation we can put in the headline,” he said.

“There's a procedure that allowed complaints involving human beings to be filed away as jurisdictional problems,” Inara replied. “The question is who actually examined them.”

“And if the prosecution service produces something else tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow we'll print what we can establish tomorrow.”

The editor picked up his pen.

“The front page will still be about energy supply. This goes beside the editorial on labor protections.”

Inara agreed.

She had set out to write a story in which every document had an owner and every statement had a defined scope. That night, it meant leaving out the most important thing she had learned.

Not because it ceased to be true.

Because she had chosen a route that would allow it to be examined without turning it into merchandise.

***

Inara was going down the newsroom stairs when Levan's messenger caught up with her.

He carried an envelope addressed to her personally, without a court letterhead. The accompanying note explained that Vera had authorized sharing **one specific extract**, solely so Inara could understand the scope of what had been delivered to the prosecution service. It was not permission to publish, nor a copy of the judicial evidence. The original remained at Seris's office, available for formal transfer under signed record.

Inara returned to her desk.

She opened the envelope beneath the lamp and checked its provenance first. The extract carried the deposit identification, a reference to the controlled opening, Vera's signature, and Levan's statement concerning the transcription made from the inventoried envelope. It contained no operators' names or addresses.

It also included confirmation that the prosecution service had received the custody certification and the application to incorporate the evidence that same afternoon.

Inara did not mistake the copy in front of her for a forensic authentication. A sheet deposited before an event could contain a plan never carried out. Its authors' initials had to be verified. A certifying officer's record of the seal and date did not make everything written before the envelope was closed true.

Even so, the correspondence gave her a feeling she could hardly name.

The inventory described a drawing of the apparatus, a lower opening, and an allocation of responsibilities. The deposit record predated the ceremony. Daren, through his independent investigation, had obtained preservation orders for components examined only afterward.

Near the end of the extract was a sentence Vera had authorized Inara to see. It came from the third sheet, entitled *Intervention Sequence for Public Procedure*.

It was not about maintenance or a protest.

It anticipated that after the closed phase, the witnesses would be shown a bodily result different from the person who had entered. Its stated purpose was to trigger an independent review of identity and medical history.

Inara read the date on the deposit entry again.

She did not know who carried out the maneuver, or even whether the people who drafted those instructions were the ones who ultimately put them into effect. The court would have to compare the original with the apparatus and hear those who could identify signatures and orders.

But someone had written, before the execution, **a plan that anticipated exactly the contradiction that later forced an investigation into the death**.

Inara folded the extract and put it somewhere other than her articles folder.

That night, she had no new story she could publish.

She had documentary grounds to fear that the truth those people had wanted to expose had itself been turned into an operation against other people.

***End of Episode 23.***
