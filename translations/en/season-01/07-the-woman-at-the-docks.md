# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 07: The Woman at the Docks

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran and Lyra Saren, third-person limited in separate scenes.

***

“What person?” Kael asked.

The woman beneath the awning did not seem surprised by his response. She carried a parcel wrapped in waxed cloth, and water ran from her hood down to the collar of her coat.

“The name you're looking for,” she said. “The one in your papers.”

“I haven't shown you my papers.”

“Then someone else is asking questions on your behalf.”

Kael was tempted to take a step backward. He did not. Behind him was the door of a small restaurant; ahead, a wet street and a tram held up by a freight cart. If the woman had meant to frighten him, she had chosen an unconvincing location.

“I work in an archive,” he said. “I look for names that aren't where they ought to be. That doesn't mean I'm looking for people.”

“An important distinction.”

“It is.”

The woman glanced toward Orven Dal's shop.

“Did he show you an invoice?”

“I can't discuss other people's documents.”

“So he did.”

Kael remained silent.

She studied him with a patience unlike that of the officials he knew. She did not seem to be waiting for a confession. She seemed to be calculating how much she could say without giving anything of her own away.

“My name is Lyra,” she said at last.

“I heard.”

The woman raised an eyebrow.

“Then you're less discreet than you look.”

“I was standing here when the shopkeeper called you by name.”

“Good. At least we won't waste time pretending we didn't see each other.”

The tram started moving again. For a moment, the noise of its wheels drowned out their conversation.

“What do you want from me?” Kael asked.

“To know whether you're following a mark or the person who used it.”

“That depends on which mark.”

Lyra laughed briefly.

“Orven said you were difficult.”

“I don't know why he'd talk about me.”

“Because old papers worry him, especially what happens when the authorities become interested in them again.”

Kael eyed the parcel. The string around the cloth was too new for an old object.

“If you have information relevant to a judicial investigation, you can present it to the inspector in charge.”

“And if the information has nothing to do with a judicial investigation?”

“Then I don't see why we need to talk.”

Lyra pushed back her hood. She had a barely visible scar near her temple. Her expression did not change, but some of the lightness left her voice.

“Because perhaps that case file isn't the first one to contain the mark.”

Rain struck the canvas roof hard.

Kael did not ask where she had seen other files. He had asked too many questions of that kind today, and he was beginning to understand that she would answer only questions whose consequences she could control.

“I'm not giving you documents,” he said.

“I didn't ask for any.”

“And I won't accept an interpretation without verifiable provenance.”

“Then we have a problem.”

“Probably more than one.”

Lyra smiled again.

“Tomorrow, when the market opens, go to the weighing office at Pier Three. Ask for whoever is responsible for the rejected-lots register. Don't mention my name.”

“Why should I?”

“Because there you can check for yourself whether Orven is right that the mark was nothing more than a workshop instruction.”

“Will you be there?”

“That depends on what you find.”

Lyra walked away before he could answer. She crossed the street between two carts and disappeared down a stairway leading to the warehouses.

Kael waited until she was out of sight.

He had received an invitation, not evidence. A stranger might have noticed an official's interest in an old mark and decided to send him to a depot for reasons he did not understand.

He opened his notebook and wrote down the time, place, and exact words he could remember.

Beneath them he noted: *The informant has not demonstrated knowledge of the mark's provenance.*

Then he added another line.

*She has demonstrated that she knows I'm investigating it.*

***

Lyra had thought the archivist would be easier to steer.

She had been told about a man who believed every question had an answer written down somewhere. That was true, but the description left something out: he did not seem willing to accept just any piece of paper someone put in front of him.

That complicated her work.

As she descended the stairs, she held the parcel against her chest to protect it from the rain. It contained no relic. Only three ordinary brass components and a worn plate Orven had kept in a toolbox for years. None could open a door or perform an extraordinary procedure. Together, however, they could illustrate a distinction most traders had long forgotten.

At the lower pier she found a young woman beside a hand-operated winch.

“You took your time,” the girl said.

“The rain.”

“You're dry from the shoulders down.”

“Then the rain and my bad temper.”

The girl's name was Ema. She ran a parcel-handling table for transporters who preferred not to deal with the central offices. Her table had permits; some of her clients did not.

“Did you get Orven's things?”

Lyra put down the parcel.

Ema checked that the string had not been cut.

“How much do we owe him?”

“He put it on my account.”

“Your account isn't a reliable institution.”

“That's why it never goes bankrupt. Nobody knows how much I owe.”

Ema gave a short laugh and placed the parcel beneath the table.

Lyra looked at the departures board. Two vessels were delayed for lack of a tow. A shipment of regulators was waiting for inspection. Farther away, a group of dockworkers argued with a municipal official over a fee they had been charged twice.

The harbor kept functioning because of small concessions and people who remembered which doors could be opened without an argument. It was not a secret organization. It was a city trying to survive its own paperwork.

“Is tomorrow's trip still on?” Lyra asked.

Ema stopped smiling.

“The driver says yes. I don't believe him.”

“Why?”

“There are new checkpoints at the north exit. Not cargo checks. Identity checks.”

Lyra rested a hand on the edge of the table.

“Who are they looking for?”

“We don't know. They're demanding full names and recording anyone carrying medical luggage.”

Lyra thought of the people waiting at the other end of the route. One needed to receive a package that was already two days late. She had promised not to use a carrier without verified authorization, even if it delayed the delivery. The promise had seemed reasonable when she made it. Now it was beginning to cost her.

“Don't approve the trip until you've spoken to me,” she said.

“If we delay it again, the recipient will think we've abandoned them.”

Lyra looked at Ema's hands, red from the cold.

“Let them think what they want. I'd rather break a promise by a few hours than have a driver arrested with the package.”

“It's not just a matter of hours.”

She knew that. She did not need the girl to explain it.

“I'll find a safe route,” she said.

Ema did not answer.

Lyra regretted phrasing it as a certainty.

***

Kael arrived at Pier Three the following day with authorization to consult commercial records, not judicial ones.

He had asked Tessa to check the name of the weighing office and confirm its jurisdiction. It was a port authority office that maintained public registers of rejected cargo. Anyone could request a copy for a fee.

The queue began beneath a stone archway. A man carried two empty cages; a woman argued over the declared weight of a flour shipment. Kael waited his turn.

At the window he asked for the register of rejected lots from the Eastern Unloading Station.

The attendant, a burly man named Soren, shook his head.

“That was forty years ago.”

“I know.”

“We keep seven years of registers here. Anything older is in historical storage.”

“Where?”

“Same floor. Corridor on the left, blue door. But you'll need the lot number.”

“I have a transfer index.”

Soren examined it.

“This concerns materials recovery, not port cargo.”

“The documents indicate that some of the material passed through here.”

“What if it came through under a different consignee?”

“That's precisely what I want to check.”

Soren returned the paper.

“Then find the consignee's name.”

Kael looked at the line behind him.

He would not argue over procedure in front of people who had already spent the morning waiting.

“Is there an index by place of origin?”

“In storage. If it survived, it'll be there.”

The attendant leaned forward to call the next customer.

Kael stepped aside.

In historical storage, a clerk directed him to a row of heavy books bound in gray leather. Volumes older than a certain date had suffered water damage and could not be handled without assistance.

He found the section covering recovered materials from closed stations. Most entries listed timber, iron, and cable. Some said “precision components,” with no further detail.

One entry had been crossed out. In the margin was a figure of three strokes around an open dot.

Kael resisted an immediate feeling of satisfaction.

“Is it common to mark rejected entries with drawings?” he asked the clerk.

“Depends on the period. They used to have classification codes that aren't preserved in our manuals.”

“Can you explain this one?”

She studied the page.

“No. I only know the notes in the margins didn't alter the rejection decisions. The reason was recorded in another column.”

Kael read that column.

*Transfer not authorized due to assembly incompatibility.*

“Incompatible with what?”

“It doesn't say.”

He requested a certified copy of the page and the book's symbol index, if one existed. The clerk searched for several minutes. She returned with a partial answer: the index mentioned the figure without assigning it a name. It referred to a supplement missing from the volume.

“Who removed the supplement?”

“There's no record. It may have been lost before the books were transferred to us.”

Kael noted the absence without turning it into an allegation of deliberate removal.

When he left the storage room, Lyra was waiting at the foot of a staircase.

“You found the page,” she said.

“I found an entry that needs interpretation.”

“That too.”

“How did you know where to look?”

Lyra glanced at the request slip in his hand.

“Because there aren't many reasons to examine rejected lots from a closed station.”

“That doesn't explain how you knew there was a marked entry.”

“No, it doesn't.”

Kael took a deep breath.

“If you want us to trust each other, you'll have to answer questions.”

“I didn't ask for your trust.”

“You asked me to follow instructions.”

“I pointed you toward a public source. You chose to consult it.”

Her precision irritated him because it was correct.

“What does the mark mean?”

Lyra looked to either side of the staircase. People passed them, too occupied with crates and ropes to pay attention.

“It doesn't have only one meaning,” she said. “In some workshops it was an instruction to inspect something. In others it identified components whose provenance had to be checked before installation.”

“Who originally used it?”

“I don't know for certain.”

It was Lyra's first answer that did not seem designed to leave her several ways out.

Kael took out his notebook.

“The figure appears on medical packaging and on a technical plan. If you know which category of components it belongs to, that could help distinguish it from a commercial mark.”

“Medical packaging?”

Lyra's expression changed too quickly for Kael to assign a reliable meaning to it. It might be genuine surprise or a practiced reflex.

“I haven't said where it appears,” he corrected himself.

“You just did.”

Kael closed his notebook.

He had made a mistake. He had disclosed a connection from an open investigation without knowing where the woman stood in relation to it.

“I can't continue this conversation here,” he said.

Lyra nodded, but her eyes remained on the staircase.

“Be careful who you tell about finding that mark. Some people recognize it without knowing why they should.”

“That's too vague to help me.”

“Sometimes precision is exactly what puts someone in danger.”

“And sometimes vagueness lets anyone manipulate an investigation.”

Lyra looked down for a moment.

“You're right about that.”

The concession did not reassure him.

***

Lyra had not expected the archivist to mention medical packaging.

She had followed the trail of old materials because rejected lots could conceal parts reused without proper controls. Kael's words introduced a circumstance she could not fit into her assumptions without making the same mistake that had irritated him: turning an association into an explanation.

She let him go toward the copying office.

There was no need to follow him. It was enough to know he had found a genuine entry and was willing to put the limits of his knowledge on record.

The problem was that others would not be so patient.

At the pier exit, she noticed a man wearing a cargo inspector's cape. Not because she knew him, but because the badge on his belt belonged to a different office from the one listed on his credentials. It could have been an administrative reassignment. It could also have been someone using a uniform that happened to be available.

Lyra did not approach him.

She stopped beside a bread seller and bought two rolls she did not need. From there she watched the man consult the weighing office's entry register. The clerk handed him a list of public requests.

A cold irritation settled over Lyra.

She had led Kael to a legitimate source. That did not make the route invisible.

She did not know who was watching the harbor. She knew only that someone was checking names.

She returned to Ema's table.

“We're changing the trip,” she said.

“Which trip?”

“Tomorrow's. Nobody goes out through the north checkpoint.”

“And the package?”

“It stays here while I verify another carrier.”

Ema looked at her reproachfully.

“You promised the recipient it would arrive on time.”

“I promised I wouldn't put their life in the hands of someone I couldn't vouch for.”

“Those aren't the same promise.”

“No,” Lyra admitted.

Ema took out a notebook and crossed a line out of the itinerary. To Lyra, the scrape of pencil on paper sounded more final than it deserved.

“Is the man from the Archive dangerous?” the girl asked.

Lyra thought of the way Kael had corrected his own words before accepting a conclusion.

“I don't know yet.”

“Then why did you seek him out?”

“Because he can ask questions in places where I'd only raise suspicions.”

“That sounds like you're using him.”

Lyra did not argue.

She had not begun dealing with Kael out of altruism. She needed to know which documents were coming to light and whether they could bring her closer to a lead she had pursued for years. She had not intended to put him in danger. The difference between those two statements was beginning to matter less.

***

Kael returned to the Archive before closing time.

The copy of the rejected-lots register would be ready the next day. He had paid the fee, received a request number, and kept the receipt. Everything could be verified.

The first thing he did was call Daren.

“I've found a third occurrence of the figure,” he said.

“In another execution chamber?”

“In a port register of recovered materials. A source independent of the other two, although we don't yet know whether it derives from the same old inventories.”

“Can we add it to the case?”

“Once it's certified. For now I have a reference and the entry needed to request it.”

Daren made no attempt to hide his disappointment.

“I thought you were going to bring me something less difficult today.”

“I also found someone who seems familiar with the original classification.”

“Name?”

Kael looked at his notebook.

Lyra had given him a first name and no proof of identity. He could report it as informal information, but doing so would turn their conversation into a police lead without knowing whether she was involved in any crime.

“She introduced herself only as Lyra,” he said. “I have no surname or way to verify who she is.”

“Did she arrange to meet you?”

“She directed me to a public source.”

“And then?”

“We spoke at the pier. I mentioned a connection I shouldn't have shared.”

Daren fell silent at the other end of the line.

“What connection?”

Kael explained.

“That wasn't wise,” the inspector said.

“I know.”

“I appreciate you telling me before I read about it somewhere else.”

“That's why I'm calling.”

Daren exhaled.

“When you have the copy, we'll go together to ask about the parts. I don't want an informal conversation deciding our investigation.”

Kael agreed.

He ended the call and opened a new folder for technical marks. He did not classify it as proof of conspiracy, but as a correspondence awaiting verification.

A small sheet of paper protruded from the envelope he had brought from the port. It was the consultation receipt, printed on poor-quality paper. On the back, the clerk had written the number of a missing supplement.

Kael held the sheet up to the light.

The number began with the same prefix as the withdrawn series.

He checked twice.

Then he left the paper on his desk without touching the terminal.

He had found a connection between an apparently forgotten industrial procedure and an archival address that appeared in no accessible catalog. He did not know what it meant. But someone had preserved that connection for decades.

Before leaving, he wrote a formal request to identify the agency that had issued the supplement.

This time, he was not going to enter a number into a screen and wait for a system to respond.

He was going to ask a person to sign the answer.

***

Across the city, Lyra finally opened the parcel Orven had given her.

She arranged the brass pieces on Ema's table. They were ordinary, except for an old plate engraved with two sets of marks. The first was the sign she had shown the archivist; the second consisted of small calibration lines worn down by years of use.

Lyra knew that arrangement.

Not because anyone had taught her what it meant in general, but because she had once seen the same sequence in a procedure that should never have been carried out without authorization. She remembered the smell of hot metal and the sound of a voice ordering a test to stop. She remembered that no one stopped immediately.

The memory ended there.

It was not enough to conclude that Orven's plate had come from that place. Its makers might have copied an older design. The object might be incomplete.

But the calibration lines appeared in the same order.

Lyra covered the piece with the cloth.

Ema looked up.

“What's wrong?”

“I need to know who sold this lot.”

“Orven said it belonged to his father.”

“That's not enough.”

“Are you going to ask the archivist?”

Lyra looked toward the door, out into the dark harbor.

“Not yet.”

The promise she had made about the delivery remained unfulfilled. She had chosen to delay it for safety. And now, for the first time since meeting Kael, she suspected that danger did not lie only along the routes she knew.

It might also lie in the archives he was capable of opening.

***End of Episode 07.***
