# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 05: The Cargo

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel, third-person limited.

***

The newspaper's editor said there was no room for another story about the execution.

Inara Vel did not answer immediately. She was looking at the front-page illustration, in which an artist had drawn the court chamber as a black box with a monstrous hand emerging from it. The headline took up almost a quarter of the page: **THE CONDEMNED MAN WHO CHANGED HIS FACE**.

“That's ridiculous,” she said.

The editor stopped chewing.

“It's an illustration.”

“It's a lie printed with expensive ink.”

“We're not claiming that's what happened.”

“Then why are we drawing it?”

The man, Joren Mal, had a habit of speaking as though the newspaper were a living creature he fed with the copies it sold. On quiet days he was charming. On difficult days he could turn any ethical objection into an argument about wages.

“The city wants to know what happened,” he said. “We need a front page people can see from across the river.”

“The city needs us to find out what happened.”

“And have you found out?”

Inara put a folder on his desk.

“I found a maintenance contract nobody has explained.”

Joren opened it without enthusiasm.

“An invoice?”

“A modification to the execution facility. The municipality paid for extraordinary work.”

“Does that prove they switched the dead man?”

“No.”

“Then it doesn't replace my front page.”

Inara breathed slowly.

She had spent almost two years trying to regain the trust of some readers after publishing an accusation that turned out to be incomplete. She had not fabricated the facts. She had trusted a source who omitted the context needed to interpret them. The newspaper published a correction, but the person named in the story lost their job before the correction appeared.

Ever since, whenever someone said, “We have to publish now,” she heard a different question: *What do we really know?*

“Give me two days,” she said.

“You have one.”

“I need to verify who was paid under the contract.”

“If you come back with an invoice, I'll print it in the business section.”

“If I come back with proof, you'll print it where it belongs.”

Joren closed the folder.

“Tomorrow, then.”

Inara gathered her papers.

As she left, she heard the editor tell the illustrator to tone down the monstrous hand.

That was something.

***

The Municipal Procurement building stood six blocks from the newsroom, but it had an unpleasant ability to turn a short journey into a lost morning.

Inara arrived at ten and found a line moving slowly between wooden benches. A notice announced that requests for copies had to be submitted in writing and that responses would be issued within fifteen working days.

She had learned not to argue with notices.

A short-haired woman was working at the counter.

“I'm looking for maintenance contracts associated with the execution square,” Inara said.

“Number?”

“I have two possibilities.”

The clerk checked one.

“This is a temporary rental agreement.”

“Does it include modifications?”

“Modifications are processed separately.”

“And the second?”

The woman searched an index.

“This code isn't municipal.”

“Whose is it?”

“It's associated with an external authorization.”

“Which agency?”

“It's not listed here.”

Inara rested her elbows on the counter.

“Can I consult the full file?”

“Submit a written request.”

“I did that yesterday.”

“Then wait for the response.”

“Can you tell me who keeps the original?”

The woman pointed down a corridor.

“Liaison Office. Third floor.”

Inara thanked her.

The third floor had worn carpets and windows overlooking an inner courtyard. At the Liaison Office, a young man explained that the external authorization had been incorporated into the file without identifying its origin because it came from a shared-services agreement.

“With which organization?” Inara asked.

“That's in the agreement.”

“May I see it?”

“You need authorization.”

“From whom?”

“The organization.”

“Which organization?”

The young man watched her for a second, then lowered his eyes.

“I can't help you with that.”

Inara took out her notebook.

“May I quote your answer?”

“I'd rather you didn't.”

“I won't use your name. Just the procedure.”

“I'd rather you didn't do that either.”

Inara closed the notebook.

It was not useful testimony, but it was a concrete administrative trail.

The contract existed, the municipality had authorized payments, and an outside organization appeared in the process without being publicly identified.

That did not prove corruption.

But it justified asking questions.

***

At noon she went to the Lower Docks.

The neighborhood had a smell all its own: river water, hot grease, wet wood, and old cargo. Narrow streets suddenly opened into small yards where men and women unloaded crates while arguing over rates.

Inara had known the area since her earliest days as a reporter. She met an accountant named Helis Rann in a tavern beside the grain warehouse.

Helis worked for a transport cooperative. She had a habit of arranging coins by size even when she was not paying for anything.

“I don't like people looking for me at work,” Helis said as she sat down.

“And I don't like being denied public information.”

“Your problem doesn't affect me.”

“I hope it doesn't.”

Inara slid a contract number written on a scrap of paper across the table.

“Do you know this company?”

Helis read it.

“Arven Assembly Services. Yes.”

“Do they transport equipment?”

“They rent out machinery and hire technicians. They also manage some warehouses.”

“Do they work for the municipality?”

“When they win tenders.”

“And for the Council Delegation?”

Helis looked away.

“I don't have access to all their clients.”

“I'm not asking about all of them.”

The accountant rolled a coin between her fingers.

“I heard some orders arrive with priority authorization. They don't go through ordinary transport scheduling.”

“Security priority?”

“That's what they say.”

“Is there a record?”

“There's always a record of something. The problem is where.”

Inara let several seconds pass.

“Could you get me a copy of an order?”

Helis gave her a cold look.

“I'm not stealing documents for you.”

“I didn't ask you to.”

“Not yet.”

Inara nodded.

“You're right. I'm looking for public arrival and departure records.”

Helis put away the coin.

“There's a movement log at the central depot. The transport entries aren't indexed by contract. If you request a date range and a vehicle registration, they can give you information.”

“Which registration?”

“I'm not telling you, because then you'd know it came from me.”

“I understand.”

The accountant stood.

Before leaving, she offered one final sentence.

“If you want to understand that company, don't look at how much it charges. Look at how many names it uses.”

***

Inara returned to the newsroom and asked the business archive for the records of Arven Assembly Services.

The assistant took almost an hour.

When he appeared, he was carrying two folders.

“I found the company,” he said. “And an earlier business registered at the same address.”

“Which one?”

“Rhal Equipment.”

Inara leafed through the papers.

The earlier company had been dissolved seven years before. Arven had purchased some of its assets, including vehicles, workshops, and rights to use storage facilities. It was a perfectly legal transaction.

But one of the former authorized representatives was also listed as a technical representative of Arven.

“Are they the same company?” the assistant asked.

“Not necessarily. Plenty of companies share owners and assets.”

“Then there's no story.”

“There are questions.”

Inara began comparing dates.

During the transition between the companies, some contracts had been issued in one company's name and performed by the other. Some had assignment addenda. Others did not.

In a railway works file, she found a maintenance authorization signed by the same representative who had participated in work on the execution square.

It was not enough to publish.

But a trail was taking shape.

At five, she left the building to find a second witness.

The sky was a greenish gray. On the tram tracks, the recent rain reflected the lights from the shops.

Inara stopped at a newspaper stand.

The front page Joren had approved was on display.

The monstrous hand no longer emerged from the black box. Now the box contained a human silhouette surrounded by flashes of light.

It was still an image nobody had witnessed.

The vendor noticed her expression.

“Don't like it?”

“I work there.”

“Ah. So you don't.”

Inara bought a copy.

On page two, she found an unsigned article attributing the incident to an extraordinary manifestation of the Pulse. It cited anonymous sources and concluded that the Council Delegation was investigating the phenomenon.

The report was not wrong in every detail.

It was worse: it presented an institutional hypothesis as the most likely explanation without identifying the evidence.

Inara folded the paper.

Now she had another reason to write the story properly.

***

Her afternoon interview was with a former employee of Rhal Equipment.

His name was Belor Sen, and he lived in a boardinghouse behind the freight station. He had been dismissed during the company's restructuring.

Inara brought neither a recorder nor a companion. She had promised him a private conversation before deciding whether she could quote him.

Belor opened the door only a few inches.

“What do you want?”

“To talk about the company you worked for.”

“I haven't worked there in years.”

“I know. I'm not looking for information about current clients.”

He hesitated.

Finally, he let her in.

The room contained a small table, two chairs, and an unlit stove. Mechanical parts stood on a shelf, and a calendar three years out of date hung on the wall.

“They told us Rhal was closing because it was losing money,” Belor said. “Then Arven appeared with the same workshops and almost the same bosses.”

“Did you keep your job?”

“No. They said my job category didn't exist in the new structure.”

“What did you do?”

“I kept inventories of tools and spare parts.”

Inara took out her notebook.

“Do you remember orders associated with judicial facilities?”

“We didn't work with courts when I was there.”

“What about the Delegation?”

Belor scratched his beard.

“There were work orders from a restricted fund. They came with codes that explained nothing. If a part was missing, we couldn't contact the requester directly.”

“Who authorized the movement?”

“A liaison supervisor. The person changed now and then.”

“Do you still have any of those orders?”

“No. I didn't steal documents.”

“I didn't say you did.”

“You thought it.”

Inara looked at him without answering.

Belor got up and went to the shelf. He picked up a small metal part: a toothed wheel with a polished edge.

“This is mine. They gave it to me when the workshop closed. A defective part.”

“What's special about it?”

“Nothing. But we made it for a project whose name never appeared on the orders. Just a code. I remember because I never learned where they installed it.”

Inara examined the piece without touching it.

“Do you remember the code?”

“Not all of it.”

“Part of it?”

The man thought for a long time.

Finally, he wrote four characters in the margin of an old newspaper.

Inara felt a stab of interest.

They were the same characters she had seen on the execution square's second contract.

She said nothing.

“Are you sure?”

“It was an unusual order. Sometimes you remember things like that.”

Inara thanked him and put away the paper.

Before she left, Belor stopped her.

“I don't want you printing my name.”

“I won't do that without talking to you first.”

“Promise me.”

Inara remembered the accusation from two years earlier, and the person who had lost their job over an incomplete report.

“I promise.”

***

When she returned to the newspaper, Joren was about to leave.

“Bring anything I can print?”

Inara placed a list of contracts and companies on his desk.

“Not yet.”

The editor stared at her in disbelief.

“You spent all day investigating just to tell me no?”

“I spent all day verifying that several transactions belong to a shared-services arrangement whose responsible authority isn't identified in the public files.”

“That's far too long a sentence for a front page.”

“I also found a connection between two companies using the same assets.”

“Is that illegal?”

“Not necessarily.”

Joren sighed.

“What do you need?”

“The complete transport records, the original authorizations, and someone who can explain the cost center.”

“How much time?”

“Two more days.”

“I said one.”

“I know.”

Joren sat down.

For a moment he looked at the front page he had approved, then at Inara's folders.

“I'm not paying for another week of investigation without a story.”

“I'm not asking for a week.”

“You have until tomorrow afternoon.”

Inara opened her mouth to argue, then decided against it.

It was more than she had expected.

***

Late that evening, she received a partial copy of a transport log.

It had arrived through the normal public-records request channel, with no personal sender. The sheet listed vehicles entering and leaving a depot beside the river.

Inara recognized the registration number of a truck used by Arven Assembly Services.

The vehicle had entered in the hours before dawn on the day of the execution and left two hours later.

The manifest described assembly tools and replacement parts.

Nothing exceptional.

But there was a second entry recorded under the name Rhal Equipment.

The company had been dissolved seven years earlier.

The vehicle registration was the same.

Inara checked the dates.

The operation was recorded at the same depot, during the same time interval, under two different companies.

One entry could be an administrative error.

Two could be a poorly documented transfer.

But the document showed a third column: receiving officer.

The name was abbreviated.

The initial and surname matched those of the missing technical supervisor of the execution chamber.

Inara felt the day's exhaustion vanish all at once.

She still had no proof of a crime.

She had a specific connection between a dissolved company, a nighttime shipment, and the person who might explain a mechanical modification.

She opened her notebook and wrote a new question.

*Why is a company that no longer exists still moving cargo?*

Beneath it she wrote the case number.

As she was putting away the papers, she discovered an additional sheet stuck to the back of the copy.

It was a delivery receipt she had not requested.

The customer field contained an unfamiliar trade name.

Inara searched the general register for that name.

No company appeared.

But the address was familiar.

It belonged to the warehouse Arven had inherited from Rhal.

There were three trade names for the same address in the city.

And one of them was not even listed as a registered company.

Inara closed her notebook.

For the first time all day, she had a story she could begin to prove.

***End of Episode 05.***
