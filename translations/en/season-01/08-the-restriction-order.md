# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 08: The Restriction Order

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Maelor Thane, third-person limited.

***

On the morning he was asked to close the execution case, Maelor Thane had to decide whether a valve should remain open.

The valve belonged to the Southern Compensation Station. It was not an extraordinary device, although its importance had become inconvenient for those who had to explain it in public. The facility absorbed load fluctuations from several workshops and kept the regulators' pressure within a safe range. Closing it entirely would leave three neighborhoods without power for hours. Leaving it operating with a damaged seal would expose its workers to a risk Maelor refused to call minor.

The engineer who had submitted the report requested a gradual load reduction and authorization to replace a precision plate. The part was in stock, but releasing it required signatures from two separate offices.

“Authorize it,” Maelor said.

Vessa, his secretary, did not lift her pen.

“The Energy Seat hasn't issued budgetary approval yet.”

“We're not approving a capacity expansion. We're preventing a component from fracturing under pressure.”

“The form doesn't distinguish between those operations.”

Maelor looked at the folder.

“Then attach a resolution that does.”

“It will need my certification signature and that of someone responsible for Energy.”

“Call that person.”

Vessa wrote down the instruction.

A second folder, bound with red tape, waited at the edge of his desk. It had come from the Vael-Tir Delegation. It contained a request for the Council to assume documentary coordination over the execution of Taren Oss.

The first folder required a limited technical decision. The second seemed to ask far more than it said.

“Who drafted this?” Maelor asked.

“The Delegation's legal office.”

“Who requested it?”

Vessa examined a sheet.

“It's signed by Elian Voss, secretary of Licensing. The justification cites a risk of disclosing arcane procedures.”

“That doesn't give him jurisdiction over a municipal criminal investigation.”

“The document speaks of cooperation, not replacement.”

Maelor read the second paragraph. The word “cooperation” appeared four times. None specified which documents they wanted to protect.

“Prepare a reply,” he said. “We will agree to authorized inspectors examining equipment subject to licensing. Criminal matters and the chain of custody will remain under judicial authority. Any restriction must identify the items and the legal grounds.”

Vessa wrote without comment.

“And the chamber case?”

“It stays open.”

The secretary glanced at him.

“The Delegation has implied it expects otherwise.”

“The Delegation can wait sitting down.”

***

The Council of Seats had convened urgently three times in eight days. At the first meeting they had discussed the execution. At the second, the price of high-purity materials. At the third, each representative had spoken about something different while pretending they were addressing the same crisis.

Maelor did not regard that disorder as accidental. It was how the organization had chosen to limit the damage of concentrating too much knowledge. Over the years, however, the division of responsibilities had also allowed some offices to hide behind decisions made by others.

The Vael-Tir Delegation's meeting room was paneled in pale wood. Through the window one could see the roofs of the Foundries and a chimney venting white smoke. Maelor had requested only three officials: Elian Voss for Licensing, Ardis Ren for Archives, and Talia Mer, the technical delegate for Energy.

They did not represent every Seat. Nor did they have the authority to decide matters outside their mandates.

“The municipality refuses to transfer the evidence,” Elian said.

“It has no obligation to transfer it,” Maelor replied. “It can allow supervised consultation where technical jurisdiction has been established.”

“There are exposure risks.”

“Identify them.”

Elian straightened a sheet on the table.

“The chamber plans employ assembly elements subject to dissemination restrictions.”

“Which elements?”

“The classification depends on a catalog we're still verifying.”

Maelor put down his pen.

“We cannot restrict an object simply because we suspect it might be restricted.”

Ardis spoke.

“Some inherited components bear marks that predate our current catalogs. A drawing alone isn't enough to establish a licensing category.”

“Then the appropriate response is to examine the component,” Maelor said, “not seize control of the investigation that found it.”

Talia Mer had an electricity-consumption report in front of her.

“My concern is different. During the work in the square, a service load was authorized that doesn't appear in the ordinary contract. It wasn't large by city standards, but the connection was made to a branch that should have been isolated.”

Maelor watched her.

“Should have been?”

“According to our current plans.”

“And the field plans?”

“I'm awaiting an inspection.”

That distinction mattered to him more than any rumor of impossible phenomena. A drawing in an office and a pipe beneath the street could describe two different cities. The latter always imposed its consequences.

“Prioritize the inspection,” he said. “If there's an unauthorized connection, determine first whether it's still energized.”

Elian opened his mouth.

“That will require entering a facility under judicial investigation.”

“Then coordinate with the magistrate. Don't enter without an order, and don't allow anyone to tamper with a sealed item.”

“We could avoid the conflict if the file passed into Council custody.”

“No,” Maelor said.

The word hung between them.

“We are not turning a difficulty in cooperation into an argument for taking over someone else's jurisdiction.”

Ardis closed a folder.

“There's another problem,” she said. “The Central Archive has requested information about a continuity collection missing from its public indexes.”

Maelor knew which collection she meant. He also knew the mere existence of a restricted series could be legal, while the appearance of its number in a civil case file could create an obligation to explain it. He would not meet that obligation by pretending the collection did not exist.

“Who made the request?”

“A documentary integrity analyst. Kael Veyran.”

The name was unfamiliar.

“What was his question?”

“The provenance of an identifier included in judicial documentation.”

“And our answer?”

Ardis took too long.

“The automated query was suspended. Then an access review was initiated.”

“That doesn't answer my question.”

“We have no authorized index we can provide at his clearance level.”

“Then there must be a written response stating the restriction, its grounds, and the procedure for appeal. Was he given one?”

“Not yet.”

“See that he is.”

Elian watched him with obvious discomfort.

“A formal reply will place the series on record.”

“The series has already placed itself on record in another document.”

Maelor watched Ardis make a note. Unlike Elian, she did not seem annoyed. She seemed concerned that the complete index might be less reliable than her office wished to believe.

***

At noon, Vessa brought a letter from the Civic Council into his office.

Its representative, Neris Daal, requested a hearing on the cost of containing the urban network and delays in inspections. The execution was not mentioned until the third paragraph, but Maelor understood that the two issues had become one in public debate.

“Shall we grant the hearing?” Vessa asked.

“Yes.”

“They've requested access to the stations' safety certificates.”

“Civil certificates and unrestricted technical summaries should be available.”

“And contingency reports?”

“Depends on their contents. Don't grant or deny a whole set of documents without classifying them.”

Vessa left a note beside the letter.

“The statement proposed by the Delegation says there was no technical irregularity whatsoever in the square.”

Maelor read the sentence and struck it out.

“There's still an open investigation.”

“We could say no arcane irregularity has been confirmed.”

“We can say the Council is cooperating with the relevant authorities. We don't need to prejudge the outcome to protect an office's reputation.”

Vessa looked at him.

“That will anger Elian.”

“A statement's purpose isn't to keep him in a good mood.”

As she removed the papers, Maelor returned to the Southern Station report. Talia had sent an updated calculation: gradual load reduction was feasible, but would mean a two-hour interruption in a residential district. The new plate could not be installed until part of the line cooled.

He signed the authorization on condition that outage times be published and emergency supply secured for the neighborhood dispensary.

The city would not have a perfect solution that week. It could have an explicable decision.

***

Late that afternoon, a call came through the Archives' internal circuit.

Ardis Ren spoke without the composure she had shown in the meeting.

“I need you to review a registration irregularity.”

“Describe it.”

“A transfer order was issued this morning to remove three files from the continuity collection. It carries a Custody restriction code.”

Maelor reached for the authorization ledger Vessa kept beside the telephone.

“I haven't signed any transfer order today.”

“The authorization is recorded under a subdelegation from your office.”

“In whose name?”

“A liaison officer. Natan Sorel.”

Maelor knew Sorel. He had worked on technical inspections and transfers of sensitive documentation. He was efficient and had the dangerous quality of remembering instructions without asking whether they were still in force.

“Where are the files?”

“According to the dispatch register, in transit to the Delegation's storage facility.”

“Is the transfer covered by an earlier resolution?”

“I can't find one identifying those three files.”

Maelor fell silent.

Delegations could issue movement orders under preapproved categories. But those categories did not authorize taking any folder one pleased. They required a reason, a responsible officer, and a destination. If the code had been used without a specific order, someone had crossed a clear line.

“Suspend receipt,” he said. “No one is to open or destroy the units. Have Sorel report to me, and preserve the transport chain of custody.”

“And if the boxes have already arrived?”

“Seal them in front of two witnesses. Nobody handles them until we've checked the paperwork.”

“The Delegation says the transfer is urgent.”

“It is. That's why I want to know who authorized it.”

Vessa appeared in the doorway as Maelor ended the call.

“Is there a problem?”

“A subordinate used a credential from our system without an order we can locate.”

“Forgery?”

“Not yet.”

Maelor detested the ease with which serious words replaced the facts needed to substantiate them. A valid credential did not make every decision taken with it valid. Nor did it prove criminal intent on its own.

“Get Sorel's subdelegation history,” he said. “And today's movement list. I don't want summaries.”

***

Natan Sorel arrived an hour later, his coat still damp.

He had an angular face and a quiet voice. He remained standing until Maelor indicated a chair.

“You removed three files,” the Custody official said.

“Yes, sir.”

“On whose orders?”

“The documentary continuity circuit's.”

“That describes a procedure, not a person.”

Sorel looked at his hands.

“The instruction appeared in my priority movement queue.”

“Did you verify it?”

“It had a valid Custody code and a restriction reference.”

“Did you compare the reference against the authorized list of files?”

“There was no list attached.”

“Then why did you remove them?”

“The system indicated that the movement could be carried out at my clearance level.”

Maelor stood and approached the window. Outside, the courtyard lamps were beginning to light up.

“What were you taught about exceptional orders?”

“That they must be recorded with a cause and a responsible person.”

“And who was listed here?”

Sorel did not answer.

Maelor turned.

“I'm not asking to humiliate you. I need to know what happened.”

“I thought it was a safeguarding operation,” Sorel said. “Since the execution, several collections have been subject to inspection.”

“Which is precisely why the orders must be traceable.”

“I didn't open the files.”

“I didn't ask whether you opened them.”

Sorel lowered his eyes.

“I removed them and handed them over for transport. I thought I was carrying out a legitimate order.”

Maelor searched for defiance. He found fear.

That did not absolve the man of responsibility. Nor was it enough to accuse him of a conspiracy.

“You'll be removed from restricted transfers while this is investigated,” he said. “You'll hand over the log showing how you received the instruction and state who assigned the job. You'll be entitled to keep a copy of your statement.”

“Am I under arrest?”

“No. I have no authority to arrest you over an administrative violation. If evidence of a crime emerges, the relevant authorities will be informed.”

Sorel looked up.

“If I hand over the log, someone will know it was me.”

“Your name is already on the dispatch record.”

“I don't mean the dispatch record.”

Maelor stopped.

“Explain.”

Sorel clasped his fingers tightly.

“When the instruction arrived, my supervisor told me not to ask where it came from. He said the order came from above.”

“Which supervisor?”

“Officer Renn. Liaison.”

“Are you prepared to put that on record?”

“Yes. If it says in writing that I said it here.”

Maelor pulled a blank sheet toward him.

“Then start with the time.”

***

Night had fallen by the time Ardis, Vessa, and Maelor met again.

The boxes had been held at the Delegation's storage facility before they were opened. Two witnesses had certified the seals. The material chain of custody had been interrupted during transit, but it was still possible to reconstruct who had been responsible for the boxes.

The access history presented a different problem.

The movement instruction had not originated in the Custody office. It had entered through a liaison service authorized to generate preliminary requests, not executable orders.

Someone had treated that preliminary permission as though it were a signature.

“A system failure?” Vessa asked.

“An administrative vulnerability,” Ardis answered. “I don't yet know whether it was exploited deliberately.”

Maelor examined the times. The request had been generated after a Central Archive query of a historical reference. The transfer had begun before Archives issued the formal response he had ordered.

Two actions could coincide by chance. Their proximity justified investigation, not an assignment of blame.

“Which files were they?” he asked.

Ardis consulted the list.

“A technical inventory, a maintenance schedule, and an index of correspondences between services.”

“Do they contain information about the execution?”

“I can't know without examining the originals. One series is linked by reference to a record the analyst Veyran consulted.”

Maelor leaned against the back of his chair.

He did not yet know whether someone had tried to protect the documents from a leak, remove them from an investigation, or merely follow an inherited instruction that had taken on a life of its own. He did know the order had been carried out without verifiable grounds.

“I want all three units returned to Archives custody under a jointly signed record,” he said. “The municipal court will be notified if any belongs to its case. Access review will be assigned to someone other than whoever authorized the movement.”

Vessa recorded every instruction.

“Do we inform Elian?”

“We'll inform the person responsible for the Delegation about the irregular use of its service. We won't hand him the documents so he can decide whether his own system behaved correctly.”

Ardis nodded.

“There will be objections.”

“Have them put in writing.”

The final folder on the table held the reply pending for Kael Veyran. Maelor read the draft: three pages of formulas explaining why the analyst could not consult the records, and not one explaining how to appeal.

He picked up his pen and added a line at the end.

*Restricting a series does not prevent identification of the authority responsible for deciding a substantiated request.*

He set the sheet beside the seal.

“Send it tomorrow.”

***

After everyone had left, Maelor remained in the Delegation's meeting room.

Rain was falling on the city again. From the window, he could see the tram lamps and, farther away, a flickering strip of light in the Foundries. Work did not stop because a Council decided to review its own jurisdiction.

Maelor knew there were reasons for restricting documents. Irresponsible disclosure could lead inexperienced people into facilities they did not understand. Unsafe procedures could not be remedied by issuing a public statement.

He also knew such reasons could become a refuge for someone who simply did not want to be held accountable.

For decades, he had defended the principle of limiting access. He would not abandon it over one badly explained execution. But if an order could remove files under his authority without his knowledge, the protection it offered was becoming something else.

Vessa returned to collect his coat.

“Shall I turn off the lights?”

“In a moment.”

“Ardis left one final note.”

Maelor took it.

It was a record of an inquiry into the movement queue. A liaison officer had accessed the file list after the boxes were detained, using a different service credential.

“Sorel?” he asked.

“No.”

“Renn?”

“Neither.”

The user field showed a generic designation: *Continuity — automatic execution.*

Below it was an audit note.

The operation had not been automatic. Someone had entered all three file numbers manually.

Maelor reread the time.

The access had occurred after he ordered the transfer suspended.

He set the note on the table.

At least one person knew the order was under investigation and was still trying to locate those documents.

This time, demanding an administrative explanation would not be enough.

***End of Episode 08.***
