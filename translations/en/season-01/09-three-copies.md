# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 09: Three Copies

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, third-person limited.

***

The official response arrived in an envelope far too thick for the information it contained.

Kael Veyran opened it under his desk lamp. There were six pages of warnings about the use of historical collections, four references to regulatory provisions, and authorization to consult only the provenance index of the port supplement.

The authorization was valid for two hours.

The authority that had signed it had not included its full name.

Kael reread the last page. There, among the conditions for reproduction, was a statement he could at least verify: *The requested unit is held in the Documentary Continuity Section of the Vael-Tir Delegation.*

It was more than he had obtained in a week.

Aldren Meret stood at the window.

“The permit doesn't authorize examination of the contents,” he said.

“Only the index.”

“Don't try opening another folder because its codes look similar.”

“I won't.”

Aldren turned.

“You shouldn't have looked through the port records on your own, either.”

“They were public.”

“Not everything public is relevant to your assignment.”

Kael rested his hands on the envelope.

“An identifier in a judicial case refers to an inaccessible series. An industrial classification supplement uses the same prefix. It's relevant to check whether they share a provenance.”

“Inspector Holt is responsible for the criminal investigation.”

“And I'm responsible for establishing the authenticity of the documents received by the Archive.”

Aldren watched him without answering.

Kael had learned from him that a single reference might be an error. Two independent references might reveal a convention. Three documents with the same anomaly demanded reconstruction of their source. Now his supervisor seemed uncomfortable with that rule being applied.

“You have two hours,” Aldren finally said. “I'll record that I'm authorizing a limited consultation. No uncertified copies.”

“Agreed.”

“And if you find something you don't understand, don't turn it into an accusation.”

“Agreed to that too.”

Aldren approached the door, then stopped before leaving.

“Kael, there's a difference between discovering a lie and discovering that a system no longer understands its own records.”

“I know.”

“I hope you do.”

***

The Continuity Section occupied the north wing of the Delegation, behind a row of windowless offices. An official checked Kael's authorization three times and issued a temporary badge. Someone else led him to a consultation room containing a table, a clock, and a single volume.

The index was bound in gray covers.

Kael recorded the opening time and the seal number. He was not allowed to photograph pages. He could make handwritten notes, subject to review by the reading-room attendant.

His first surprise was finding the port supplement without any drawing.

It did not contain the mark of three strokes. It contained a table matching office names, material categories, and administrative destinations. The symbol appeared in an external catalog referenced by the table, but that catalog was not available.

Kael set the figure aside for the time being and followed the numbers.

The supplement had been issued to organize materials recovered from facilities that had changed names or jurisdictions. Each transfer retained an identifier for the source. When a station was closed, its components did not necessarily lose that reference; they might pass through depots, auctions, and new installations.

It was a reasonable explanation for an old code appearing in modern documentation.

It was also an explanation that could be tested.

Kael copied three equivalences. One corresponded to the Eastern Unloading Station; another, to a port depot; the third, to a maintenance unit whose name had been replaced by a number.

He did not recognize the number.

The validity column showed a closure date.

Forty-two years earlier.

The next column, reserved for updates, contained a later entry.

Thirty-nine years earlier.

The index did not declare that the station had reopened. It recorded a change of custody.

Kael noted it. An update after closure did not prove the installation had continued operating: the property of a closed station could be moved for decades afterward.

He turned the page.

The same unit had received another update six months earlier.

This time the column did not say “custody.”

It said “maintenance.”

Kael looked up at the clock.

He had forty-seven minutes left.

“I need to identify the document supporting this entry,” he told the attendant.

The woman examined the line without touching the book.

“The index isn't authorized for follow-up inquiries.”

“My permission covers provenance.”

“Provenance of the unit, yes. Not the associated files.”

“Then please record the exact reference and the reason for denying access.”

The attendant nodded.

“That I can do.”

It was the correct answer. Kael appreciated it, even though it prevented him from searching any further there.

He finished copying the numbers and returned the index with seven minutes to spare.

***

Daren Holt was waiting for him in the outside courtyard.

“You look like someone coming out of a library with a fine,” he said.

“A documented refusal.”

“Almost the same thing, except you collect those.”

They walked to a small café, away from the Delegation's service windows. Daren had requested a copy of a municipal inventory of the Eastern Station and wanted to compare his information with Kael's.

“The problem is the name,” the inspector explained as they sat down. “The plan from the square uses a technical designation, not a company name. The municipal register uses another.”

Kael opened his notebook.

“Let's not assume they're equivalent. Let's check the numbers.”

Daren slid a document across the table.

It was a certificate issued by the Urban Infrastructure Archive. It listed equipment removed from the station and the dates of delivery to the municipal property depot. In the final section, a note stated that the building had been closed and its installations disconnected from the public network.

Kael found the number.

It matched one he had copied from the Continuity index.

“That confirms its historical origin,” he said.

“What about this year's modification?”

“I don't know what was modified. The index says ‘maintenance,’ but it doesn't identify a physical object.”

Daren leaned over the table.

“They could be maintaining a warehouse.”

“Yes. Or a recovered component. Or a computer system still using the old name.”

“Or the station.”

“We can't say that yet.”

Daren sipped his coffee.

“We need a third source.”

“One that doesn't derive from those indexes.”

“Know of any?”

Kael thought of the transport network. Every intervention on a connected substation required a consumption record and, if outside personnel were involved, a work order. Even if administrative archives changed, power and labor had to be accounted for somewhere.

“The utility provider,” he said.

“I doubt they'll want to give us bills from forty years ago.”

“I don't need old bills. I need to know whether there's an active meter associated with that installation number.”

Daren smiled.

“That's a question I can ask with a court order, if we can justify the connection to the square.”

“The connection is still indirect,” Kael said, closing his notebook.

“Then let's request the public service information first. If we find something, we'll take it to the judge.”

***

The Tariff Office stood on the other side of the river.

It had no historical reading room. It had three counters, a broken ticket machine, and a woman who seemed to regard every inquiry as an interruption to her real work.

“I'm looking for the administrative status of a connection,” Kael said.

“Account number.”

He gave her the old unit identifier.

She entered it into a terminal.

“Doesn't exist.”

“Can you check retired installations?”

“Only if you have a case number.”

Kael showed her the closure record.

The woman searched again.

“There it is. Closed.”

“Since when?”

“The date matches your document.”

Kael wrote down her answer.

“Is there a record of later operations?”

“Service histories aren't released without authorization.”

“Can you tell me if there's a link to a current meter?”

“That requires a technical inquiry.”

“Where do I submit one?”

She pointed to a counter at the back.

Kael went there and waited again. The second clerk examined his paperwork and requested a fee for a certificate matching installation identifiers.

“It can take ten days,” he warned.

“I'd still like to apply.”

As Kael filled in the form, a warning plaque beside the counter caught his attention: *Every service connection has a party responsible for its load, even when the property is out of use.*

It was not a universal rule. Retired equipment could retain a number without consuming power. But if the maintenance entry referred to a real operation, there would be an invoice or an exemption.

Kael also requested the procedure for verifying exemptions.

The clerk gave him another form.

Outside, Daren was waiting under the eaves.

“Anything?”

“Two applications.”

“Evidence?”

“Not yet.”

“I'll buy you lunch when you find some.”

“Then perhaps you should start saving.”

***

That afternoon, Kael received a call from Tessa Ord.

“I don't have permission to access the series you're looking for,” the archivist said. “But I found a note that may help you tell a transfer apart from an operation.”

“I'm listening.”

“The municipal property inventory has three closure categories: physical closure, removal from the accounts, and suspension of service. They don't always happen together.”

“Where's the inventory?”

“Public Archive. There's a copy in our commercial reading room.”

“Have you checked it?”

“Yes. The station you're interested in was physically closed and disconnected from the public network. But part of its property remained in technical reserve.”

“Reserved for repairing other equipment?”

“That's what the category says. It doesn't say which equipment.”

“Can you send me the reference?”

“It's already logged. Come for the copy whenever you like.”

Kael thanked her.

The new information weakened a spectacular interpretation of his findings. If some of the materials had been retained to repair other installations, the recent maintenance entry might refer to a component rather than the closed building.

It would have been easier to ignore that possibility.

He wrote it down first.

***

The following day, a response arrived with a speed no one had requested.

The Tariff Office could not release consumption details for an inactive connection, but the identifier-matching system had generated an automatic certificate. The Eastern Station's historical number was associated with two derivative records.

One referred to a water-treatment plant they already knew about.

The other had an abbreviated address: *Subunit E-0, special maintenance.*

There was no public street address.

Kael asked Daren to verify the certificate's authenticity. The inspector checked its issuing seal with the Tariff Office and obtained written confirmation that the institutional system had generated the record; it had not been added by hand.

That established that the equivalence existed.

It did not establish that the second record referred to a functioning building.

Kael needed a third supporting source. He found it where he least expected: in his own supervisor's office.

Aldren called him in shortly before closing.

“We've received a copy of a purchasing statement,” he said. “I don't know why someone sent it to the Central Archive.”

The document bore a consultation authorization issued by the Shared Services Office. It had been incorporated into a routine expenditure audit file.

Aldren placed it on the desk.

“Before you ask, there's no restriction on this statement. It's commercial documentation.”

Kael examined the heading.

Subunit E-0 appeared as the cost center for a maintenance allocation. A repair company had billed for the replacement of regulator plates, filters, and cooling service.

The date was more than four decades after the historical closure.

“They could be stored parts,” Aldren said.

“Yes.”

“Or a cost-center error.”

“That too.”

“Why do you look disappointed?”

Kael looked up.

“I'm not. Now we have three records we can cross-check: the provenance index, the service equivalence, and a commercial invoice. If the cost center is wrong, there should be a correction. If the parts were installed elsewhere, there should be a transfer order.”

Aldren sat down.

“That doesn't authorize you to investigate purchases unrelated to your assignment.”

“I'm not going to inspect workshops. I'll report the discrepancy and ask whether it falls under the court's jurisdiction.”

“Your task is to verify documentation related to the execution.”

“The identifier appeared in that documentation.”

The silence stretched.

Aldren rubbed the bridge of his nose.

“I once told you every system leaves traces of its corrections.”

“I remember.”

“I wish I could remember when I stopped believing it.”

Kael did not know what to say.

Aldren signed the receipt acknowledgment and returned the document.

“Submit a formal report. I won't stop you.”

***

That evening, Kael laid out three copies on the table in his home.

The first: the Continuity index, with its recent maintenance entry.

The second: the equivalence certificate issued by the Tariff Office.

The third: the commercial statement assigning expenditure to the same subunit.

The documents came from different offices, used different formats, and had been produced for unrelated purposes. Matching numbers did not absolutely guarantee independence: they might all share an original administrative source. So he drew a line between the three and noted what still had to be checked.

A call from Daren interrupted him.

“The judge agreed to request the details of interventions involving the subunit,” he said.

“On what grounds?”

“A material discrepancy connected to plans used in the square. The request is limited to the source of components, work orders, and the service location. It doesn't include restricted collections unrelated to the case.”

“That should be enough,” Kael said, setting down his pen.

“The magistrate said that if we find nothing but an invoice charged to the wrong account, he still wants to know.”

“That's reasonable.”

“I've never heard you compliment a magistrate before.”

“Don't put it on the record.”

Daren laughed.

“Tomorrow we'll see whether that subunit has a workshop, a warehouse, or an administrative ghost.”

Kael put the copies into separate envelopes.

The telephone rang again before he went to bed.

This time it was someone from the Tariff Office. She was calling from an institutional number and asked Kael to confirm his request for equivalences.

“Mr. Veyran, there's something you should know,” the woman said. “Your certificate shows the connection's status, but not the scheduled interventions.”

“I didn't request those details.”

“I know. I'm mentioning it because the system links the connection to a maintenance route.”

Kael sat down.

“A current route?”

“I can't give you the addresses. I can only tell you what's in the administrative field.”

“What does it say?”

The silence on the other end lasted several seconds.

“That the subunit has an open periodic inspection. There's no completion date.”

“Since when?”

The woman read from a screen.

“I can't see the original date. But there's a completed intervention from eleven days ago.”

Kael looked at the three copies.

“Can you certify that in writing?”

“You need a judicial request for the details. I shouldn't have told you this much.”

“Why did you call?”

“Because I've worked with out-of-service connections for fifteen years. This is the first time I've seen a closed station with an active maintenance order and no public address where the workers are supposed to report.”

The call ended.

Kael recorded her words as unverified verbal information. The conversation could not substitute for a certified record.

Still, if the system existed as she had described it, a facility or set of equipment linked to the station was receiving maintenance after its closure.

The three copies no longer explained why.

The next day, they would have to find out exactly what someone was still repairing.

***End of Episode 09.***
