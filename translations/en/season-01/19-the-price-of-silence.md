# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 19: The Price of Silence

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran and Inara Vel, third-person limited, switching only between scenes.

***

The notice arrived at Kael's home in an envelope that did not bear the Archive's seal.

At first, he took that as a good sign.

He had requested that decisions concerning his suspension also be served through the administrative review channel, rather than through the service queues responsible for the disputed movements. The Personnel representative had accepted the request and entered it in his case file. This delivery bore the signature of an office with a name and an address.

Its contents were less reassuring.

*Reorganization of conservation units. Transfer of reproductions awaiting return. Execution date: next working shift.*

Kael read the inventory numbers.

They belonged to the two copies he had left sealed in the conservation room, with Tessa as witness. The order did not say they were to be destroyed. Nor did it explain why they had to leave the location recorded in the incident report.

Below was a service address. The same kind of destination he had challenged before losing his permissions.

He had no way of knowing whether someone had initiated the transfer to clear a legitimate backlog of documents, resolve his disciplinary case, or remove material that could answer an uncomfortable question. Intent remained beyond his reach.

The fact was simpler: someone was going to move the copies before the review of their custody had concluded.

On the table were the papers from his own case, obtained as a party to the proceedings. There were no restricted originals or institutional reproductions in his home. He could point out the defect again and await another response.

That was what he had done last time.

He dressed, put the notice away, and left for the legal-assistance office listed at the bottom of his response form.

***

The lawyer was named Dalia Ferren, and her desk was too small for the files she received.

Her job was not to declare him innocent. She advised employees facing administrative liability proceedings and could accompany them in filing appeals. Kael appreciated that even before they discussed his case.

“Did you take those copies?” she asked.

“No. According to the last record I received, they remain in the Archive's conservation room.”

“Can you prove it?”

Kael laid out the incident acknowledgment, the corrected register of reproductions, and the witness certification. The copies were identified by number and location. None of those documents contained the text of the protected collections.

Dalia examined them.

“There's a registration omission you acknowledge,” she said.

“Yes.”

“That may justify a proportionate sanction. It doesn't entitle the administration to turn the material's destination into a field with no responsible person.”

Kael felt compelled to explain the entire chronology. Dalia raised a hand.

“First I need to know what you want to achieve. Reinstatement?”

“I want the material to remain traceable until it's determined who has authority to move it.”

“That's not the same thing.”

“I know.”

“Because a preservation request may delay your defense. The office investigating your conduct will argue that you're obstructing the return.”

Kael looked at the acknowledgment. He had signed the items into storage precisely to avoid that accusation.

“I'm not opposing a formal return,” he said. “I'm opposing the loss of its physical trail while they're still deciding whether I improperly retained it.”

Dalia leaned back.

“We can request a preservation measure from the body reviewing the proceedings. If the transfer is imminent, we can seek limited judicial oversight. They won't give us access to restricted contents. The request must concern inventory numbers, the condition of the seals, the place of deposit, and the identity of whoever authorizes the movement.”

“That will be enough.”

“And you'll have to attach the late entry you made. It's the best proof that you corrected the omission, but it also proves you committed it.”

Kael took longer than he expected to answer.

“Attach it.”

Dalia wrote down the instruction.

“Then let's prepare a request that doesn't depend on anyone believing we're good people.”

***

At the newspaper, Inara found Joren Mal sitting in front of the article she had submitted the night before.

He had changed the headline.

*MUNICIPAL SUPPLIER SUSPECTED OF FALSIFYING CERTIFICATES.*

“I'm not putting my name on that,” Inara said.

Joren looked up.

“The laboratory found a discrepancy.”

“A discrepancy in the provenance of an identified sample. The report doesn't say the supplier falsified the certificate, or who issued it.”

“Didn't you say the documents contradicted one another?”

“I said we could publish them separately. We're mixing a technical result with an accusation that still has no identified author.”

Joren set down his pen.

“Our competitors have spent three days selling this as fraud on a massive scale.”

“And they've spent three days without explaining where their numbers came from.”

“People don't buy our methodological reservations.”

Inara held the page in front of him.

“People can lose their jobs, their contracts, or a judicial investigation because of a sentence we can't prove.”

Joren watched her. Their disagreements had become so frequent they no longer felt like fights. It was a conversation that always began in the same place and ended a little farther on.

“What would you publish?” he asked.

“That a municipal batch contains a sample that doesn't match the specific origin named on its certificate. That we've asked the intermediary to identify the issuer. That the company answered some of our questions, but not that one. And that we're still checking the commercial chain.”

“No workers' names.”

“No sources' names unless they've agreed to be identified.”

Joren rubbed his forehead.

“That belongs on an inside page.”

“Then put it on an inside page.”

The editor pushed the sheet aside.

“And the former inventory clerk you told me about?”

Inara did not answer immediately.

Belor Sen had been dismissed years ago from a company that no longer existed. He could explain procedures and recognize working marks, but he had not witnessed the recent contracts she was writing about. His name should not give the appearance of proof to events outside his experience.

“He isn't a source for that conclusion,” she said.

“But can he contribute anything else?”

“If he agrees to testify about his own records, and if we can verify the provenance of what he still has.”

Joren picked up the revised version.

“How much time do you need?”

“I've asked him to meet.”

“You have until tomorrow's deadline. And I don't want you becoming the guardian of documents no one dares publish.”

Inara gathered her folder.

“Neither do I. I want someone to be able to reproduce the questions when we do publish.”

***

Belor received her in the bookbinding workshop, not at his boardinghouse.

The place smelled of glue, damp paper, and hot metal. A press moved slowly beside the window; at the back, two workers were cutting cardboard for book covers. Belor had chosen somewhere he could speak without being alone with her.

“I don't want you coming back to my room,” he said.

“I wasn't planning to.”

“Then listen.”

He took out a heavy-paper envelope.

Inside was an employment settlement issued when Rhal Equipment closed. It listed his name, years of service, inventory-clerk classification, and personal items given to him as part of his final compensation. One line described a rejected cogwheel from an experimental series.

“You saw that wheel the first time you came,” he said.

Inara remembered the object on the shelf, its polished edge, and the four characters Belor had written on an old newspaper.

“Is this your document?”

“It's my original. I kept it because it was the one thing the company gave me without an argument.”

The sheet was worn along a fold. There were stamped marks and a signature whose authenticity Inara could not verify merely by looking. But the document type and date could be compared with public records of the company's closure and settlements.

“Why show me now?”

Belor indicated the line describing the component.

The internal number contained the same family of reference he had remembered from memory. It was not a present-day order. It was information recorded years before the execution.

“The person handling special orders told me to forget about it,” Belor said. “When the company closed, I thought he was embarrassed about a failed job. Now I see that kind of reference appearing at the harbor again.”

“Did you see it on a recent document?”

“I saw a label on a box. I don't know what was inside. I saw it because I walk past the depot on my way to the workshop.”

Inara did not write *same component* or *same customer*. She noted: *similarity in reference family; later observation with contents unidentified*.

“Why didn't you tell me about the settlement last time?”

Belor gave her a tired look.

“Because last time you could still have written an article that identified me from two details. And because when you lose a job to restructuring, you learn that silence has a price too.”

“Who's charging you?”

“I didn't say anyone was paying me.”

Inara closed her notebook.

“I'm not going to interpret that sentence for you.”

Belor kept a hand on the document.

“I want it on record that I worked there, and what they gave me when I left. I don't want anyone inventing a story about the last seven years because I remember a wheel.”

“We can have that recorded by an independent certifying officer,” Inara said. “You decide which parts can be cited and under what conditions. I don't need to take the original.”

He looked down.

“Can that be done without giving out my address?”

“The office will have to verify your identity. We can request that it remain confidential to the public, and have the document deposited in a sealed envelope. I can't promise that a competent court will never request it.”

Belor studied her for a long time.

“At least this time you don't sound as though you're selling me safety.”

“I can't sell you something I don't control.”

The man put the document back into the envelope.

“Tomorrow. In the morning. If I can change my shift.”

***

Dalia drafted Kael's appeal with a patience he found uncomfortable.

She made him remove adjectives, separate facts from assumptions, and state where he had obtained each supporting record. The request did not claim an office was stealing files. It described the planned movement of two reproductions, an unresolved custody incident, and an order that did not identify the receiving custodian.

“Why don't we add that the service address appears in other records?” Kael asked.

“Because a matching address doesn't prove it's the same person. We can request preservation of that address's history, but we can't present repetition as proof of authorship.”

Kael nodded.

It was the kind of distinction he would have demanded from any apprentice at the Archive.

When they finished, Dalia pointed to the final line.

*The appellant requests limited preservation of the identified records until the person materially responsible for the transfer is established, without opening or disseminating restricted contents.*

“This is the measure we can defend,” she said. “If it's granted, the documents won't become yours. They'll remain where they belong, in the custody determined by the competent body.”

“That's what I want.”

“Are you sure? If someone accuses you of blocking the transfer, you'll have to explain why you exposed yourself to an additional hearing.”

Kael remembered his empty desk, the gray badge, and Tessa's face as she signed the witness record.

“I'd rather explain a written objection than try to reconstruct a handover with no recipient.”

Dalia gathered the sheets.

“Then let's go.”

The review office had a public counter separate from the Archive's registry desk. It received the petition, numbered the attachments, and returned a stamped copy. An employee confirmed that the appeal would be communicated to the custody office and the compliance officer.

She did not promise it would be granted.

Kael also asked that Tessa be notified of any hearing, but did not ask her to act outside ordinary procedures.

On their way out, Dalia stopped on the staircase.

“One more thing. Until this is resolved, don't ask anyone at the Archive to take out a copy to help you. Even if they offer.”

“I won't.”

“The best way to preserve evidence is not to turn it into evidence of another violation.”

***

Inara was offered three tables before they found one where Belor could put down his envelope without crossing a crowded room.

The private certification office occupied two rooms above a bookshop. It was authorized to record voluntary statements, verify identities, and retain original documents against receipts. It could not transform a statement into conclusive proof of a crime or grant immunity to anyone who testified.

The certifying officer was named Seris Hal.

“If you deposit the document, it remains yours,” she explained to Belor. “It will be identified in our custody and released only under an authorization you sign or a valid legal request. We can certify that we received a document with particular characteristics. We can't guarantee that everything it says is true.”

Belor looked at Inara.

“Are you going to read my statement?”

“Only the portions you authorize me to use to check facts. I don't need an unrestricted copy.”

Seris prepared two documents. The first was a record of deposit of the original, describing the paper, visible seals, and its condition. The second was Belor's statement about his duties at Rhal, the defective wheel he had received, and the limits of his memory concerning later orders.

Inara waited in a separate chair while they verified the worker's identity.

She could have tried to hear everything. For years she had believed persistence was the most important virtue in her profession. Now she recognized another: knowing when to step away so someone could speak for their own protection, rather than to fill an article.

Belor took almost an hour.

When he came out, he held a copy of his receipt. The original settlement remained in an envelope identified by number, date, and signature. The certifying officer also held a restricted statement. Inara received only a certificate of scope: supporting records existed for employment and the delivery of a component under an internal number; any link to recent contracts remained unverified.

“You can't accuse anyone on the strength of this,” Seris said.

“I know.”

“And if you mention the existence of this statement in a public article, you must make sure the person giving it has authorized that.”

Inara looked at Belor.

He shook his head.

“Not yet.”

“Then I won't.”

Outside, the man stopped beside the bookshop.

“They told me registered statements can't be erased,” he said.

“They can be corrected or challenged,” Inara replied. “But the original submission remains on record, along with any later change.”

“That's what I wanted to know.”

“Why?”

Belor took a moment to answer.

“Because you can lose a sheet of paper. You can lose your voice, too. That isn't the same as never having said anything.”

Inara wanted to ask who he was afraid of. She had asked twice in previous conversations, and the answers remained incomplete.

“If you need help finding a lawyer, I can give you a list of free legal-assistance services,” she said.

Belor accepted the sheet she offered.

“Don't promise me anything else.”

“I won't.”

They parted on the stairs.

Inara watched him disappear into the market crowd. He looked like a worker returning to his shift. She had no way to tell how much of his silence he was choosing and how much he had been taught to endure.

***

The response to Kael's appeal came that same afternoon.

It was neither reinstatement nor acquittal. The reviewing body ordered that the condition and location of the identified reproductions be preserved until the custody hearing. Any transfer would require a reasoned authorization, an outgoing inventory, the recipient's name, and preservation of the electronic history. Restricted contents would remain inaccessible to Kael.

Dalia read the ruling beside him.

“It's a provisional measure,” she said. “It doesn't resolve the disciplinary issues.”

“I understand.”

“The office can request that it be amended if it demonstrates an urgent technical reason.”

“I understand.”

The lawyer set down the paper.

“You don't look pleased.”

Kael was pleased in a way he had almost forgotten: a decision no longer depended entirely on the service that would execute it.

“I want to know whether they received it before the transfer,” he said.

“The reviewing body sent it by registered courier and through the case-management system. We can request the acknowledgment. We can't enter the storage room and check ourselves.”

“Let's request it.”

By nightfall, the second notice arrived: the conservation supervisor had acknowledged receipt. Both inventory numbers remained in storage with their seals on record. The scheduled movement had been stopped.

Kael sat on the edge of a chair.

The measure did not prove anyone had wanted to destroy evidence. Nor did it make the Archive safe forever. It had prevented a specific change of custody while that custody was under review.

Dalia handed him the acknowledgment.

“Keep this with the papers from your case. It's yours.”

Kael held it between his fingers.

“I thought I might have to take something out of there to keep it from disappearing.”

“And that would have given the administration another action to investigate.”

He nodded.

He was disturbed to realize how badly he had wanted to do precisely what he was being accused of doing.

***

At the newsroom, Inara prepared an article that mentioned neither Belor nor the Rhal cogwheel.

She used the unrestricted summary of the municipal test, the public figures from the Kharven guild, the contract certificate, and the intermediary's written replies. Every sentence had a source the newspaper could explain without compromising a personal informant. The figures indicated a discrepancy still open to commercial explanations; the laboratory supported a more precise finding of incompatibility in one batch.

Joren read the draft.

“This isn't the article I asked for.”

“It's the one I can prove.”

“And the former inventory clerk?”

“He made a private statement. He hasn't authorized publication. Its contents aren't part of this article.”

Joren put down his pen.

“Then what did you spend the day on?”

“Helping someone preserve his documents without handing them over to me. And finding out which part of his history I couldn't publish.”

“That doesn't pay for a page.”

“It might save us paying for a correction.”

The editor did not smile.

“You know the problem with investigating like this, Inara? You always find a reason to wait.”

“Last time I waited because I didn't have a sample. Then I waited because I didn't have an answer from the supplier. Now I have both. The article's ready. What's not ready is a name to accuse of forgery.”

Joren looked at the page.

“I can publish this. But it'll be the last edition in which your investigation doesn't move beyond the documents.”

“The documents have moved forward.”

“For you. Not always for the reader.”

Inara thought of the woman from the dispensary, the drivers losing hours of work, and the plates someone installed because of what a certificate said. Readers needed to know why a component's origin mattered without learning the jargon of administrative offices.

“Then let's change the opening,” she said.

She wrote a new introduction about the consequences of installing a component whose description could not be traced back to its origin. She did not claim a failure had occurred for that reason. She explained why the contract required a verifiable chain.

Joren read it again.

“That I understand.”

The article went to typesetting under a smaller headline than either of them would have chosen weeks before.

Inara felt she had surrendered something. Not the truth of the facts, but the hope that publishing them would be enough to resolve them.

***

Kael left the review office the next day with a new summons.

He was required to attend a hearing concerning his registration omission and the validity of the transfer orders. The preservation ruling did not restore him to his job. He still needed a defense, and the institution might conclude he had breached his duties even if the documents remained intact.

Dalia explained the deadline.

“There'll be time to prepare witnesses. Don't pressure Tessa. If she decides to testify, we'll summon her formally.”

Kael nodded.

In the entrance hall he found a public noticeboard. Someone had posted an announcement about new measures for preserving disputed administrative documents. It mentioned neither his case nor the inventory numbers.

He read it without pleasure.

He had wanted an institution where a record was enough so nobody would have to defend it personally. The past weeks had taught him that a procedure could be correct on paper and fail because of a decision with no name attached.

He stopped at a lending desk and requested a catalog from the School of Surveying that he was entitled to consult as a citizen. The librarian reminded him the volume was available at the public branch, not here.

“Thank you,” he replied.

He was not going to enter another room through the wrong door.

Outside, a vendor was calling out the edition of the newspaper Inara had just sent to press. Kael bought a copy to read on the tram.

He found an article about traceability in municipal supplies, written with care. He did not know the journalist personally. He nevertheless recognized the difference between identifying an anomaly and deciding what explained it.

He put the paper away beside his summons.

The two documents might concern different problems.

The duty not to destroy the trail of a decision did not belong to any one office.

***

In midmorning, Inara went looking for Belor to check that he had received his copy of the deposit record.

She did not want him to lose the very thing he had risked giving a statement to preserve.

The boardinghouse manager received her in the corridor. She recognized the journalist and asked her not to go upstairs.

“He's not here anymore,” she said.

“Did he change rooms again?”

“He left.”

Inara felt as though the next question was too large for her.

“When?”

“Last night. He paid what he owed and handed over the key. Took a bag of clothes and his toolbox.”

The manager sounded irritated, not afraid. The room had been left clean, and Belor had caused no trouble with his rent.

“Did he say where he was going?”

“No. I asked in case any mail came. He said there wouldn't be an address to forward it to.”

“Was he alone?”

“When he gave me the key, yes.”

Inara looked toward the staircase.

“May I see his room?”

“No. It's ready for another tenant.”

The refusal was reasonable. No crime had been reported, and she had no order allowing her to enter. Nor did she have the right to photograph someone's belongings simply because his absence worried her.

“Did he seem all right?” she asked.

“Tired. Like he usually was. Why do you care so much?”

Inara could have explained her investigation, but a conversation with the manager was not permission to reveal a witness.

“Because I needed to confirm he received a document,” she said.

The woman frowned.

“I don't know. I'm not his secretary.”

Inara thanked her and left.

In the street, she had to stop beside a wall. She had imagined that if something went wrong, there would be a forced door, a threatening note, or an account clear enough to publish. What she had was an empty rented room and a woman saying a guest had paid his debt.

That was not proof of kidnapping.

Nor was it the end of her questions.

***

The certification office confirmed that Belor's deposit remained intact.

Seris Hal checked the number Inara showed her and spoke from behind the counter.

“I can't give you his statement or tell you what personal instructions he left when depositing the document,” she explained. “I can confirm that the original remains under our seal and that we've received no valid order to release it.”

“Did he ask to cancel the deposit?”

“I can't provide any information beyond its preservation status. If there's a safety concern, contact the competent authorities.”

Inara thanked her.

It was frustrating, and it was the protection she herself had recommended the day before.

On her way out, she crossed the transporters' square. There were several passenger-coach offices and a freight desk. She asked whether anyone remembered seeing Belor depart, describing his appearance without revealing that he had given a statement.

One vendor shook his head. Another said a man like him had boarded an intercity coach late in the afternoon, but was uncertain when. Inara recorded the observation as unverified.

At the third counter she found a ticket collector who knew the former inventory clerk from the neighborhood. The woman remembered seeing him the previous night with a bag and his toolbox. She said she'd greeted him as he boarded a coach on the northern route, departing Vael-Tir at dusk.

“Do you know where he got off?” Inara asked.

“No. My shift ends at the terminal. The driver and passengers carry on.”

“Was he traveling with someone?”

“Not when I saw him. Other people were aboard.”

“Did he look as though he was being forced to board?”

The ticket collector looked surprised.

“He bought his ticket and sat down. I didn't see anything unusual.”

Inara wrote down exactly that.

She did not know whether Belor had chosen to leave out of fear, for work, because of an offer, or for reasons unrelated to her investigation. His departure was confirmed by someone who knew him. His destination was not.

And Belor owed her no explanation.

At the terminal, the next coach was already taking passengers. A family boarded with two baskets; a man argued about the baggage charge. Nobody seemed to know that someone whose words mattered to two separate stories had just left the city.

***

Inara returned to the newspaper with a copy of her notes, the receipt for the published article, and no new name to write down.

Joren was finishing an argument with the advertising manager. When they were alone, he received her without getting up.

“What happened?”

“A source who agreed to give a statement left Vael-Tir last night. We have confirmation that he took an intercity coach. We don't know why or where he went.”

The editor straightened.

“Was he threatened?”

“I have no evidence of that.”

“Did he take the documents?”

“The documents he deposited with a certifying officer are still protected. We can't use them without authorization.”

Joren looked at the newspaper page they had just printed.

“Do you want us to publish his disappearance?”

“No. Not yet. A private citizen leaving town doesn't become news because it's convenient for us. And an article might identify him as a witness to people who don't know he gave a statement.”

“Then what do we do?”

Inara laid out the checks she could substantiate without revealing Belor's name: public documents already consulted, the unrestricted technical response, questions sent to the supplier, and the status of the published article. Beneath them she left space for material whose use depended on consent or a lawful request.

“Continue with what we have,” she replied. “And keep a record of what we don't know.”

Joren examined the sheet.

“That won't bring him back.”

“No.”

The newsroom window shuddered as a tram passed. Outside, a delivery boy was collecting copies of the new edition. The article about supplies was already circulating.

Inara thought of the clean boardinghouse room and an envelope she could not open out of respect for the person who had left it.

She had made sure a document no longer depended on her notebook or one person's memory.

She had not made sure that person stayed in the city.

She sat down to work.

For the first time, keeping a name out of print was not a concession to fear. It was the only way not to turn an absent witness into the property of her investigation.

***End of Episode 19.***
