# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 15: False Coordinates

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, third-person limited.

***

The first door Kael Veyran tried to open after his suspension had no lock.

It was the entrance to Vael-Tir's Public Cartography Room. Two glass doors, a plaque displaying opening hours, and a sign listing reproduction fees. Nobody asked him for an employee badge. They asked whether he wanted to consult current maps or historical collections.

The question left him motionless for a second.

He had spent so many years obtaining documents by virtue of his position that he had almost forgotten how to seek an answer when he had authority over no one.

“Published historical collections,” he said. “Survey indexes for freight stations.”

The clerk handed him a form and directed him to the payment counter.

“Original charts from before the territorial reform must remain in their folders. You can read the facsimiles and request assistance with the tables.”

Kael paid the fee.

He had not used the blue badge he had left on the Personnel office table. Nor had he asked Tessa to retrieve anything from the Archive for him. The summons to his disciplinary hearing was still in his pocket, alongside a sheet on which he had recorded events he had witnessed and receipt numbers he was permitted to retain.

His formal investigation was on hold.

His ability to read a public catalog was not.

In the main room, a couple argued over the boundary between two inherited properties. A surveyor was preparing a lecture for students. Near the window, two workers spread out a sewer plan because a street had been flooding for weeks after every rainfall.

Kael found the section devoted to old unloading stations.

He had come because of a phrase in the published technical manual Tessa had shown him before his suspension: *dual-origin references*. The chapter on old validators warned that a maintenance signature could contain both an installation identifier and an alignment reference. A modern office might preserve both without knowing how to interpret the second.

Kael had verified the signature family. He had not identified its issuer.

He wanted to understand what an alignment reference was before asking again about an authority nobody recognized.

***

The first booklet had been published by a college of surveyors that no longer existed. It explained how a station could use local coordinates different from the city's: a distance measured from its own origin plate, a bearing relative to a calibrated axis, and a third quantity that did not always represent height.

Kael read it twice.

In modern atlases, the third figure usually expressed elevation. In old industrial systems it could indicate the level of a platform, a tolerance band, or the position of a tool within an assembly.

Without the reference plan, three numbers did not constitute a place.

A dark-haired woman with graphite-stained hands came over to collect a ruler.

“Are you looking for the meaning of a system, or for a particular site?” she asked.

The badge on her chest read **Lina Ores, Reading-Room Assistance**.

“I don't know yet.”

“That answer causes trouble here every day.”

Kael showed her the manual's cover, not his notes from the judicial case.

“I'm looking for public tables explaining what the third value of an alignment reference used to mean.”

Lina checked the year of publication.

“There's a collection of concordances. Some were published for bridge and depot maintenance crews. Others never got beyond internal instructions.”

“I only need the published ones.”

She pointed toward two volumes on a low shelf.

“Then start with those. And be careful with the word *coordinate*. Maintenance books use it for more than one thing.”

Her precision reassured him.

In the first volume he found ordinary leveling tables. The entries could be converted to known elevations. In the second was an index of references for facilities dismantled before the last reform.

Some pages were covered with printed corrections, not censorship: the authors had amended the tables and published an errata sheet at the end. Kael checked every affected number before copying it.

One row stopped his hand.

It was a reference designated **fourteen, eight, three**.

It appeared beside no street name. Nor was it attached to any particular station. The table classified it as an *alignment point not convertible by territorial cartography*.

Kael carried the volume over to Lina.

“What does ‘not convertible’ mean?”

“That depends on why it was written. It may be a system whose origin point hasn't survived. It may be a workshop measurement that never referred to an outside location. Or it might be an error no editor wanted to correct.”

“And how would I tell which?”

“Find the original source and check whether another table translates it. Don't treat the note as proof of something extraordinary.”

Kael copied her answer almost word for word.

In the margin of the book was a reference to an old chart of industrial routes. That edition was not held in the room, but the public catalog identified where a copy had been deposited.

A nautical instruments shop in the Lower Docks had donated facsimiles years earlier.

Kael recognized the donor's name: Orven Dal.

***

The reading-permits officer reminded him that reproductions took time.

“We can make a copy of the page,” she said. “You can collect it tomorrow.”

“Can I keep a transcription today, with the edition number?”

“Of course. Just don't copy the volume's seals and present them as office certifications.”

Kael looked up.

“I wasn't going to.”

“You'd be surprised how many people do.”

On the way out, he stopped in the corridor displaying modern maps. One covered nearly the entire wall: Vael-Tir, its neighborhoods, the river, protected areas, old industrial compounds, and the network of roads leading toward neighboring regions.

He looked for point fourteen, eight, three.

It was not there.

The search was absurd. The reference did not belong to the map's grid system, and he had just read as much.

What troubled him was not the absence of a point. It was that some technician had once had a reason to preserve an address that later generations could no longer convert.

Kael returned to the table and wrote a distinction in his personal notebook:

*A repeated number is not a destination. A missing conversion rule is not proof that the destination does not exist.*

Then he went to the harbor.

***

Orven Dal was repairing a dockside compass when Kael entered.

“You're not wearing the Archive coat,” he said without looking up.

“It's my coat.”

“Then the Archive ought to pay you for making it look like a uniform.”

Kael did not respond. He was getting used to the shopkeeper's jokes and still lacked the energy to compete with them.

“I'm looking for an old chart your family supplied for reproduction to the Public Cartography Room.”

Orven set the compass on a cloth.

“Which one?”

Kael gave him the title and year. The man frowned.

“That belonged to my father. A register of equipment transport routes, not a map for traveling.”

“Do you still have the original?”

“The original's where it's always been. Why do you think we supplied a facsimile instead of the book?”

Orven pointed to a wooden cabinet. It had a large lock and a notice warning against moisture.

“I'm not asking to borrow it,” Kael said.

“Good. Because I wouldn't lend it to you.”

“I want to know whether I can check a reading from the facsimile in front of you. The public room won't release copies until tomorrow.”

Orven studied him.

“What reading?”

“An alignment reference.”

The shopkeeper swept metal filings off the table with his hand.

“The three-armed mark?”

“I don't need to interpret the mark. I only want to know whether the figures in the register were printed exactly as they appear in the original.”

Orven took his time answering.

“I remember you,” he finally said. “Last time you came looking for a provenance and ended up talking to someone you didn't know.”

Kael thought of Lyra, the awning in the rain, and the rejected-lots ledger.

“I can't speak for other people,” he said. “I'm asking about your book.”

Orven unlocked the cabinet and took out a stiff cloth-bound folder. He did not place it in Kael's hands. Instead, he opened it on the counter and kept a weight on each corner.

There were convoy diagrams, load tables, and marks drawn to show how sensitive components should be secured. Some symbols were simple storage warnings. Others referred to procedures no longer practiced.

Orven turned several pages.

“Here's the table. My father used it to work out which vessel could carry a piece of equipment without changing its calibration position.”

Kael pointed to the reference.

It was not written as it had been in the cartography booklet. The chart used a nominal bearing, a displacement, and an adjustment band. Alongside the row for an industrial assembly appeared the words **fourteen, eight, three**.

The number had not been added by hand. It was printed in the same ink as the rest of the register.

“What does it indicate?” he asked.

“I don't know,” Orven said. “A working position, I suppose. Or a way of packing something. My father would have known.”

“Did your father copy this register from a public manual?”

“He bought tables from the company that did the testing. We used them whenever there was delicate cargo. That doesn't prove the company invented the figures.”

It was another necessary limitation. The two publications could derive from the same older source.

Kael requested permission to note the page number, edition, and declared provenance of the chart. Orven agreed, then closed the book.

“What's so special about three numbers?” he asked.

“So far, that a municipal table and a commercial chart preserve the same sequence under different descriptions.”

“That sounds like what happens when workshops buy the same instructions.”

“Yes.”

Orven leaned his elbows on the counter.

“Then you still haven't found what you're looking for.”

“No,” Kael admitted.

He had learned to appreciate people who did not congratulate him too early.

***

At Pier Three, Ema was reviewing a parcel manifest with the expression of someone who had spent hours correcting other people's mistakes.

Kael introduced himself without suggesting that he represented a public office.

“I'm no longer working on the case as the assigned analyst,” he told her.

“Did they fire you?”

“I'm under precautionary suspension. It's an important administrative distinction.”

“When it comes to getting paid, it usually isn't.”

Kael let the remark pass.

“I'm looking for Lyra. I need to ask her about a component she showed me during our last conversation.”

Ema closed the manifest.

“Do you have an order?”

“No. It's a personal inquiry. You don't have to answer if you don't want to.”

The young woman studied him. Then she pointed to a bench opposite a window.

“Wait there. I can't promise she'll come.”

Kael waited.

He watched a driver arrive arguing about an unloading fee he had paid twice. An employee explained that the weighing office had changed its form and that the driver could claim a refund with his receipts. The city kept solving problems that no conspiracy needed to create.

The smell of wet timber drifted through the open doors. For a moment, Kael remembered that before the execution his most pressing concerns had been finding misfiled documents and getting home before the last tram.

Lyra appeared as the sun began to touch the harbor roofs.

She wore neither a hood nor Orven's parcel.

“Ema says you lost your permissions,” she said.

“The ones attached to my assignment.”

“And now you're doing the same work on your own?”

“I'm here to ask about something you own. I can't examine it without your consent.”

Lyra leaned against the table.

“What thing?”

“The brass plate you collected from Orven's shop. It has a mark of three strokes and a calibration series.”

Her eyes narrowed.

“How do you know about the calibration?”

“Orven showed me a reference to parts of that kind in a register. I haven't seen yours in full. At the harbor you mentioned a family of marks and said you recognized it in certain workshops.”

Kael paused.

“I shouldn't have claimed that your plate has the same series. I suspect it from what you said about the mark.”

Lyra did not correct him.

“What do you want to measure?”

“The distance between three consecutive marks. I need to know whether they correspond to an alignment pattern in two old publications.”

“Will you tell me which?”

“Yes. They're publicly accessible. I've noted the title, edition, and page.”

Lyra took the notebook he offered and read the references.

“Do you want to borrow the plate and take it to a laboratory?”

“No. Only to compare it here with a printed scale, if you agree. I don't need to dismantle it.”

“What for?”

Kael chose his words again.

“Because two records using the same alignment direction might be copies of a standard. A calibrated component manufactured through a different process could help establish whether that direction was used physically.”

“That wouldn't tell you where the component was.”

“No.”

“Or whether anyone used it afterward.”

“No.”

Lyra watched him for a long moment.

“When you say you don't know something, at least you don't seem offended.”

“I usually am,” Kael replied. “I just try not to show it.”

She gave an unexpected laugh.

Then she handed his notebook back.

“Come back at nightfall. No one from the Archive or the court.”

“I wasn't going to bring anyone.”

“Say it anyway.”

***

The waiting had a cost.

Kael had to appear first thing the next morning to review his own suspension file. The Personnel representative had authorized him to do so as an interested party, not as an employee with general access. If he missed the appointment, it could be taken as a decision not to respond within the allotted period.

He bought paper, an ordinary ruler, and a small drawing compass at a nearby stationery shop. Nothing he carried could produce an arcane reading. The procedure he intended to repeat consisted of comparing physical distances against a reproducible printed standard.

The third quantity remained a problem. In the tables it might designate height, band, or working phase. If the plate came from equipment using another scale, reading its marks as coordinates would be an unintentional falsification.

He found a dry bench and reread the booklet until he reached the conversion note.

The instructions said the scale had to be read from the reference edge, not from the center of the figure. A footnote warned that an incomplete plate could produce three apparently coherent values even if it belonged to a different device.

It was reason enough not to get excited.

Kael copied the warning alongside his other notes.

A coastal vessel passed the pier. Its wake lifted a smaller boat and struck the stone wall. The metallic sound of a chain reminded him of the window from his childhood; not the whole memory, only the difference between something that vibrated and something struck by a blow.

He let the memory pass.

When darkness fell, he returned to Ema's table.

***

Lyra was waiting for him with a folded cloth.

“Don't touch it yet,” she said.

She spread a palm-sized brass plate on the table. Its edges were worn. Three strokes surrounded an open dot, and a line of small calibration marks grew closer together toward one end.

Kael recognized the family of components from Orven's register, but could not certify that this was one of them.

“Do you know where it came from?” he asked.

“Orven gave it to me as part of a lot he said he'd kept from his father. That's not a complete chain of provenance.”

Her reply had precisely the caution he would have used.

“May I place the ruler beside the edge?”

Lyra nodded.

Kael laid the ruler on the table without touching the component. He compared the first interval against the scale in the public booklet and read the next two. Then he turned the paper, not the plate, and repeated the procedure to check that he had not confused left and right.

The conversion produced fourteen, eight, three.

He looked up.

Lyra seemed to be waiting for a question.

“The sequence matches,” he said.

“With two books.”

“With two records. And with a component whose provenance we still can't establish completely.”

Lyra covered the brass again with the cloth, but did not take it away.

“And where is that point?” she asked.

Kael remembered the map in the public room.

“I can't locate it. The table says not to convert it using current territorial cartography.”

“Did you try?”

“Yes.”

“Despite the warning?”

“I wanted to see what error it produced.”

She raised an eyebrow.

“That sounds dangerously close to a good idea.”

“It gave me a meaningless coordinate. I wouldn't treat it as a real place.”

Lyra looked down at the cloth.

“Some instruments indicate positions that aren't streets or mountains.”

“I know. An assembly bench can have internal coordinates.”

“And some marks seem to indicate a place when they're really describing a relationship between two things.”

Kael said nothing.

The statement was compatible with the manuals he had consulted. It was also too vague to use as evidence.

“Are you saying you know the system?” he asked.

“I'm saying you shouldn't go looking for that figure on a city map.”

“I already established that.”

“Then for once you're ahead of the work.”

Kael took a deep breath.

“I'd like a record that you allowed me to examine the plate. I don't need your surname or the origin of your other belongings. Just a description of what I measured and the date.”

Lyra considered the proposal.

“You can write that I was present. Don't sign on my behalf or say I handed the component over to you.”

“Agreed.”

“And don't publish a reproduction of the complete marks.”

“I have no intention of publishing them.”

“Intentions change when someone loses a job and needs to prove they're still useful.”

The remark hurt more than he expected.

“I'm not trying to get my position back at your expense,” he said.

Lyra met his gaze.

“Then make sure you don't do it tomorrow.”

***

Kael did not sleep much.

At home he opened the notebook containing the three supporting sources and separated the data from the conclusions.

The first was an industrial cartography table published for public consultation. The second, an old commercial chart kept by Orven, with its edition and declared source identified. The third, a physical plate Lyra had authorized him to measure under specific conditions.

He could not claim the three were independent. The public table and commercial chart might reproduce the same standard. The plate might have been made according to that standard, demonstrating physical use of the sequence without proving its provenance.

Nevertheless, all three sources agreed under documented reading rules.

The result was not a matching pair of trading names. Nor was it the graphic similarity of three strokes. It was a metric sequence that kept the same order when transferred from paper to an object.

Kael drew a table with three columns and wrote beneath it the only conclusion he could support:

*The reference fourteen, eight, three appears consistently in two printed sources and one calibration component. The nature of its spatial frame remains undetermined.*

He thought of Daren. The inspector did not need another hypothesis presented as criminal evidence. The coordinate might eventually help identify a device or a route; it proved nothing about the execution.

He thought of Tessa. He must not ask her to search for the same figure in the Continuity Collection. His suspension remained in force, and a colleague searching in his name would be precisely the shortcut he had promised to avoid.

He turned off the lamp.

The next morning he would return to the Archive only to exercise his right to defend himself.

Tonight, at least, he could allow himself a small conclusion: someone had preserved a technical reference for generations, and that reference had not lost its coherence as it moved between different media.

***

The Personnel representative received him at the appointed time.

He was allowed to examine only the disciplinary file in which he was a party. It contained a list of the actions attributed to him, the date his response had been added, and a deadline for replying in writing. None of its pages authorized him to open unrelated collections or use a work badge.

Kael took notes on his own case.

On the last page was a new appendix: the institution had asked the conservation department to describe how documents were moved under service addresses not associated with named individuals. It was a limited measure. It did not identify the authority responsible for the Continuity Collection.

Kael requested a copy of that directive as an interested party in the proceedings. The clerk explained which portions he could receive and which remained restricted because they belonged to other cases. He accepted the limit and signed the receipt.

On his way out, he found Tessa in the public entrance hall.

“I can't discuss your case outside the consultation room,” she said.

“Neither can I.”

The archivist glanced at the notebook he was carrying.

“Are you using your days off to learn something more pleasant than regulations?”

“Cartography.”

“That doesn't sound more pleasant.”

“There are old reference systems that don't correspond to any identifiable point on a modern map.”

Tessa studied him.

“Work-related inquiry?”

“No. Public reading. I'm not going to ask you for any document.”

She seemed to relax.

“Then I can recommend something already published: the School of Surveying's catalog of retired measurement systems. Its index is organized by alignment family, not by location.”

Kael wrote down the title.

“Thank you.”

“Don't thank me until you've seen how badly organized it is.”

Before they parted, Tessa pointed to the folder containing his own case.

“Don't forget to respond before the deadline.”

“I won't.”

He left the building without attempting to enter a restricted room.

***

The School of Surveying had closed its old technical cabinet decades before, but its public library still held the printed indexes Tessa had mentioned. Kael was able to consult them that afternoon using an ordinary reader's card.

The catalog contained no secret map.

It contained instructions for identifying the type of reference from three pieces of information: the system of units, the class of origin, and the number of axes.

He searched the ordinary surveying tables first. None matched the fourteen, eight, three pattern exactly.

Then he reviewed workshop scales. Several admitted three values, but the third identified a fixed elevation or a position inside a machine. The way the sequence was written in Orven's commercial register did not match those conventions.

In a section on obsolete systems he found a note he initially thought irrelevant.

*Dual-origin references must not be translated into a single point until the relationship between the two frames is known. The absence of a territorial conversion does not imply that the record is corrupt.*

Kael read the sentence again.

It was not a cosmological revelation. A machine might use one moving part and one stationary part as origins. A pier might take the current and the jetty's position as references. There were many ways to obtain a consistent direction that did not indicate a location in an atlas.

But all three sources he had found used precisely the warning about a dual origin.

The correspondence suggested a narrower working hypothesis: the sequence might be incomplete because the second frame was missing, rather than because one of the numbers was wrong.

He looked up the index of associated references.

No city was named.

There was a compatibility entry referring to a series of calibrators withdrawn from service. The card was classified as ordinary technical bibliography, but the volume it cited was no longer held by the school.

The last line identified where it had been transferred.

Kael knew the designation.

**Administrative Continuity Collection.**

He closed the catalog and rested his hands motionless on the table.

He had reached, by a public route, the same documentary boundary that had cost him his duties. This time he had not touched a single restricted series and could account for every consultation with the corresponding receipt.

The reference did not tell him where the point was.

It told him why the city map was not enough.

And the only document that could provide the second origin was held in a collection whose responsible authority nobody had yet identified.

***End of Episode 15.***
