# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 27: The Cost of Returning

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Lyra Saren and Kael Veyran, third-person limited, alternating only at scene breaks.

***

Lyra Saren had often imagined what she would do if she reached another shore.

She had never imagined the first thing would be finding a chair for someone who had just lost an afternoon.

Kael stood beside the observation window at the Port of Quiet Winds, his notebook open on his knees. Selivar's wind pushed the salty air against the low frames. Across the terrace, the five lights he had measured were beginning to fade into the brightening sky.

“Don't try to get it back now,” Lyra said.

Kael looked at the page.

“I'm not trying to get it back. I'm establishing what I can't remember.”

He could repeat the facts he had written before the journey: rain, glass, metal, wood, orange, the woman who had raised him. But when he tried to revisit that childhood room, the emotion and the lived scene were gone. The page was intact. The memory was not.

Lyra knew the temptation to offer a comforting explanation. She carried gaps of her own from an old test. She had learned to dress them up as professional mysteries because it was easier to believe someone had concealed evidence than to accept she might never recover part of her experience.

“The doctor needs to examine you,” she said.

“You've already told him.”

“I'll stay until he comes.”

Kael closed the notebook and pressed it to his chest.

“Do you remember the afternoon I wrote about?”

“Only what you told me. I wasn't there.”

“Then don't tell it back to me as if you saw it.”

An unexpected pain tightened behind Lyra's ribs.

“I won't.”

An operator from Selivar appeared in the doorway. She carried a blanket, a record sheet, and a message that the doctor was preparing an examination room. Lyra helped Kael stand, though he could balance without her.

As they walked, they saw two workers putting away ropes and closing a toolbox. Nothing about their movements was extraordinary. It was the same care with which workers in Vael-Tir closed a shift so another crew could begin.

Lyra had crossed into another Domain.

And the first thing she needed to protect there wasn't a route.

It was Kael's right not to pretend what he had lost still belonged to him.

***

The Port's doctor was named Tomer Ild.

Before asking questions, he introduced himself, showed his clinical authorization, and explained what information would enter the test's safety record. Kael agreed to the documentation of symptoms but requested that the full description of his childhood memory stay in the restricted clinical annex, not the operational report.

“The safety office needs to know there has been a memory disturbance,” Tomer said. “It doesn't need to know what you ate that afternoon.”

Kael appreciated the precision.

The first questions were ordinary: his name, approximate departure time, places visited since arrival, and the order of the measurements. He answered without difficulty. He recognized Lyra and remembered taking an instrument from Eren Mal and obtaining readings that differed slightly within permitted tolerances.

Then Tomer asked him to describe the loss without consulting the notebook.

“I know I was with the woman who raised me,” Kael said. “I know which house it happened in. If you ask what I felt while listening to her, I can repeat the sentence I wrote before Transit, but I can't feel it again.”

“Are other afternoons difficult to recall?”

Kael thought of a winter argument, a fall in the yard, and a meal shared years later. The images were imperfect but still carried emotional weight.

“Not in the same way.”

Tomer marked a box.

“We can't guarantee the loss is limited to that. Nor can we promise it will return. The record made before departure helps establish the difference, but cannot recreate the experience. I'll recommend monitoring and another assessment after the return.”

“Could I stay here until I feel better?”

“You may choose not to travel while we assess your condition. The connection has a limited operating window, and our resources cannot support two visitors indefinitely without another residence agreement. If you decide to return, we must establish your fitness. I won't declare you fit merely because you're in a hurry.”

Kael glanced at the door.

He did not want to stay in Selivar out of fear of losing more. Nor did he want the crew to spend energy returning him to a life whose dearest part had become foreign to him.

“I want to understand the risks of returning first,” he said.

Tomer nodded.

“That's appropriate.”

***

Selivar's station kept an independent reserve in a room opened only by its operators.

Lyra did not enter. She watched the check from an authorized corridor beside Eren Mal. The Port's custodian explained that receiving two travelers had consumed part of the containment capacity. The margin was recovering within the expected interval, though it had not yet fully recovered.

“We have enough reserve for two individual return cycles,” he said. “Not a third test. Each cycle requires approval after we check the previous one.”

“What if one of us can't travel?” Lyra asked.

“We'll provide supervised accommodation and open a case for residence and later transport. We won't consume the other's reserve out of desperation.”

“Is the point of origin still responding?”

Eren showed her a recording strip. The latest message from Vael-Tir confirmed that the Anchor on the northern hillside remained within reception limits. His crew had checked the certificate for the strategic battery charged on Eryndor before both outward journeys. That battery would not power the return. It would serve only for containment and reception at the origin, within its own reserve.

Lyra traced the marks.

“Does the regional authorization cover both returns?”

“The operation annex provides for them, subject to clinical review, validation at both ends, and time limits. This Port has also signed its local authorization. Neither replaces the other.”

“And the destination?”

“We'll verify it again immediately before each cycle. A reference valid yesterday may become unusable if the connection's stability changes.”

Lyra looked through the inner window toward the arch.

The metal looked no different from what she had seen upon arrival. It was the people measuring its limits who prevented it from being mistaken for an open road.

“I want Kael to return first,” she said.

Eren did not answer immediately.

“We'll determine the order by medical assessment and available power, not one traveler's wishes. If he's fit and consents, we can consider it. You'll be examined too.”

Lyra lowered her eyes.

She still struggled not to speak as though protecting someone gave her the right to decide for them.

“You're right,” she said.

***

Kael requested a second sheet before signing.

The return form was written in two registration systems. One described the obligations of the Port of Quiet Winds; the other, those of the northern hillside station. Destination references were held in a sealed annex that operators could verify without handing it to travelers.

Tomer had written his assessment: oriented, capable of consenting, with a localized autobiographical disturbance according to the initial examination and no signs of acute injury. He recommended monitoring during and after the return. He did not claim the danger had ended.

“How much reserve remains to receive us?” Kael asked Eren.

“Ours will power departure from here, one person per cycle. The origin crew reports sufficient power and dissipation to receive two scheduled arrivals, separated by recovery. Both operator teams control the documentation. If a check fails, we don't depart.”

“And if the first person arrives but the origin station can't confirm readiness for the second?”

“The second stays here. There's no authorization to force another cycle.”

Kael reread the paragraph on memory effects. Another loss was possible. Nobody claimed that a memory already gone was automatically protected from further damage.

Tomer let him ask one question without the rest of the crew.

“Does it make sense to risk crossing again?” Kael asked.

“That's not a decision I can make for you. What I can say is this: you're conscious, you understand the situation, and current controls allow a return to be prepared within established limits. The risk isn't zero. If you choose to stay, we'll care for you while arranging another option.”

Kael thought of Dalia Ferren, the copies preserved in the Archive, his hearing still awaiting a decision, and the house where he had written down the memory he could no longer inhabit.

His life was more than one afternoon.

But he couldn't pretend that afternoon had not mattered.

“I'm returning,” he said. “I want it recorded that I'm not doing so because I think the journey is harmless.”

Tomer looked at him.

“I'll record that.”

***

Before preparations began for the first return, Lyra received a message from Sena Vard.

It did not come from a restricted case file or hidden chamber. It arrived over the information channel the operators used to coordinate both ends, with personal transmission authorization appended to the log. It carried no objects and revealed no other route.

Sena had reviewed the repair report with Myra. The manual correction would be issued with a provisional warning, but some officials proposed limiting it to the specific northern hillside unit. Sena believed the authority needed to hear those who had witnessed the out-of-range return reading before deciding whether other stations holding similar supplements should be inspected.

*The technical hearing is in three days,* the message read. *I signed the warning, and I'll testify. I don't need you to confirm wrongdoing you can't prove. I need you to describe the moment we agreed to stop the diagnostic test. Will you stand with me?*

Lyra held the paper for several seconds.

She had answered Sena's first warning while still in Vael-Tir. She kept the dispatch receipts and her personal notebook. This was different. Sena was putting her name before an authority that could decide how other equipment would be reviewed. She wanted help defending a safety decision they had both supported.

Lyra requested a fresh sheet.

She wrote by hand:

*I'll be at the hearing. I'll testify only to what I saw and know. I won't leave you alone to answer a question that I also have a duty to answer.*

She added Selivar's date and the incoming message number. She gave the reply to Eren for authorized transmission. She requested an acknowledgment and kept it with Sena's earlier letter.

It was not an impossible promise. If she returned within the scheduled window, she could attend in three days and describe an inspection she had witnessed. If she fell ill or the return failed, she would have to send notice immediately rather than leave Sena waiting for help that never came.

Kael appeared in the doorway.

“Everything all right?” he asked.

Lyra folded the copy.

“Sena needs me at a hearing. I promised her. When we get back, I need to tell her we're on our way.”

“Want me to write it down?”

“It's already written.”

She smiled, almost embarrassed by how much she had learned from him.

The smile faded when she saw the notebook he held against his body.

A record could preserve words.

It could not guarantee that after another crossing they would still feel like her own.

***

Kael made the first return crossing.

Lyra stayed behind the observers' line, a low-power communicator on the table and Sena's letter in her coat. Eren had received a fresh confirmation from Vael-Tir: Galen and Myra were at their stations, the receiving Anchor had dissipation available, and the permit limits remained valid. Selivar's reserve had been measured after the arrivals, not merely estimated from a historical figure.

Tomer gave limited medical approval.

Kael stood before the arch.

“I don't know whether I'll ever remember my afternoon again,” he said quietly.

Lyra answered from within earshot.

“I won't promise you will.”

He looked up.

“Thank you.”

The cycle began with a restrained vibration. The crew held the natural Threshold within the verified reference. No bolted door appeared, no corridor through which they could see the city of origin. The surface seemed to measure space differently, and Lyra's breathing shortened as she remembered the first departure.

Kael waited for the signal.

He walked without stopping.

His figure vanished from the stabilized interval, and the bench began its controlled discharge. Eren ordered the transfer load closed and containment maintained while the dissipator absorbed the losses.

Nobody asked to start the second cycle.

Vael-Tir's confirmation took several intervals.

When it arrived, Lyra did not move.

*Traveler received. Conscious and oriented. Medical observation begun. Origin installation recovering before another cycle.*

She read the words twice.

They did not say Kael had regained his memory.

Nor that he had lost another.

They stated only what the receiving team could establish.

Lyra sat for the first time since the procedure began.

Her knees were trembling.

***

At the northern hillside station, Kael reappeared on his feet.

Galen and another technician held his forearms as Myra ordered the reception passage cleared. He had crossed while conscious but needed to sit before answering the Eryndor doctor's questions.

“Full name.”

“Kael Veyran.”

“Departure point.”

“Port of Quiet Winds. Selivar.”

“What do you remember of the last few minutes?”

“A signal. Eren's voice. The arch. Then these hands.”

The doctor recorded the answers.

He did not ask about an idealized childhood. He requested a specific sequence of observations at the Port, a measurement Kael had repeated, and a recent detail from his conversation with Lyra. Kael answered. Then he opened his notebook to the rainy-afternoon page and confirmed he still could not enter that memory.

The second journey had restored nothing.

He could not immediately detect any new gap.

“That doesn't rule out changes you haven't noticed yet,” the doctor reminded him. “We'll follow up. Don't certify a complete absence of aftereffects based on one conversation.”

Kael agreed.

Ruva entered once visits were permitted. She carried a copy of the reception confirmation and the acknowledgment that the notebook remained in Kael's personal custody. She did not ask him to explain what he had seen in Selivar.

“The crew still needs to receive Lyra,” she said. “If the margin doesn't recover, she'll remain there. We're not authorized to take energy from another feeder because it's urgent.”

Kael looked toward the door.

“Can I wait here?”

“Yes. But if the doctor orders rest, you'll rest.”

He sat with his hands on his knees.

The room smelled of soap, old wood, and orange. Someone had left a peel on a plate.

Kael recognized the scent and its factual association.

He could not find the woman from his memory.

The loss had not ended simply because his journey home had.

***

Lyra waited in Selivar while the system dissipated the load of the first return.

The room was quieter. Eren had sent one assistant to rest and replaced another after Kael's confirmation. A worker crossed the courtyard carrying an empty toolbox. Through the window, mountains cut the sky into bands no chart of Vael-Tir could describe.

Myra sent a verification request from Eryndor. Receiving Kael had reduced the available margin, but the reading remained within the recoverable band specified by the procedure. The second maneuver could not begin until both ends confirmed readiness with current data.

Lyra watched Eren receive the message.

“If they don't recover the margin, I stay,” she said.

“Yes,” he replied. “Not our preferred option, but it's provided for.”

“And if the authorized window expires?”

“We'll open a residence case. We'll need a new license and reserve. Nobody can invent them tonight.”

The Port's custodian showed her the contingency supply list. There was lodging, water, and medical care. No promise of indefinite support or immediate return. Lyra recognized the same economy of decisions Ema had defended at the docks: a plan was honest only when someone had paid for sustaining it.

She sat by the window.

She thought of Sena.

She clearly remembered writing her answer that afternoon. She remembered the weight of the pencil and the relief of not asking the other woman to bear the hearing alone. The promise was still hers.

She took the message copy out and checked that she had it. When she tried to put it away, her hand trembled.

“Are you ready for your departure examination?” Tomer asked, entering.

Lyra nodded.

This time she let him ask every question he needed to.

***

The second return required a fresh check at the point of origin.

The message came after Galen and Myra confirmed the energy closure of Kael's cycle and the recovery of their dissipator. It did not authorize an extra test; it was confirmation solely for Lyra's reception. Eren checked the code with his Selivar crew and verified that his reserve could fund one more cycle while preserving the required containment margin.

Lyra signed her consent again.

Tomer had recorded her orientation and a memory inventory prepared before crossing. It did not contain her whole life. It listed obligations she could not abandon without harming others: the completed delivery to Highbank, her response to Sena's earlier warning, the new technical hearing, and the message just sent.

“Don't confuse remembering an obligation with reliving the moment you accepted it,” he told her.

Lyra studied him.

“Do you think I'll lose that?”

“I can't know. That's why we put it in writing.”

In the Anchor room, the operator read the authorization and stopping conditions.

Lyra stood on the departure mark.

She looked one last time at the low ceiling of the Port of Quiet Winds. She had not found the Imprint she sought. She had seen a Domain that was not hers and watched Kael learn that truth could exact a cost no document could return.

The signal changed.

Lyra walked.

The sensation differed from the first time. She did not feel herself advancing; she felt an impossible distance arrange itself around her, demanding that her body remain whole. A sound like a taut rope filled her ears. Suddenly she remembered the overheated-metal smell of that old test and the voice telling her to stop.

There was no revelation.

She did not remember who gave the order or what happened afterward.

The pressure ended.

Stone floor met her feet.

Myra took her arm and guided her to the treatment area. Lyra recognized the northern hillside room, Galen, and the light of Vael-Tir beyond the window.

“Name?” the doctor asked.

“Lyra Saren.”

“Where have you come from?”

“The Port of Quiet Winds.”

“Do you remember why there's an envelope in your pocket?”

Lyra looked at the blue thread showing beneath her coat.

“It's a letter from Sena,” she said. “I have to tell her I'm back.”

That was true.

It was not the whole truth.

***

Lyra's examination continued in a separate room.

She had returned oriented, with no acute injuries. She could describe Selivar's crew, the sequence of both return crossings, and Kael's confirmed arrival. She remembered Sena's earlier warning about an undated table, her own written notifications, and why the northern hillside test had been stopped.

The doctor showed her the obligations sheet she had signed before departure.

Lyra read the name of the technical hearing, the date, and the message sent from Selivar.

*I'll be at the hearing. I'll testify only to what I saw and know. I won't leave you alone...*

“Do you remember writing these words?” the doctor asked.

Lyra opened her mouth.

The sentence looked right. She recognized her handwriting and the way she had folded the paper. She knew she had written to Sena and had reasons to support her. But the precise moment of promising was gone.

Sena's question was gone.

Her own decision to answer was gone.

So was the relief she had felt afterward.

“I know it's my signature,” she said. “I understand the commitment. But I can't go back to the moment I made it.”

The doctor wrote carefully.

“Is this different from the older gap you described before traveling?”

“Yes. That one was already there when we left. This conversation happened today, before I returned. I remembered it in Selivar.”

The precision frightened her more than pain would have.

She had not lost a word.

She had lost the experience of giving it.

“Could you ask me the question again?” she said, ashamed of how absurd it sounded.

“I can ask what you remember and record your answer. I can't make the moment reappear.”

Lyra laid the paper on the table.

She had demanded that others accept the risk of remembering less in return for evidence of a larger world.

Now she faced a debt without the feeling of having chosen it.

***

Sena Vard arrived at the station before the light faded.

She did not enter the medical room directly. She requested Myra's permission, presented identification, and left outside the safety folder containing information about other equipment. She carried only one sheet.

“You don't have to see me now,” she said through the door.

Lyra recognized her voice.

“Come in.”

Sena sat without moving too close.

“I received your message from Selivar. I've confirmed your return through the operations channel. Before we discuss the hearing, I want to know how you are.”

Lyra indicated the paper on the table.

“I can read what I promised. I don't remember promising it to you.”

Sena lowered her eyes.

“Do you remember what happened during the hillside diagnostic?”

“Yes. The out-of-range return reading, Myra's cutoff, the two procedure editions. I remember you asking us to check the dates.”

“Then you know what you could testify about if you chose to.”

“That doesn't mean I remember telling you I'd stand with you.”

Sena took time to answer.

“I won't use a signature against you.”

Lyra had expected reproach. The words disarmed her.

“It's not just any signature,” she said. “I wrote it so you wouldn't have to appear alone.”

“And even before you wrote it, you'd decided not to conceal what you witnessed. That's still yours, even if the moment of promising is gone.”

Lyra looked at the sheet.

She could not verify that last claim the way she could verify a seal. But she remembered the diagnosis, the danger, and the decision to stop. She had reasons, here and now, to accompany Sena. She also had a history of abandoning obligations because she confused her own urgency with what others owed.

“I'll read the public hearing file,” she said. “I'll speak to the doctor and whoever advises the witnesses. If I'm fit, I'll go. Not because this signature orders me to, but because I still believe today that what happened deserves to be heard.”

Sena slowly exhaled.

“That's all I need to ask.”

Lyra shook her head.

“No. You need me not to lie. Not even to help you.”

Sena nodded.

“That too.”

***

Kael was waiting in the courtyard when he was permitted to see Lyra.

The station crew had finished closing the load from both returns. Galen and Myra were checking residual containment and preparing the wear report for Eryndor's accumulator, which had not supplied departure power from Selivar. There would be no further operation that night. The exceptional license would be recorded as executed, with reassessment required before any reopening.

Kael had received an acknowledgment for the observation copy destined for the regulatory office, with no complete addresses or information about people outside the test. He kept his witness notebook. The technical originals at both ends would remain under their respective custodians, and any later consultation would have to identify who was asking and why.

“Are you all right?” he asked when he saw her.

Lyra leaned against the railing.

“I can walk, think, and speak. But I lost a moment.”

Kael watched her without rushing.

“Which one?”

She told him what she knew about the message to Sena and what she could no longer relive. She did not pretend her loss was identical to his. Kael's afternoon belonged to his childhood. Lyra's gap lay in a decision someone needed her to keep within days.

“I have the paper,” Lyra said. “I don't remember choosing to write it.”

Kael opened his notebook to the rainy-afternoon page.

“I know I was there. I can't go back either.”

They looked at the written lines.

In the courtyard, a worker coiled a safety hose. A cart waited at the foot of the hill to carry the recording equipment back. The station had succeeded in a limited operation and still needed days to assess its cost.

“Do you think an Imprint could return it?” Lyra asked.

Kael studied the page.

“It might describe it, if somebody recorded it. That would be information. Not the missing experience.”

Lyra closed her eyes.

The answer hurt because it offered no false comfort.

“Sena wants me to testify,” she said.

“Do you want to?”

“I still need to establish I'm fit. But yes, I want to tell them what I saw.”

Kael nodded.

“Then the promise can become a decision again, even if you don't remember making it the first time.”

Lyra looked toward the city.

She had traveled to another world searching for answers about what she had lost. She returned with evidence of the journey and a new gap no route could circumvent.

She could not recover the moment she gave her word.

But she could decide what to do when someone needed her again.

***

Before they went down the hillside, Ruva called both travelers to sign the closure of their participation.

She did not ask them to attest to conditions they had not witnessed. Each signed a receipt, observations, and their own statement of symptoms. Energy, containment, components, and return readings remained in Galen and Myra's technical register; destination verification and Selivar's measurements stayed within the bilateral system defined by the license.

“The exceptional permit ended with the last return,” Ruva explained. “We can't reopen the Anchor simply because we managed to get back. The reserves have wear, and the technicians need to assess the supports.”

“Will the station remain closed?” Lyra asked.

“For Transit, yes, pending new authorization. Inspection and security operations will continue under current safety conditions. No permanent closure has been ordered.”

Kael read the consequences section.

It mentioned two autobiographical disturbances reported by travelers without describing their private content. The health office would have to complete follow-up, and the regional authority would assess whether the tolerances accepted were appropriate for another operation.

“Will you record the differences in procedure we found during the first diagnostic?” he asked.

“Yes. Correcting the manual isn't just a filing note. Everyone using that family of equipment must be notified, with a review of which units received the wrong supplement. That decision belongs to the technical authority, not to our personal conclusions.”

Ruva handed him the receipt.

Kael put it with the other papers of his life.

No document could give back a rainy afternoon.

But documents could keep another crew from ignoring a difference they had paid too dearly to understand.

At the gate, Lyra looked back toward the dark building.

She did not need to promise she would return.

For the first time, it seemed enough to know that the door would stay closed until someone could show that opening it again would not demand a price the travelers did not understand.

***End of Episode 27.***
