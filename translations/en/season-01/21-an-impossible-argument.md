# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 21: An Impossible Argument

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, third-person limited.

***

The book arrived with nothing to distinguish it from an ordinary delivery.

The bookseller asked Kael Veyran to sign the receipt, waited as he counted out the coins, and warned him that one of the covers had been repaired. The volume came from the School of Navigation's public library. It bore a lending card, a circular water stain, and a list of errata tied to it with string.

“You can't take it out of the city,” the woman said. “Or remove the plates. If you need to make tracings, they have a table at the school.”

“I'll consult it there.”

“Then I don't understand why you paid to have it brought here.”

Kael looked at the cover. *Atlas of Observations of Eryndor's Firmament: Concordances for Navigation and Orientation.* The edition brought together records from northern and southern stations, corrections for instrumental errors, and an index of patterns visible without magnifying lenses.

“I wanted to confirm it existed before requesting anything else,” he replied.

The bookseller gave him a bewildered smile.

Kael put the book back in its protective sleeve and headed for the school with his receipt.

The previous evening, Lyra had made a claim none of his notes could substantiate. She had spoken of other Domains, other regions of reality, and shown him an old sheet distinguishing a departure reference from an arrival reference. The sheet remained in her possession. Kael had transcribed only the general characteristics she had permitted him to record; he had no right to reproduce a complete address or present the sheet as an authenticated document.

The atlas would provide an independent test of something much smaller.

It would not determine whether Lyra was telling the truth.

It might help establish whether a description of stars matched the sky navigators had recorded from Eryndor.

And before he could make that comparison, Kael needed to know exactly what the description said.

***

The School of Navigation was open to anyone who paid the consultation fee. It was nothing like the Archive: there were no corridors restricted by clearance level, no readers whose permissions depended on their employment. There was a room full of models, shelves of manuals, an instrument workshop, and students arguing about the condition of a sail as though the difference might decide the fate of the continent.

Kael sat at a table beside a window.

The atlas had three sections. The first explained how to measure apparent elevation above the horizon. The second collected positions and magnitudes of fixed lights, corrected for different periods and regions. The third contained diagrams of angular separations: patterns that allowed a reading to be checked without knowing the latitude from which it had been taken.

Kael began with the third.

A drawing of five bright points could look different when its orientation on the page changed. But the angular distances between those points remained the same. Rotating an instrument or traveling to another city changed the horizon, not the pattern the lights formed relative to one another. Time altered their apparent positions in ways that could be calculated; within the observational history covered by the atlas, the corrections were small and had been tabulated.

It was a general principle, not proof of anything about Lyra's sheet.

Kael recorded it as such.

As he finished his second page of notes, a woman arrived carrying a short ladder and a box of lenses.

“You're using the field atlas,” she said. “If you need the instrument corrections, they aren't all in there.”

Her hair was pinned up, and a dark ink stain marked her thumb. Her identification read **Irena Dalce, Instructor of Celestial Navigation**.

“I need to know how to distinguish an impossible configuration from an observation made with a misaligned instrument,” Kael replied.

Irena set the box beside a shelf.

“That depends on what we mean by impossible.”

“That no observer on the planet could reproduce the separations described.”

“That's a stronger claim than ‘it doesn't appear on my chart.’ Do you have measurements?”

“Not transcribed reliably enough yet.”

The instructor regarded him with more professional interest.

“Then start by obtaining them. And don't give an ancient pattern a name you can't yet prove it had.”

Kael felt a familiar discomfort. He had told almost the same thing to people who arrived at the Archive with a mark and demanded a culprit.

“May I consult the correction records?”

“They're public. Fortunately, they even kept the errors. Otherwise we'd teach our students that our measurements had never been wrong.”

Irena directed him to a section.

Kael spent the rest of the morning learning how to calculate the difference a misaligned mirror could produce, how to recognize a reversed drawing, and how certain measurements became distorted when a scale was copied from an earlier edition.

He finished with more questions than he had brought.

For the first time in several days, that did not feel like defeat.

***

Lyra arrived at the Pier Three desk empty-handed.

Kael had asked to speak with her through Ema and had come alone. He did not want word of the meeting reaching Personnel as though he were using his suspension to build a new network of informants. Nor did he intend to disguise a collaboration Lyra was free to refuse as an official procedure.

“I need to see the celestial field on your sheet again,” he said.

Lyra leaned against the doorframe.

“Why?”

“To make a comparison that anyone could repeat if they knew the figures. I don't need to copy the route or the references at either end.”

“And what if the figures for the sky allow someone to reconstruct what's missing?”

“That's exactly what we don't know yet. We could start by agreeing on a reduced transcription: only the relative separations, the order of the lights, and the uncertainty recorded alongside each measurement. If you find that a figure could locate an installation, we'll leave it out and record the exclusion.”

Lyra was silent.

“You want to take the transcription to the school?”

“Yes. As a private document of incomplete provenance. Not a Council report.”

“Will you say where it came from?”

“That I prepared it from a sheet in your custody. That I don't know its earlier chain of ownership and haven't been able to authenticate it.”

“They'll laugh at you.”

“Better that than have them believe I possess a proven original when I don't.”

Lyra turned toward the canal. A tug was moving between two stone-laden barges; the water slowly closed behind it.

“Ema says you spent the morning looking at printed stars.”

“Looking at corrections to errors.”

“That sounds more like you.”

She went to a box stored under the table and took out her folder. She did not allow Kael to open it.

“I'm going to read out the fields first,” she said. “If you recognize a reference that might give away an address, tell me before you write it down.”

He agreed.

Lyra unfolded the sheet and pointed to a pattern of five lights surrounding a dark space. It bore no familiar star names. Along its margins were distance relationships between several pairs, a scale of uncertainty, and a note about the order in which the lights were verified.

Kael did not touch the paper.

“I need the drawing without its orientation on the page,” he said. “We can represent it as relationships among five points. The order of the letters won't identify a route.”

Lyra nodded.

They worked for almost an hour. Kael wrote each relationship on a fresh sheet and then read it aloud so she could catch any mistakes. Where a figure had been partly erased, he marked it *illegible*, without completing the stroke by resemblance to the others.

Lyra allowed him to keep the reduced diagram. Beneath it, she added a brief statement in her own hand defining its scope: she had watched him transcribe the relationships visible on the sheet and reviewed the reading; she did not certify the object's age or origin and did not authorize reproduction of its other fields.

She signed with her first name only.

“Now you can take this,” she said. “Not the rest.”

Kael put the diagram into an envelope. On a separate sheet he noted the date, place, duration of the comparison, and the express restriction on its use.

“One question,” he said before leaving. “Why did you choose to show me this particular pattern?”

Lyra did not smile.

“Because it's the part that still doesn't require anyone to switch anything on.”

***

Back at the school, Kael did not ask Irena to interpret the sheet's origin.

He presented a comparison problem.

“Five visible points,” he said. “A set of separations between certain pairs, an order of observation, and error margins I've transcribed from a private source. I want to know whether a compatible pattern exists in Eryndor's sky within the periods covered by the catalogs.”

The instructor examined the diagram.

“Did you produce this?”

“I transcribed it in front of the person holding the document. The original's provenance is incomplete. The limitations are set out here.”

Irena read the note.

“I can't tell you whether it's authentic.”

“I'm not asking you to.”

“And if I don't find a similar pattern, I won't tell you where it came from.”

“I want you to rule out my transcription errors first.”

Irena laid the sheet beside a measuring glass.

“There's an ambiguity here. These two separations might be measured from the edge of an optical field rather than from the center of each light.”

“I considered that.”

“And this figure?”

“I marked it illegible. We won't use it.”

She looked at him.

“At last, someone brings me a genuine unknown and doesn't ask me to fill it in.”

The first attempt was disappointing.

If the numbers were interpreted as measurements on a plane, several patterns in the atlas looked similar. One group of southern-hemisphere lights matched three points of the drawing. Another, observed near the northern coasts, reproduced two separations.

Neither matched the remaining relationships.

Irena did not declare the pattern impossible. She brought out a calculating rule and transferred the separations onto a demonstration sphere.

“There's a common mistake,” she explained. “Two angles on a flat chart don't preserve all the distances we measure across the sky. If the source uses a different projection, we can manufacture a false pattern.”

Kael asked her to continue.

Over the next hour they tested alternative interpretations. One reversed the reading order. Another treated the figures as distances between instrument marks. A third allowed the same scale correction throughout the field.

Three configurations were ruled out because they were internally inconsistent. A fourth remained mathematically possible but did not match the catalog.

“That still doesn't prove it doesn't exist in our sky,” Irena said.

“What else do we need?”

“Independent coverage. The atlas was compiled from several stations, but its editors could have carried over a common error. We have published observation notebooks from two expeditions that never used these plates. There's also an index of bright fields revised after the reform.”

Kael checked the clock.

His administrative defense appointment was two days away. He could not neglect that preparation. Nor did he have unlimited money for consultation fees and reproductions.

“How much does it cost to examine the public originals?” he asked.

Irena showed him the fee schedule.

Kael calculated how much money he had left until the end of the month and abandoned some of the tracings he had intended to request.

“Let's begin with the expedition notebooks,” he decided.

***

In the School of Navigation's library stood an instrument that seemed too simple for the task.

It was a wooden sphere painted with small metal markers. Students used it to learn that the heavens did not revolve around a city, even though the altitude of lights above the horizon changed with latitude.

Irena allowed Kael to use it in the public room under an assistant's supervision. It required neither a source of the Pulse nor a Resonance reader. The points were printed positions, and a crank turned the sphere.

Kael reproduced five positions corresponding to the pattern in the document, without assigning them star names.

The proportions were strange.

One pair lay close together. Another was more widely separated. The fifth point had to lie at a precise distance from three of the others. It was not impossible to draw on the sphere; the geometry closed under the corrected interpretation. What he could not do was find it among the observed fields of Eryndor.

“Maybe it's an invented constellation,” the assistant said.

“It could be,” Kael replied. “Or a table copied from an instrument that wasn't observing the sky.”

“Then why bother looking for it?”

Kael paused.

At the Archive, he would have answered with a regulation reference. Here he had to find another way.

“Because an invented constellation still has a shape. If someone describes it as a real observation and no independent observation confirms it, we know which claim we mustn't use as proof. And we can ask why they wrote it down.”

The young man turned the sphere.

“Couldn't it be visible from another continent?”

Irena, who had just arrived, answered before Kael.

“Other locations change which parts of the sky are visible and how they're oriented. They don't change the distances between the same lights. Once we examine the full sphere of observations, latitude stops being an excuse.”

“What about centuries?”

“Positions may shift slightly,” she said. “That's why the atlases record periods and tolerances. The correct question isn't whether the drawing resembles an ancient one, but whether its relationships fall within the measurements and their possible changes.”

Kael had the assistant remove the markers whose positions depended on illegible figures.

Eight cross-separations remained among the five points, enough to demand a specific configuration within the recorded margins.

Not enough to assign a place.

Enough for a better comparison than he had attempted the day before.

***

The first expedition had observed the firmament from the northernmost reaches of the shipping routes. The second had spent years recording southern fields. Their notebooks had been published before the modern atlas unified the names.

Kael requested public copies, checked their dates, and noted the methods each group used to measure separations. Some results disagreed. The second notebook corrected an orientation error in an area of faint lights the first expedition had not been able to observe.

That seemed valuable to him.

Two documents agreeing on everything might have been copied from each other. Two arguing over a specific error made it possible to trace the disagreement.

He examined the brighter stars, because Lyra's document described five lights distinguishable without magnification. The modern catalog claimed its compilation covered the bright fields observable from the planet's various regions within the preserved time bands. Kael recorded that claim of coverage; he did not turn it into a law of the universe.

Three times, he thought he had found a match.

The first time, the closest pair matched, but the outer point lay too far from its neighbors.

The second time, he could reproduce the outline of four lights by reversing the drawing; the fifth would have to appear in a space where neither expedition had recorded a light of the stated magnitude.

The third time, all the separations seemed to fit if Kael increased the tolerance of one relationship and decreased that of another. Irena rejected the maneuver.

“You can't change each measurement's error margin to obtain the pattern you're looking for,” she said.

Kael set down his pen.

“You're right.”

He was tired. The window had gone from midday gray to the yellow of late afternoon, and he had not yet eaten lunch. It reminded him of days at the Archive when an incomplete series kept him seated until a supervisor turned out the lights.

The difference was that he used to be paid to stay.

“How many fields do we still need to check?” he asked.

Irena studied the list.

“Those in the revised post-reform index, and a group of altitude observations that might help us eliminate a different convention. Everything else is covered by your selection of lights.”

“Then let's finish tomorrow.”

“That would be sensible.”

Kael gathered his papers.

Irena stopped him.

“I don't know where you obtained this, but I can see you're hoping for an answer more important than a navigational correction.”

“I'm trying to find out whether someone described an impossible observation to me.”

“Did she say it was impossible?”

“She said it wasn't from this world.”

The instructor was silent.

Kael saw on her face the same expression he would have worn at such a claim a few weeks earlier: not fear, but the need to establish whether the person speaking understood what he was saying.

“Then ask a smaller question,” she replied. “Find out whether the pattern can be reproduced from Eryndor's observed data. That's a question our school can answer. The other isn't.”

***

Dalia Ferren received him early the next morning.

Kael had requested a brief meeting to organize the documents for his hearing and keep his new reading from consuming his defense preparation. On the table lay a copy of the ruling that had kept the reproductions immobilized inside the Archive. The measure remained in force, as did the disciplinary proceedings.

“Have you prepared your explanation for the late registration?” Dalia asked.

“Yes.”

“In your own words?”

“My own.”

The lawyer reviewed the statement.

Kael acknowledged that he had registered two institutional reproductions after the deadline and that failing to record their temporary retention immediately had been a mistake. He asked that the true date and the witnesses be retained. He did not try to excuse his failure by claiming others had behaved worse.

Dalia indicated a sentence.

“This suggests you saw an irregular instruction before making the copies. Are you certain that's the sequence?”

Kael checked his notes.

“No. The instruction arrived later. I need to correct it.”

The lawyer returned the page.

“Do it now. A defense can be ruined by confusing a concern that arose afterward with the motive you had at the time.”

Kael corrected it.

He did not mention the astronomical inquiry. It had nothing to do with the subject of the hearing and could divert the review toward questions about his friendships, reading, and private activities.

“Will you request Tessa as a witness?” Dalia asked.

“Yes, if the reviewing body summons her formally. I don't want her to lose her position for meeting me outside the proceedings.”

“The request has already been submitted.”

The lawyer studied him.

“Are you sleeping?”

Kael considered lying.

“Not enough.”

“It shows. Your defense deserves a rested mind, even if the rest of the world's documents keep waiting.”

He nodded.

On the way out, he found an atlas reference card in his pocket that he had forgotten to put away. It seemed proof that Dalia was right.

He bought bread and returned to the School of Navigation only after eating.

***

The observation room was full of students preparing for an examination.

Irena met him beside a shelf of wooden boxes. On the table she had placed the revised index she had promised to consult and a sheet with several marked fields.

“I checked the relationships you transcribed separately,” she said. “I didn't use your informant's conclusion or the notes about the document's possible origin.”

Kael sat.

“What did you find?”

“First, a problem. There are two historically documented ways to read the order of these lights. One measures from a reference point. The other numbers the points in the order in which they appear above the horizon. If we mix the conventions, we can manufacture a pattern that doesn't exist.”

Kael felt a brief lurch.

“Which does the sheet use?”

“We don't know. That's why I tested both.”

Irena showed him the tables.

The first reading became internally inconsistent when the relationships were closed: the distances between three lights could not simultaneously satisfy the recorded measurements. That was not a result against Eryndor's sky. It was a result against that interpretation of the sheet.

The second reading did form a consistent pattern.

It matched no bright field in the atlas, none in the independent observations of the two expeditions, and none in the subsequent instrument review.

Kael read the summary slowly.

“What if the drawing was reversed?”

“I checked.”

“If the scale had changed after a repair?”

“A common scale correction doesn't solve it either. And if we allow different changes for every interval, we're no longer testing the observation the document claims to describe.”

“The date?”

“We made comparisons across the periods covered by the observations and published corrections. The field doesn't fit within their tolerances. A very remote period outside our records would be a different hypothesis, not a correction we could claim to have demonstrated.”

Kael touched the edge of the table.

“And another latitude?”

“That doesn't change the separations between points in the sky. It changes which ones you see and how they lie relative to the horizon. We've compared the configuration independently of that orientation.”

The instructor removed her glasses.

“I can't claim it's physically impossible for a different sky ever to have existed. I don't know the paper's origin, and I won't authenticate it. But if the numbers were transcribed correctly and genuinely describe five lights of the stated magnitude, **they do not describe Eryndor's firmament according to any of the reliable observations and corrections our school can compare**.”

Kael listened to the noises from the room beyond the door: an assistant dragging a chair, someone laughing over a calculation, a bell announcing the end of a class.

Her words had not changed the room.

They had changed how many explanations could still be sustained without forcing them.

“I need someone else to be able to repeat the comparison,” he said.

Irena nodded.

“That's why I prepared a list of the plates, the fields tested, and the exclusion rules. I'll give it to you as an academic consultation note. It doesn't include any opinion about other worlds.”

“I wouldn't ask for one.”

“It also makes clear that the result depends on the fidelity of the private transcription.”

“That needs to be in writing.”

Irena signed the note and added the date.

Kael paid the copying fee and received a copy.

It was not a Council declaration.

It was not a key.

It was a result that could be checked without Lyra's presence.

***

Kael walked to the docks without deciding what he would say.

He could take the school's note to Lyra and ask for another observation identifying the second origin. He could demand the names of those who had created the sheet. He could announce that the calculations proved her claim.

None of those options entirely satisfied him.

He stopped before the Water Service notice. The old path was still closed for repairs. There was a scheduled date for another inspection but no permission to enter.

The closure now seemed more significant to him even though its cause had not changed. That disturbed him.

It would be tempting to interpret every closed door as the defense of a newly discovered truth. The slab had sunk before he had asked a single question about the sky. The coincidence still proved no surveillance, just as the absence of a star from an index did not, by itself, prove that someone had traveled from one Domain to another.

Lyra stood beside a disassembled crane, speaking with a driver. Kael waited for her to finish. There was no need to interrupt.

When she noticed him, she approached with her hands in her pockets.

“Did you find an answer?” she asked.

“I found one I can defend up to a point.”

He showed her the School of Navigation's report, not the documents from his hearing. Lyra read the opening lines.

“There's no destination listed,” she said.

“I didn't request one. The comparison establishes an incompatibility within the known period and coverage of observations. It doesn't authenticate your sheet or prove that an active passage exists.”

Lyra continued reading.

“But it isn't just your opinion.”

“No. The instructor was able to reject one interpretation and test the other against public materials that didn't come from you. We can repeat the work.”

She held the paper between her fingers for a few seconds.

“Then now you know why I couldn't show you a street on a map.”

“I know why the celestial description you showed me doesn't fit Eryndor's catalogs. I still don't know where it was made or who wrote it.”

Lyra gave the report back.

“That's more than you knew yesterday.”

“Yes.”

She looked toward the canal.

“What do you want to do with it?”

Kael had thought of taking the conclusion to his former offices. He had no badge, no authority to examine restricted collections, and no way to prevent the statement from turning into a reckless order. He could also keep it as a private certainty, which would do nothing for the people working on unsafe installations without enough information.

“First, I want to preserve a method that can be challenged,” he said. “Then find out who made the observation. I'm not going to ask anyone to open an installation on the strength of this note.”

“I would have stopped you.”

“I expected you to.”

Lyra smiled.

“Do you believe me now?”

Kael took a moment to answer.

“I believe the record describes something our astronomical cartography cannot explain as an observation from Eryndor. I believe you knew about that incompatibility before I did. The existence of other Domains explains the result in a way I can no longer dismiss out of hand. But I still want material evidence of a connection.”

Lyra accepted the distinction without arguing.

“Then we won't discuss the rest on the dock.”

***

At home, Kael placed Irena's note beside the transcription Lyra had authorized.

He assigned them separate numbers and recorded what he had personally witnessed and what depended on another source. The school's result rested on public observations and described methods; the starting data came from a sheet whose authenticity had not been established. That distinction did not disappear just because the conclusion impressed him.

He inventoried the remaining alternatives.

Deliberate forgery was still possible.

So was a technical document representing a simulation.

An observation from a period outside the known records could not be excluded completely, although it would have to explain differences the school considered far greater than the preserved historical corrections.

And there was Lyra's explanation.

He could not yet demonstrate it through direct experience. But he could no longer treat it as an empty phrase.

Darkness gathered over the city outside his window.

Kael took a pencil and began a second table, separate from his administrative defense. In the first column he wrote *attributed observation*. In the second, *independent comparisons*. In the third, *alternative causes*. The fourth he left blank.

He gave it a heading: *What remains to establish a real Transit operation*.

He did not fill it with place names.

He recorded the requirements Lyra had described and that the old manuals treated as separate operations: a natural connection, a stable Anchor, adequate energy and power, a verified destination, and a competent crew.

Those were material conditions. Finding a pattern of stars satisfied none of them.

Kael set down his pen.

For weeks he had believed the mystery was an address hidden somewhere on a map of Vael-Tir. Now he had two documents any reader at the school could examine, and a conclusion he could not return to Eryndor's maps without changing their rules.

The result did not send him to another world.

It forced him to recognize that a written description did not belong to the sky he knew.

And if someone had recorded it as a real observation, a harder question remained than locating a door:

**Who had been there to look up?**

***End of Episode 21.***
