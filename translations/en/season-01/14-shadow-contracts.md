# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 14: Shadow Contracts

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel and Daren Holt, third-person limited in separate scenes.

***

The truck did not bear the name of the company that had paid for its transport.

Inara Vel watched it cross the depot yard from the opposite sidewalk. It was one of those old vehicles that stayed on the road because their owners knew every mechanic in the harbor. Its cargo bed had been repaired with planks of different ages, its registration was painted above the door, and a paler strip marked where lettering had once been.

The entry log listed it as a vehicle operated by Arven Assembly Services.

On the driver's door, a small identification plate read: *Fleet under management.*

It was not a contradiction. A company could lease vehicles, hire drivers, or manage assets belonging to others. What bothered Inara was that each explanation emerged only after she asked about the previous one.

Helis Rann was waiting beside the transport cooperative's entrance.

“If you're here to photograph the truck, don't make me stand anywhere near it,” she said.

“I didn't bring a camera.”

“You brought a notebook. Sometimes that's worse.”

Inara slipped her pencil into her pocket. They had agreed to meet to consult the public tariff for special movements, not to obtain private manifests. For more than a week, Helis had been explaining that a trading name on a vehicle did not establish who had given an order.

“Did you get the shared-services list?” Inara asked.

“The public portion. Companies declare a responsible operator for each contract. The rest is handled by the offices that pay.”

Helis directed her to the guild's noticeboard, where tariff sheets and safety warnings hung. A new notice stated that precision cargo destined for urban facilities had to arrive with legible seals and separate descriptions of its contents and packaging.

“They changed that after the unloading accident,” Helis said. “At least some of your questions did some good.”

Inara read the notice. It carried neither her name nor the cooperative's.

“Why did you ask me to meet you here?”

Helis pointed to a list of contractors authorized for shared services. Arven appeared twice: once as a temporary assembly provider, and once as a transport operator for precision components.

That was not unusual. A company's activities could encompass both.

The difference lay in the identification column.

“One entry uses Arven's commercial registration number,” Helis said. “The other uses its old asset-management registration.”

“Rhal's?”

“It doesn't say that. And I'm not going to say it for them.”

Inara compared the numbers. The second matched a reference she had found while investigating the purchase of workshops and vehicles from Rhal Equipment. The asset register had been preserved through a lawful transfer; commercial invoices did not always show when the information had been updated.

“Could both entries be correct?”

“Yes. That's what I want you to check before you publish that they're two different companies.”

Helis accompanied her to the exit.

A man was pushing a cart loaded with three toolboxes. As he turned beside the railing, one wheel came off its axle. Two workers walking behind him helped lift the weight without waiting for a supervisor.

Inara watched them move the cart out of the way. It was an ordinary operation, without mysterious codes or ancient seals.

“One more thing,” Helis said. “Penalties for late deliveries are being deducted from drivers' wages, even when the delays were ordered by the people buying the cargo.”

“Do you have receipts?”

“We have complaints filed with the guild. They're not secret.”

“Do you want me to publish that?”

“I want you to remember, when you write about contracts, that some people didn't get to decide which company owns a truck.”

Inara nodded.

The observation seemed more useful than the list of names.

***

Back at the newsroom, Joren Mal had left an empty space on page three.

It was not as large as a front page. It was more than Inara had managed to secure in recent days.

“The supplier replied,” Joren said.

He handed her a typed letter.

Arven Assembly Services confirmed its participation in supply and assembly contracts for public agencies. It denied that the identity of an intermediary established any causal connection with irregular certification. It added that its operations had been audited under the applicable procedures.

The company did not answer which office had certified the origin of the batch questioned by the municipal laboratory.

“At least they put their name on it,” Joren said.

“What I needed was the name of whoever was responsible for that certification.”

“Can we print that they refused?”

“We can say they didn't answer that specific question. Not that they refused to cooperate with the entire investigation.”

Joren sat opposite her.

“You keep making your sentences more difficult.”

“I want every sentence backed by a document.”

“People don't buy the newspaper to read chains of custody.”

“People buy it so they don't have to reconstruct those chains themselves.”

The editor looked toward the ceiling and smiled wearily.

Inara opened the folder she had brought from the docks. She would not reproduce Helis's references or reveal the precise office where a source had obtained a tariff. She had brought other documents: municipal contract awards, Arven's public business registration, and a commercial certificate of asset transfer.

On a separate page she had written three unanswered questions. First, who had issued the certificate. Second, whether Arven had paid directly for the transport or merely arranged it. Third, who had physical custody of the components during transfers between depots.

Joren read the second.

“Why does it matter who paid for the truck?”

“Because a company can organize a service without owning what's being carried. If we confuse the operator with the owner, our investigation becomes an accusation against someone who hauled freight.”

Joren leaned over the sheets.

“And what if the same operator worked at the execution square?”

Inara did not answer immediately.

She had followed that lead from the beginning. The truck registration appeared on a movement slip from the night before the execution; the same company later appeared in municipal purchases of precision materials. The matching name was a matter of public record, but it did not establish what each journey had carried.

“Then we need to know whether the company billed for both jobs,” she said.

“You don't know?”

“I have a supply invoice and a record of a vehicle passing through the depot. That isn't the same thing as an order performed by that company.”

Joren removed the blank sheet from page three.

“You have one more edition.”

“You said that last time.”

“I'll keep saying it until you believe me.”

***

The commercial registry consultation was brief.

The difficult part was figuring out what she needed to request.

Inara did not ask for a restricted file or a client list. She requested an updated certificate of Arven's legal identity and declared activities, including the public history of its branches and registered transfers. The clerk checked the fee and explained that the document would be ready that afternoon.

While she waited, Inara went to the Municipal Contracts building.

Public information about the execution chamber was still limited by the judicial investigation, but the ordinary contract to rent and assemble the platform could be consulted without opening the criminal case file. An employee gave her an extract identifying the main contractor and referring extraordinary work to a separate authorization.

Inara recognized the supplier's name. It was the company Daren Holt had stopped when it tried to dismantle the apparatus before the inspection was complete.

It was not Arven.

Some services had been subcontracted, but the published summary listed categories of expenditure, not the people responsible.

“Can I find out who the assembly subcontractors were?” she asked.

“Those listed in the ordinary contract, yes,” the employee replied. “The ones involved in extraordinary modifications are part of the court file.”

“Is there any way to consult the commercial portion without seeing the evidence?”

“You can request an extract by contractor. The legal office will decide which data can be released.”

Inara filled out the application.

“When will you decide?”

“I can't promise a date.”

“Can I have confirmation that I filed it?”

“Of course.”

The employee stamped the form.

On her way out, Inara passed a small demonstration by transport workers. They had hung a banner protesting deductions for delayed deliveries. One speaker explained that instructions to wait beside a depot did not come from the driver, and responsibility ought to lie with the company that scheduled the cargo.

Inara put away her filing receipt and stopped to listen.

She did not know whether the protest had anything to do with the contracts she was investigating. She did know that her article could not portray transport as a series of names without people attached to them.

***

Daren Holt had managed to persuade a judge to sign a document-production order without mentioning a mining company even once.

It was deliberate.

The court was investigating the execution of Taren Oss and possible tampering with the chamber, not the origin of every industrial supply entering Vael-Tir. To examine documents beyond the case, Daren had to show why they mattered to the mechanism in the square.

The order was limited to work carried out in the forty-eight hours before the ceremony: employees present, entry slips, contracting companies, and tools used to adjust the lower platform.

The inspector brought a copy to the main contractor's storage yard. A judicial clerk and Odel Mar, the expert who had examined the chamber floor, accompanied him.

The manager received them beside a row of dismantled platforms.

“We already supplied the invoices,” he said.

“You supplied those for the rental contract. We need the work records from the final night.”

“Those are kept in another system.”

“Which is why the order also identifies the office where they're held.”

Daren showed him the document. The manager read until he found the magistrate's signature.

“I'll call whoever is responsible for the archive,” he said.

They waited beneath a sheet-metal roof. In the yard, a worker was trying to separate two rusted components with a wrench that was too short. Odel watched him with a concern Daren recognized.

“If that wrench breaks, he'll get hurt,” the expert murmured.

He went over and showed the worker a safer technique. Daren let him. Not everything that happened there needed to be entered in an official record.

Eventually a warehouse supervisor appeared with three ledgers and a box of tool cards.

“The adjustment order is recorded,” she said. “But part of the work was provided by a service company.”

“Which one?” Daren asked.

“There's a reference. The name isn't on the sheet I was given.”

The judicial clerk recorded her answer. Then she asked that the ledgers' provenance and the person who had held them in custody be identified.

The supervisor opened the first page. It showed the register's opening seal and consecutively numbered movements. No torn-out pages were visible, although that was not enough to rule out earlier substitutions.

Daren pointed to the date on the night before the execution.

Two entries appeared in the margin: a toolbox entering the site and a portable lifting system being returned. The lift's inventory number was complete.

Odel compared it with photographs of the cavity beneath the chamber.

“That model can raise a platform from the side,” he said. “But it's also used to level workshop floors.”

“Can you tell us whether it was used in our chamber?”

“Not from the model alone. I'd need contact marks or a statement from whoever operated it.”

The supervisor found the loan card.

The lift had been collected by a driver from Arven Assembly Services, who signed on behalf of an external intervention unit. The card showed the vehicle registration, tool number, and departure time. It did not identify who later operated the tool.

“Does Arven own the lift?” Daren asked.

“No. It rented it on behalf of the main contractor,” the woman answered, showing him the checkout entry. “According to our books.”

Daren requested certified copies of the loan card, the register page, and the return form. The judicial clerk matched the numbers and sealed the reproductions. The originals stayed where they were, with a record of the production.

For the first time, the investigation was not relying solely on a company name appearing in a list. There was an identifiable piece of equipment handed over to a person acting under that company's name, and a contractual reason for its presence.

They still did not know what had been done with it.

***

At the courthouse, Daren arranged photographs of the mechanism beside the tool records.

He did not touch the components that remained under seal. Odel pointed to two parallel impressions beside the lower plate in one photograph.

“They could match the feet of that lift,” he said.

“Could?”

“Yes. They could also match several tools from the same family. I didn't find any adhering material or measure individual deformations that would let me attribute them to one specific unit.”

Daren recorded the limitation.

Then he opened the statements from the guards who had witnessed the square being prepared. None described the lift being used beneath the chamber during the night. That did not prove nobody had used it; the guards watched the main entrance and worked in shifts.

“I need to find Eron Bale,” he said.

The technical supervisor still had not appeared. The first notices had gone to the address provided by the contractor. One came back undelivered. Another was marked received by someone whose signature was illegible.

Daren was not about to call him a fugitive.

For now, he was someone they had been unable to interview.

The judicial clerk arrived with a notice.

“The magistrate won't authorize expanding the order to inspect all of Arven's accounts,” she said. “He says renting a tool for public works doesn't justify reviewing supply transactions unrelated to this case.”

“I agree,” Daren replied.

She raised her eyebrows.

“First time I've heard you so calm after a refusal.”

“I need an order that will stand up in court, not one that lets me inspect the whole city.”

He picked up the platform folder and found the extraordinary adjustment authorization. Its cost-center code did not belong to the ordinary contract. The discrepancy had been identified days earlier and remained without a conclusive explanation.

Daren wrote down what was missing.

He had an identifiable tool and a transport operator. He had an adjustment authorized under a different allocation. To determine whether both arose from the same instruction, he needed one specific accounting entry, not a general audit of the company.

He prepared a narrow request for payment details from that night and identification of the office that had received the invoice.

***

The following morning, Inara found her commercial certificate on the newsroom table.

It showed that Arven Assembly Services maintained a single legal identity covering both lines of business. The old asset-management reference came from a registered transfer of Rhal Equipment's assets; it could be used to trace inherited machinery, but it did not turn Rhal into an active company after its dissolution.

It was an important correction.

If Inara published that two current companies existed merely because the systems displayed two codes, she would be repeating the very kind of error she had been trying to avoid.

She called Helis to explain what she had verified.

“So it's a mistake?” the accountant asked.

“It's a historical reference still used in some records. I don't know whether every invoice was issued correctly.”

“Well, at least that I can understand.”

“I also have a pending request about the square's subcontracts.”

Helis fell silent.

“Be careful,” she finally said. “Drivers don't choose the work each client does.”

“I know.”

“And someone's saying the transport workers have been leaking manifests.”

Inara looked toward the window.

“Who?”

“I'm not repeating it over the telephone. I'm just asking you not to put a driver into a story you don't yet know how to tell.”

Inara did not press her.

After the call, Joren summoned her to his office. He had received an additional commercial response from Arven: the company agreed to be identified as an operator of temporary assembly and industrial transport, but warned that its contracts were governed by different clauses, and that having two clients did not establish a connection between their commissions.

Inara read the note.

“That last part is correct,” she said.

“Do we always agree with the company we're investigating?”

“When it's right about the limits of the evidence, yes.”

“And what do we do with page three?”

Inara closed her folder.

“An article about the lack of supply traceability, supported by the municipal test we already have. Without accusing a driver or attributing the material to a mine the laboratory didn't identify.”

“And the square?”

“I'm not connecting it to the supplies without documents showing the link.”

Joren planted both hands on his desk.

“What document are you missing?”

“Proof of a contract actually performed. Not a registration number or a surname.”

The editor looked at the edition about to close.

“Then that part stays out for now.”

Inara nearly protested. She had worked too hard to accept another week of silence. But she thought of Helis, the drivers, and the time someone had lost their job over an incomplete accusation.

“For now,” she agreed.

***

Daren received the reply from Shared Services with a cautionary note.

The office agreed to certify details of a specific transaction under judicial request, but would not disclose technical details of the installation associated with the cost center. The certificate included the invoice number, payment date, commercial beneficiary, and authorization reference.

It was exactly what he had asked for.

The invoice concerned the extraordinary adjustment of lower access and platform calibration. The payment beneficiary was not the main contractor who had rented out the chamber.

It was Arven Assembly Services.

Daren checked whether the commercial registration number matched the legal identity on the lift's loan card. The numbers were not interchangeable: one identified a company, the other a service document.

Even so, he requested separate verification from the issuing office.

“Does that mean Arven made the modification?” the judicial clerk asked.

“It means Arven was paid for the intervention described. We need to establish what work was done, who authorized it, and who was present.”

“And the cost center?”

“It still belongs to a different system from the chamber rental.”

The clerk pointed to a line.

The authorization section contained a shared-services reference also used for infrastructure maintenance. It did not name the office that had approved the technical work.

Daren felt a measured anger. The file was not empty; it contained too many documents that declared themselves compatible with each other while no one took responsibility for the outcome.

He called the commercial certification office and requested the beneficiary's current legal identity. Then he asked the court to preserve the transaction's original accounting records for possible expert examination.

He did not seek access to the restricted collection Kael had tried to inspect. The archivist had been suspended, and the files remained under institutional control. A criminal investigation should not become a justification for bypassing every other safeguard.

The magistrate received the new evidence and authorized Daren to summon Arven's commercial manager as a witness concerning the extraordinary order. He did not authorize an accusation against the company solely because it had received the payment.

It was a distinction the inspector appreciated, even if it meant more work.

***

He encountered Inara in the courthouse's public corridor.

The journalist was waiting for a response to her request for commercial extracts. Daren carried a closed folder beneath his arm and had already spent the morning answering questions from people who confused suspicion of fraud with a conviction.

“Inspector,” she said. “I have two questions you can answer without showing me the case file.”

“That depends on the questions.”

“Is the court still investigating the modifications to the chamber?”

“Yes. That's a public proceeding.”

“Does the investigation include the contractors who worked on it?”

“It includes the work performed and the people who may be able to explain it. That doesn't mean every contractor is a suspect.”

Inara nodded.

“Then I won't ask what you found.”

Daren regarded her with a degree of suspicion.

“I didn't expect to hear that from a journalist.”

“I'm learning to ask questions that aren't ready to be headlines.”

Inara showed him a copy of Arven's commercial certificate. It was a public document anyone could pay to obtain. Daren read it without touching it.

“What do you want me to confirm?” he asked.

“Nothing from your case file. I want to know whether it would be wrong to report that a company can operate in two different sectors without that proving its contracts are connected.”

“That would be correct.”

“Then I need to keep looking.”

Daren hesitated before speaking.

“And I need to ask you that if you find someone who says they worked in the square, you don't turn their statement into a public interview before it can be formally preserved.”

“You can't forbid me to interview people.”

“I'm not trying to. I'm saying some questions can lose their evidentiary value if a statement changes to fit a news story.”

Inara put away the document.

“And evidence can disappear if nobody knows it exists.”

“That's true too.”

They did not agree on when something should be published. Daren suspected they never would completely.

“What do you do with a coincidence you can verify?” she asked.

“I turn it into a question a witness has to answer under their own name.”

Inara studied him for several seconds.

“Perhaps we're not so far apart.”

They parted without exchanging any documents from the criminal case.

Daren headed back to his office.

***

The commercial reply arrived before the end of the afternoon.

It confirmed that the Arven number in the lift register and the beneficiary of the extraordinary invoice belonged to the same legal entity. There were not two companies being confused because of similar trading names.

Daren laid three supporting documents on his desk: the certified loan card, the adjustment invoice, and the commercial certificate.

The card established that a driver acting for Arven collected a tool during the square's work window. The invoice established that Arven was paid for an extraordinary intervention involving the lower access. The commercial register established that both operations were attributed to the same company.

This did not prove what Eron Bale had done inside the chamber. Nor did it prove that the payment had authorized a criminal act.

But the entire chain could no longer be explained away as a misreading of inherited names.

The inspector called the judicial clerk.

“Prepare a summons for Arven's head of extraordinary services. We also need the main contractor to identify who actually ordered the work.”

“Shall we summon Eron Bale again?”

“Yes. This time include the new intervention reference and any valid workplace address the employer can certify. Without assuming he's in hiding.”

The clerk made a note.

As he closed the folder, Daren noticed a line in the margin of the Shared Services certificate. It was not a restriction code. It was an ordinary accounting notation he had overlooked.

Payment for the intervention had been charged to infrastructure maintenance, although the certified work had taken place in the execution chamber.

Daren already knew that allocation existed. What he had not had until now was the confirmed identity of the party that had been paid.

He opened a second file, concerning interventions at older facilities that the court had requested weeks before. Those proceedings had their own limited scope and remained under comparison.

In the public commercial list of maintenance orders, Arven appeared as an authorized supplier for one of the allocations.

Daren kept the two documents separate.

Participating in two chains was not proof that they formed a single operation. They could be lawful jobs performed by a company with a broad range of specialties.

He leaned over the Shared Services certificate and reread the description of the work paid for in the square.

**Lower access adjustment and platform calibration.**

The accounting office had charged it to infrastructure maintenance.

He did not need to invent an explanation to know what to do next.

He took a fresh sheet and prepared a question for whoever testified on Arven's behalf:

*Who ordered work on a judicial execution chamber to be charged against maintenance for a different facility?*

When he finished writing, another notice arrived from the court. The request to identify the technical officer responsible for the expenditure had been accepted.

The magistrate had signed the demand. Now an office would have to answer under its own name.

The company that had been paid was identified. The authority that had commissioned the work was not.

***End of Episode 14.***
