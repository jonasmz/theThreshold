# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 17: What Lyra Knows

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Lyra Saren, third-person limited.

***

The box was still where Lyra had left it several days earlier.

Ema had moved it away from the ordinary parcels and kept it on a dry bench, far from the dock's soaked ropes. It was small, made of pale wood, with two metal clamps and a medical shipping label that still bore the seal of origin. Nobody had opened it.

Someone had added a second label.

*Delivery not completed. Recipient requests an answer.*

“It came by telegraph last night,” Ema said. “They had enough supplies left to keep the equipment going for a few days. Now I don't know how long.”

Lyra draped her coat over the back of a chair.

“Why didn't you tell me?”

“I did. Twice. You weren't at the boardinghouse or Orven's shop.”

“I was working.”

“That's what you say when someone needs you to stop working.”

Ema pointed to the envelope. The transit office at Highbank had transmitted a note from Niva Arel, the woman running the shelter. They used a conventional breathing-assistance device there for patients who could not easily be moved. The box contained replacement valves and seals. None of the parts generated power. The machine depended on the building's electrical installation and a small charged backup, and a worn component could make it useless even while the electricity kept flowing.

They were not asking for miracles.

They were asking for a date.

Lyra knew the usual route: the north exit, the customs road, the first checkpoint handover, and delivery to local transport. She had suspended it after discovering identity checks nobody could explain. She was not going to hand medical cargo to a driver whose authorization she could not verify.

At the time, the decision had seemed easy to defend.

Now the yellow label seemed to take up the whole table.

“Were they able to get a replacement any other way?” she asked.

“A used gasket, Niva says. It holds as long as they don't increase the load. It's not a solution.”

Lyra read the message again.

Ema had done exactly what she should: kept the cargo sealed, retained the receipts, and informed both parties. The delay belonged to Lyra, not to the girl who had followed her instruction.

“I'll take it,” she said.

“Through the north?”

“No.”

“Then tell me how before you promise it again.”

Lyra looked up.

It was a fair demand. And one she could not yet answer completely.

“I have two sections verified,” she admitted. “I still need to check the crossing and who receives it afterward.”

“That's not a route.”

“Not yet.”

Ema released the envelope.

“Good. That's the first thing you've said today I can write down without lying.”

***

The harbor knew three ways to reach Highbank. The first appeared on every map. The second was used whenever a bridge closed for repairs. The third combined cargo boats with a towpath hardly anyone remembered, because it required changing vehicles twice and was useless for large shipments.

Lyra had used the first two. She knew the connection points and some of the access routes for the third, but not their current condition.

Roads did not remain safe out of gratitude simply because someone had traveled them once.

She started with the navigation register.

A coastal tug still accepted small cargo on the southern canal. Its captain, Salma Venn, held a valid license, insurance for delicate goods, and a crew of two. None of that guaranteed an uninterrupted journey; it established only who would be answerable for the cargo.

Salma was not delighted to see her.

“You haven't asked about that canal in months,” she said while checking an engine hose. “What do you need to get out of the city?”

“A box of declared, sealed medical supplies. I don't need you to evade checkpoints.”

“Then use ordinary transport.”

“Ordinary transport is holding medical luggage at the north exit.”

“If they have an inspection order, they can stop me too.”

“I wouldn't ask you to go through an inspection without presenting the manifest.”

Salma straightened.

“That'll cost you.”

“I know.”

Lyra explained the proposed delivery: the boat would travel as far as the old lock landing, then an authorized cart would take the towpath. There was no need to cross closed technical compounds; the outer service route remained a municipal right of way as long as the water did not rise above the safety mark.

Salma shook her head.

“There's a new barrier on the towpath. I don't know whether it's because of construction or rising water.”

“Who manages the crossing?”

“The water-service crew. Ask before sending anyone through. And don't put me down on the manifest as if I'd approved the second leg.”

Lyra agreed.

Salma gave her a written quote and departure time. She asked for no illicit payment, false names, or impossible promises of silence.

Lyra put away the paper.

She could arrange the first leg.

That was less than she needed, and much more than she had when she woke up.

***

By midmorning she was at the old locks.

The canal ran between stone walls bearing decades of repairs. The water carried branches, foam, and a silty smell that could not quite mask the nearby workshops. A pedestrian walkway followed the outer wall, and steps led down to an operations platform.

The barrier was there.

It was recent, painted iron, with a board displaying two separate notices. The first prohibited carts from crossing a slab under repair. The second permitted pedestrian passage as far as the water-service checkpoint during daylight.

Lyra approached the water warden's booth.

A man was sorting worn valves on a cloth. According to his badge, his name was Olan Mer, and he did not appear interested in discussing old routes with visitors.

“Is the towpath still open beyond the lock?” Lyra asked.

“On foot, as far as the inspection post. Not for vehicles. The slab at the second bend has sunk.”

“How long is the damaged section?”

“Long enough for a loaded cart to end up in the canal if the driver thinks he knows better than the sign.”

Lyra accepted the answer.

“Is there a legal detour for a small box?”

Olan pointed to the municipal map on the wall.

“Along Millers' Street. It's longer and uphill. You'll need a short cart. But it reaches the Highbank post.”

“Is it open?”

“Unless there's a new order. I can't answer for what happens beyond my jurisdiction.”

Lyra copied the route. She let the water warden explain which bridge was being repaired and where the road administered by the river district began.

She could pay for the crossing by water and for transport overland. It would double both the original fare and the delivery time.

On her way out, she saw a woman pulling a vegetable cart up to the barrier. The woman read the notices, turned around, and started pushing uphill. Nobody appeared to make an exception for her.

Lyra helped her over the first few yards.

“Is it always like this?” she asked.

“Since the last rain,” the woman replied. “We used to cross without even thinking.”

Lyra watched the wheels climb slowly over the stones.

A route was not a line drawn on paper. It was the sum of the people who could still travel it.

***

She returned to Pier Three before lunch.

Ema had set another box beside the first. It contained dry blankets that a cooperative wanted to send to the shelter if space allowed.

“The blankets aren't urgent,” she said. “Don't change everything just to make them happy.”

“Replacement parts first. If the weight and manifest allow it, the blankets can go afterward.”

Lyra explained the legs of the journey: Salma's boat, documented transfer at the landing, a cart along Millers' Street, and receipt at Highbank by an identified person from the shelter. It was an expensive, slow, feasible route. It required neither false names nor entry into a locked depot.

Ema listened without interrupting.

“Who's driving the cart?”

“I haven't hired anyone yet.”

“Then you don't have a delivery yet.”

Lyra breathed through her nose.

“No.”

“What if we arrange it today?”

“I'll find a licensed carrier. Not one who'll take my word for it.”

Ema picked up the tariff sheet.

“I can cover part of this from the table's expense fund. Not the rest.”

“I'll pay the rest.”

The girl looked up.

“You don't owe me money.”

“It's not for you.”

Lyra had spent months setting aside money to buy information about an Imprint component that might preserve a verifiable fragment of her history. Every small expense put that possibility further away. She did not need to imagine what she might remember there to feel the weight of giving it up: the object could be a copy, a fraud, or a real lead, and for the moment she could not afford to find out.

She took out two coins and placed them on the table.

Ema did not touch them.

“Why now?” she asked.

“Because I'm the one who told them to wait.”

“You said you'd find a safe way.”

“I'm trying.”

“That doesn't give them those days back.”

Lyra looked at the yellow label.

“No.”

It was the only answer she could give without reaching for an excuse.

***

The carrier's name was Tovel Arin. He had a small cart, two mules accustomed to hills, and a municipal license that could be checked in the district register. His usual work was taking tools to rural houses, not transporting fugitives or anonymous documents.

When Lyra explained the route, he asked to see the landing permit and the box's weight.

“If the road is open, I can reach the Highbank post before nightfall tomorrow,” he said. “I'm not promising to enter the shelter. Delivery happens at the post, to an identified recipient.”

“That will be enough if Niva agrees to collect it there.”

“And if she doesn't, I bring it back. I'm not leaving it on just anyone's doorstep.”

Lyra smiled.

She had spent so long working with intermediaries who seemed proud of leaving no trail that the man's clarity felt like a luxury.

Ema telegraphed the shelter. The response arrived as shadows began to lengthen: Niva agreed to appear at the Highbank post and asked that the box's two clamps be kept intact.

The shipment was registered with the carriers' real names, the category of goods, and the person who would receive the parts. There was no false description designed to attract fewer questions.

When Salma loaded the box, Lyra checked the seal alongside Ema. The blankets traveled as a separate parcel with their own number.

The tug cast off.

The two women watched the vessel move toward the middle of the canal.

“Are you following it?” Ema asked.

“As far as the landing. I want to see the handover.”

“Then take something to eat. You can't live on checking seals.”

Ema offered her half a loaf of bread wrapped in paper.

Lyra accepted it.

Sometimes trust took a form so small that naming it felt embarrassing.

***

The first leg went well.

The tug had to slow for branches accumulated near the lock, but it arrived before the cargo crossing closed. Salma handed the box to Tovel against a receipt. He checked its number and the condition of its packaging without opening it. Lyra signed as the handover intermediary and kept a copy.

There was no pursuit, no secret signal making the road easier.

There was rain.

It began while Tovel was securing the cargo and grew heavy as they reached the Millers' Street incline. The road was narrow, the cart moved slowly, and one of the mules slipped on a smooth stone.

Tovel stopped the vehicle.

“We wait until it eases,” he said.

Lyra looked up the slope.

“We could unload the box and carry it ourselves.”

“We could,” he replied. “But I'm not moving the mules until they have traction again. If you take the box, that's a new transfer and you'll have to sign for it.”

“Then I'll wait.”

“I thought you were going to argue.”

“I hired a carrier because he respects the limits. It would be absurd to ignore him on the first hill.”

Tovel laughed.

They waited under an overhang of stone. Lyra shared the bread Ema had given her. The driver told her he had lost a cart years earlier after accepting directions from someone unfamiliar with floodwater. Since then he charged more for journeys involving changes of route.

“Some people call me a coward,” he said.

“That's cheaper than another cart.”

“And much cheaper than a life.”

The rain let up. Even so, they waited a few more minutes, checked the wheels, and resumed their journey.

Lyra could not make the road shorter by needing it to be.

***

The Highbank shelter occupied a stone house beside an old rest station. Niva Arel was waiting at the post as agreed. Her hands were rough from constant washing, and she carried a notebook in which she had recorded the parcel number before they arrived.

“Late,” she said when she saw Lyra.

There was no greeting.

“Yes.”

Niva checked the seals, opened the box in front of Tovel, and confirmed that the valves matched the requested model. A shelter technician carried the supplies into the building. Nothing would be installed before the parts were examined.

“How are the patients?” Lyra asked.

“One had to be moved to another house. We managed it yesterday. The others are stable as long as the auxiliary equipment keeps working.”

Lyra lowered her eyes.

“I could have sent it sooner.”

“You could,” Niva replied. “It could also have been held at the north exit. I have no way of knowing.”

The answer was harsher than an accusation because it offered no way to absolve herself by comparison with an imaginary disaster.

“Ema told me you found a used gasket.”

“We installed it to buy time. Now we're going to replace it. What I need from you isn't an explanation of why you were late, but for you to tell me when you decide you can't keep a date.”

Lyra opened her mouth.

Then closed it.

“You're right.”

Niva checked the receipt, signed it, and gave Tovel a copy.

“Can the driver take the blankets up to the house?” she asked.

“Yes,” he said. “That parcel is registered to the shelter.”

Lyra helped unload it. In the entrance hall there was a food table, and three people were folding clean bandages. An older woman was resting by a window. They were not the image of an unpaid debt or entries in a file. They lived here.

The technician returned after testing the replacements.

“The line is responding within tolerance again,” he told Niva. “We'll leave the backup charging and repeat the test before the night shift.”

The woman in charge nodded.

Lyra felt relief, but did not confuse it with repairing what had happened during the delay.

“Is there anything else to do?” she asked.

Niva held up her notebook.

“Yes. The next time you're afraid of putting someone at risk, tell us. We can make decisions too.”

***

They returned to Vael-Tir without the box.

During the journey, Lyra sat near the edge of the cart, listening to the regular clatter of its wheels. At last she had completed a delivery that did not need to be clandestine. She had paid for a verifiable route, put her commercial name on a manifest, and asked others to accept a risk that had been described.

Not long ago, she would have regarded that way of working as reckless.

Not because paperwork guaranteed justice. She knew better than most how easy it was for a register to describe a different operation from the one actually carried out. But the absence of records was no form of innocence either.

She thought of Kael Veyran.

The last time she saw him, he had asked to examine a brass plate and then agreed to leave it where it was. He had not demanded that she prove its origin or called the court to force her to answer. Later she heard from Ema that the Archive had removed him from his duties.

He had not used that news to claim special privileges.

Lyra was accustomed to recognizing what people wanted from others. Her own interest in Kael had not been innocent either. At first, she had needed to check which case files were circulating again, and whether the archivist might find a reference she had pursued long before meeting him.

She still needed that reference.

What was new was that she also cared about the possibility of drawing him into a route whose cost he did not understand.

The brass plate had brought back the same sensation she experienced when she first uncovered it on Ema's table: the imagined smell of hot metal and a voice ordering a test to stop. She remembered that moment, and that the order had not been obeyed immediately. She did not remember what followed. She would not invent it because she needed to justify her fear.

But she knew something that did not depend on the gap in her memory. She knew the operating conditions of the old Anchors. Finding a stone entrance or learning a calibration sequence was not enough. A Transit operation required a real connection, a verified destination, energy reserves, stabilization, and people capable of keeping the system running without damaging it.

Most of the routes in her notes were not in usable condition.

Some no longer led to any functioning installation.

Others could kill someone who trusted an old drawing.

The number Kael had found in three different sources was important. It still did not tell her whether anyone could use it.

That distinction could save his life.

***

Ema was closing the parcel register when Lyra returned.

Lyra showed her the receipt signed by Niva, Tovel's delivery record, and the total cost. Ema examined them without rushing.

“You spent more than we had,” she said.

“I paid it.”

“I can see that.”

“What's wrong?”

The girl pointed to a name.

“Salma says an inspector asked about the lock route while they were unloading. Not about our box. About old service routes.”

Lyra felt her stomach tighten.

“Did she identify him?”

“She didn't get a close look. He asked the water warden and left. That's all.”

It was little. It could have been a legitimate inspection prompted by the damaged slab. It might also have been something else. Lyra was tired of pretending every coincidence was a threat, while also knowing that names could circulate long before anyone realized what they were being used for.

“What did you tell him?” she asked.

“Nothing. I wasn't there.”

Lyra nodded.

Ema closed the book.

“You haven't asked what I'm trying to tell you.”

“Then tell me.”

“The man from the Archive came yesterday. He asked whether you'd received my message. He didn't ask for names or addresses. I told him you were away and that if you wanted to see him, I'd let him know.”

Lyra looked toward the dock entrance.

“Will he come back?”

“I don't know.”

“And what do you think?”

Ema planted both hands on the counter.

“I think you've spent days trying to decide for everybody. For Niva, the drivers, me, and now that man. If you're going to trust him with something, don't turn it into a test he isn't allowed to refuse.”

Lyra heard in those words an echo of their conversation when the first delivery was postponed.

“I could put them in danger,” she said.

“Then show how much danger there is. Don't dress it up as a little outing.”

Ema turned off the table lamp.

“And don't use my name to make him believe you.”

***

Kael was in the public cartography room when Lyra found him the next morning.

Three notebooks lay open on his table, alongside a copy of a measurement chart. He wore no uniform or Archive badge. When he saw her approach, he closed a volume to clear a chair.

“Have you come to tell me I interpreted the plate incorrectly?” he asked.

“I'm still considering that possibility.”

“Then this is a promising visit.”

Lyra sat down.

She had thought of a dozen ways to speak to him. Every one began with an evasion.

“I owe you an answer,” she finally said.

Kael set down his pen.

“You don't owe me access to any of your belongings.”

“I'm not talking about the plate. You want to know whether the references you found correspond to something that could once be traveled.”

The archivist studied her.

“First I want to know what kind of references they are.”

“It's a way of asking the same thing from the safer side.”

“It's a way of not imagining a place where there are only three numbers.”

Lyra could not keep herself from smiling.

“There's a service path I can show you. It's physical, older than the harbor reforms, and still accessible from a municipal passage. I'm not going to tell you it reaches the destination in your tables, because I haven't established that.”

Kael kept his hands on the notebook.

“Why show it to me now?”

Lyra remembered the box on Ema's bench and the deadline she had allowed to pass.

“Because I've made the mistake of assuming that protecting someone gives me the right to make every decision for them.”

It was not the whole answer. She also wanted to know whether Kael could read the reference points without turning them into certainties. And she wanted to check how much of her old maps still corresponded to stone, water, and metal.

She did not deceive herself about those motives.

“What conditions do you have?” Kael asked.

“We don't enter closed areas. We don't move mechanisms. We don't activate any equipment. You won't identify my contacts in your notes, and I won't ask you for Archive documents. If we reach somewhere unsafe, we turn back.”

“And if I observe something relevant to a criminal case?”

“You can report what you saw. I won't ask you to lie.”

“Are you authorized to bring a visitor?”

“On the section I'm proposing, anyone can walk during the permitted hours. Beyond it there's a barrier we won't cross.”

Kael looked at the maps spread before him.

“Then I'll be able to establish for myself that the path exists. Nothing more.”

“Exactly.”

Silence settled between them.

“Why do you think I'll agree?”

“Because you want to prove you know when to stop, too.”

Kael closed his notebook.

“What time?”

***

They set out while the sun still lit the canal.

Lyra avoided the fastest routes. She took Kael through streets lined with houses and open warehouses, where people could easily identify the path they were following. They rode a tram to the old locks and continued on foot along the outer edge, where the sign permitting pedestrian passage was still in force.

Olan Mer was in the water warden's booth. He recognized Lyra and greeted her with a brief wave.

“Don't take any carts through,” he warned. “The second bend's still in bad shape.”

“We're walking as far as the inspection post,” she replied.

Kael read the notice. Lyra gave him time.

The path narrowed between a row of trees and an old wall. Beneath it, water could be heard passing through a partly closed gate. Every few paces there was a stone bearing maintenance numbers. Some were worn; others had been replaced by newer plates.

“This appears on the navigation charts,” Kael said.

“On some of them. The newer plans show the canal, but don't always mark where the crew walks when the main road is closed.”

“And you know it because you've worked here?”

“I know people who've maintained equipment in this section. And I've walked it before.”

She did not add that she had traveled through other, much less public entrances. It was not necessary to describe this stretch.

They reached an inspection platform open to the river. It had mooring rings, height marks, and a staircase leading down to a closed iron gate. Beyond it, a stone structure was built against the wall. No door stood ajar.

Kael studied the layout.

“It isn't marked as a station on the modern map.”

“Because it no longer operates as a station,” Lyra said. “The pedestrian path still does.”

Water struck beneath their feet.

Kael leaned forward to inspect the level markings. Lyra caught his shoulder before he placed a foot on a loose section of the edge.

“That support is broken.”

He stepped back at once.

“I didn't see it.”

“That's why we look twice when walking somewhere unfamiliar.”

The archivist recorded the defect and its approximate position relative to the platform, not the address of anyone who lived nearby.

Lyra leaned against the sound railing.

“This is as far as I can take you today.”

“And the gate?”

“It's a technical access. We have no permission to open it, and I wouldn't tell you it was safe to cross even if we did.”

Kael regarded the stonework.

“So you showed me a route, not a destination.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Lyra noticed he had not asked whether she had brought him here because of a number.

“Because a route needs to be walkable before someone ventures to use it as evidence of where it leads. Today you can say this section exists, that it reaches a maintenance platform, and that there's a closed access. You can't say what's behind it.”

“Or that it explains my references.”

“Not that either.”

Kael rested a hand on his notebook.

“This is the first time someone's shown me something and explained with such precision what I'm not authorized to conclude.”

“I hope it doesn't become a habit.”

The humor came out harsher than Lyra intended.

***

They were walking back along the path when they came upon two municipal workers beside a wheelbarrow. One held a roll of warning tape; the other studied a board bearing an order to close part of the passage for safety.

Lyra stopped.

“Are you closing the path?” she asked.

The older man looked up.

“Only beyond the inspection post. There's a structural inspection scheduled for tomorrow. The main lock's still working, but nobody can use the old section until they're done.”

“Who requested the inspection?”

“The Water Service. I don't know who wrote the report. The supervisor's bringing it this afternoon.”

The men stretched the tape along the outer side of the barrier. They did not try to drive visitors away from the section of path that was still open.

Kael noted the announced inspection date.

Lyra looked toward the closed gate one last time.

It was not a sign that someone had discovered them. The path needed maintenance, and she had seen a sunken slab that very morning. The coincidence could be entirely ordinary.

Even so, the decision to show him the place could not be undone.

“Do you think the closure has anything to do with our visit?” Kael asked as they resumed walking.

“I have no evidence of that.”

“But it worries you.”

“Yes.”

Kael put away his notebook.

“We can go back without trying to find out today.”

Lyra felt an immediate relief, and a small embarrassment at feeling it.

“That would be sensible,” she said.

“Then perhaps we ought to try it sometime.”

They walked to the lock without speaking.

For the first time since meeting the archivist, Lyra had chosen to trust him with one real stretch of her world without demanding anything in return.

She had not given him a destination.

Nor had she promised that he could reach the end.

***End of Episode 17.***
