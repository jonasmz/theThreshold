# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 06: A Symbol Without an Origin

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, third-person limited.

***

The first time Kael saw the figure, he thought someone had laid a wet tool on the paper.

It was a small mark in the margin of a medical report: three short strokes around an open dot, like the legs of an unfinished measuring instrument. It was not part of the typed text. The ink was gray, almost faded, and the copy had lost part of its lower edge.

Doctor Mara Ellin had sent the report through the ordinary judicial channels. Daren Holt had added a note: *Don't interpret it before you call me.*

Kael set the sheet aside and read the conclusions first.

The dental measurements did not match those of Taren Oss. The interval of death remained uncertain because the body's prior preservation affected the estimates. Tissue samples remained in the custody of the municipal laboratory. None of those findings was new.

On the last page, however, there was a list of observations concerning the support on which the remains had been received. An assistant physician had recorded a repeated impression on the inner lining of a piece of packaging, beside an old inventory mark.

The description read: “Three-armed figure, no match among known municipal seals.”

Kael held the copy up to the window.

His first impulse was to call the laboratory and ask why a mark on packaging had been included in an autopsy report. His second, more sensible impulse was to check whether the detail appeared in the appendices.

It did not.

The packaging was not among the evidence transferred to the Archive. Nor was there a photograph attached.

Daren answered on the second call.

“Did you see the mark?” he asked.

“I saw a reproduction that might not be faithful to the original.”

“That's a very you way of saying yes.”

“Where's the item?”

“Under judicial seal. Mara separated it from the remains storage when she saw the impression.”

“Why did you send it to me?”

Daren took a moment to answer.

“Because something similar turned up on a plan of the mechanism in the square. And because before you tell me what it means, I want to know if it could mean something else.”

Kael opened a clean sheet of paper.

“What plan?”

“A copy of an old installation. The one we requested to compare with the modifications beneath the floor. There's a symbol in one corner. The municipal draftsman says it's a technical mark.”

“It could be.”

“That's what he said. But he's never seen it on another plan.”

“I need to know where that copy came from.”

“I'll bring it to you. Don't take it out of my office until the judge authorizes a comparison.”

“I wasn't planning to.”

“I know,” Daren said. “I'm making that clear so we can stay friends when you start thinking you should.”

***

The Central Archive had survived two fires, a flood, and four reforms of its classification system. Kael did not believe this proved its strength. It proved that institutions could keep their names while replacing almost everything else.

In the historical indexes room, an archivist named Tessa Ord leaned over the copy of the report.

“That's not a number,” she said.

“I didn't ask whether it was a number.”

“You looked at me as though you expected me to search for it.”

“I need to know whether the figure matches any older graphic convention.”

Tessa had a kind of patience that wore down slowly but could never be restored. She had spent twenty years working with municipal collections transferred to the Archive and distrusted people who called every stain a discovery.

“Builders' marks vary by trade. Stonemasons, gearmakers, surveyors. All three use simple figures.”

“And inventory marks?”

“Those too. Sometimes the same figure means different things in different periods. Without the physical medium or a date, I can't tell you anything useful.”

Kael appreciated the objection.

He showed her the estimated date of the lining and the plan Daren had mentioned, still in a provisional transcription. Tessa did not change her mind, but she agreed to consult the catalog of trade marks.

For an hour, they searched records of closed workshops, commercial seals, and railway annotations. Two figures looked similar. One identified parts manufactured for a freight tram; the other marked an assembly technique used by western craftsmen.

Neither had the open dot at its center.

“See?” Tessa said. “A resemblance isn't enough.”

“That's exactly why we're still looking.”

Tessa peered at him over her glasses.

“Do you know what usually happens to people who follow a figure until they find the one they want?”

“They end up mistaking a coincidence for proof.”

“Good. Save me having to say it again.”

Kael copied the discarded references. Negative findings also narrowed a search, even if they were difficult to explain to someone demanding an immediate conclusion.

In the last drawer of industrial marks, Tessa found a card without a registration date. The record was blank apart from a handwritten note: *Transferred to continuity collection; original not available for consultation in this room.*

There was no drawing.

There was a transfer number.

Kael recognized its opening characters.

They matched the prefix of the service address that had appeared when he searched the withdrawn series.

He carefully closed the drawer.

“Who can authorize access to that transfer?”

“The unit that received the collection.”

“It's not listed here.”

Tessa checked the responsibility field. The box carried a closure stamp without a name.

“Then request the movement history,” she said.

“The last time I looked up a similar reference, the system notified an address no one will admit to knowing.”

She laid the card on the table.

“Do you want us to withdraw the request?”

It was an administrative question. It was also a genuine opportunity to stop attracting attention.

Kael thought of Aldren Meret, of the meeting with the woman in gray, and of the withdrawal statement he had refused to sign.

“I want to record that this card exists, that it bears a transfer number, and that I can't trace its origin through ordinary channels.”

Tessa nodded.

“That, I can certify.”

She did not ask about the execution. Kael was grateful for that silence too.

***

Daren arrived after two, carrying the plan between two rigid sheets.

They met at a reserved table in the courthouse. A judicial clerk remained by the door to log the consultation.

“The chamber was assembled according to the contractor's plans,” Daren explained. “But the modification beneath the floor uses dimensions from a structure shown in this other document. The plan was added as a technical reference, not as an approved design.”

“Where did it come from?”

“The urban infrastructure archive. It's a copy. The original supposedly belonged to a station dismantled decades ago.”

Kael examined the scale and the annotations. One corner showed a set of conduits and a service chamber whose width roughly matched the cavity the inspectors had found. Beside it was the three-stroke figure.

“Is the mark on the packaging the same size?” he asked.

“We don't know. Mara measured the impression, but we haven't compared the originals yet.”

“Then we don't have identity. We have a formal resemblance.”

“A resemblance appearing in two places connected to the same corpse.”

“Yes. And that connection is a legitimate reason to request a comparison.”

Daren smiled.

“Every now and then you bring good news.”

The clerk looked up when Kael asked to examine the back of the plan. There were reproduction stamps, a receipt signature, and two dates forty years apart.

The more recent signature belonged to a retired municipal archivist. The older one belonged to an office Kael did not recognize.

“The dismantled station,” he said. “Do they still have its name?”

“Eastern Unloading Station. They shut it down when the transport network changed.”

“And its components?”

“One lot was auctioned off. Another went into public storage. There's an inventory.”

Kael returned to the mark.

Something was more troubling than its repetition. On the plan, it had not been drawn as a company emblem. It stood precisely where the conduit joined an access plate. It could identify a part, an assembly procedure, or an instruction.

“I need to see the station inventory and the transfer orders,” he said.

“Of course you do.”

“And whoever supervised that closure, if they're still alive.”

“That'll be harder.”

Kael looked at the figure again.

Suddenly he understood why Mara's report had made him think of a tool stain. If the mark was a technical instruction, its meaning depended on the device it appeared on. Taking it out of context and calling it a symbol only gave it an importance they could not yet justify.

“We don't know what it represents,” he said.

“But someone repeated it.”

“Or used two parts from the same system.”

Daren closed his notebook.

“And if they were reused parts, there should be a commercial trail.”

Kael remembered the name Inara Vel. He had seen her signature on requests for access to municipal contracts and knew her newspaper was investigating the square's suppliers.

“There's a journalist following that trail,” he said.

“I know her. She asked me three times whether we had a maintenance order.”

“Did you answer?”

“I told her which documents were public and which were under judicial custody.”

“Good.”

“You don't sound satisfied.”

“I still don't know whether her findings and ours describe the same event.”

Daren put away the plan.

“Then let's not tell her they do until we know.”

***

The Eastern Unloading Station inventory had been published forty-two years earlier. Kael found it on microfilm held by the Archive, not in the restricted series.

That detail gave him some small comfort.

He requested a certified reproduction of the index, but the reader was out of service. A technician explained that its feed mechanism had worn down and that the replacement part would arrive the next day.

Kael did not ask for an exception. He waited while the staff found another reader and reviewed the transfer catalog in the meantime.

The list included pulleys, regulators, insulating plates, and seventeen “assemblies of no industrial utility.” The assemblies had passed to a materials recovery consortium. The attached contract recorded only the total auction price.

The consortium had disappeared years later.

Kael wrote down its name, searched its registered address, and found a commercial property near the Lower Docks.

There were two hours of daylight left.

He had no judicial authority to inspect a warehouse, and he did not need it to ask about a company's history in a business open to the public.

He informed Aldren that he was going out to make a historical inquiry. His supervisor's secretary received the message without comment.

Kael boarded the westbound tram.

At that hour, the cars were full of workers and students. A woman carried a small household accumulator wrapped in a towel; judging by the rattle of its inner plates, it needed repair. A man argued with his neighbor about electricity prices. The city went on living around the case files, unaware that its documents could lead to dangerous places.

When he got off, Kael discovered that the property had become a nautical instruments shop.

A copper bell rang above the door.

“We close in half an hour,” a voice called from the back.

The proprietor was a white-haired man with large hands and glasses hanging from a chain. According to the sign by the till, his name was Orven Dal.

“I'm not here to buy anything,” Kael explained. “I'm looking for information about a company that operated here decades ago.”

“That's what all debt collectors say.”

“I work at the Central Archive.”

“Even worse.”

Orven pointed to a chair but continued arranging spools.

Kael showed him the name of the recovery consortium. The shopkeeper read it without hurry.

“My father rented them a storeroom. They brought in scrap from old stations.”

“Do you still have records?”

“Invoices, maybe. What do you want them for?”

“I'm trying to reconstruct the origin of some parts.”

“That's what people say when they want to sue someone.”

“I'm not bringing a lawsuit.”

Orven studied him.

“You Archive people always sound like you're testifying before a judge.”

Kael accepted the criticism.

The man crouched behind the counter and brought out a box of paper labels. He searched by the consortium's name and placed three invoices on the wood.

“They're rental invoices. If you want copies, you'll have to ask me in writing.”

“Of course.”

In the margin of one invoice was a little pencil mark.

Three strokes around an open dot.

Kael felt the air grow cold, though the shop was warm.

He did not touch the paper.

“Do you know what this figure means?”

Orven looked at the margin.

“An old mark. My father used it to separate crates that weren't supposed to go into the furnace.”

“Why not?”

“Some of them held delicate parts, plates that cracked if you heated them too fast. It wasn't magic. It was about not setting a warehouse on fire.”

“Where did those parts come from?”

“All sorts of places. The consortium bought entire lots.”

“Do you remember any customer who bought the assemblies from the Eastern Station?”

Orven shook his head.

“I was a kid. And my father didn't discuss his clients in front of me.”

Kael asked permission to copy the mark and the invoice reference. Orven agreed once he was satisfied that Kael was not taking any documents away.

“You're chasing something expensive,” the shopkeeper said as Kael closed his notebook.

“I'm chasing something old.”

“In this city, it's usually the same thing.”

***

Rain started as Kael left the shop.

He took shelter beneath the awning of a small restaurant, close enough to the harbor to hear the boats' whistles. He could return to the Archive with three visual resemblances and a partial technical explanation. It was progress, though not the progress he had hoped for.

The mark might identify parts requiring special handling. Its presence on medical packaging and the plan of a service chamber remained relevant, but it still did not prove the existence of a clandestine network.

He checked his notes by the yellow light of the restaurant window.

Then he saw Orven emerge from the shop with a small parcel under his arm. The shopkeeper crossed the street and greeted someone waiting beside a railing.

Kael was about to look away when he heard a familiar word.

“Lyra.”

The name meant nothing to him. What caught his attention was the tone: the shopkeeper had abandoned his jokes and was speaking cautiously.

The woman wore a dark coat, her face partly concealed by the edge of her hood. She did not look like a customer interested in nautical instruments. She reached for the parcel, but Orven did not hand it over immediately.

Kael could not hear the entire conversation through the rain.

But he heard the man say:

“Someone came asking about the old marks.”

The woman went still.

Orven gestured vaguely toward the shop and added something Kael could not make out. She turned her head, not toward him but toward the corner where the tram passed.

Kael closed his notebook.

He had learned not to mistake temporal coincidence for causal connection. The conversation might mean nothing. Or he might be overhearing a shopkeeper warn a customer that someone had been asking about an old classification system.

He stayed where he was.

At last the woman took the parcel. Orven left, and she remained by the railing for a few seconds.

When the next harbor bell drowned out the rain, she crossed the street toward the awning.

Kael had time to put away his notes.

The stranger stopped a step away from him. Beneath her hood, her eyes were watchful, as though she had already decided what she wanted to ask.

“You're from the Archive,” she said.

It was not a question.

Kael did not immediately answer.

The woman glanced at the notebook protruding from his coat.

“If you're looking for the meaning of that mark,” she continued, “there's someone who's going to want to know what you've found.”

Kael held her gaze.

“Who?”

She did not answer.

Across the street, Orven had turned off the shop lights.

“Before that,” the woman said, “I need to know why you're looking for a person who officially doesn't exist.”

***End of Episode 06.***
