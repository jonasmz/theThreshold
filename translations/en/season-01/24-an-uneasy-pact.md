# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 24: An Uneasy Pact

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Maelor Thane, third-person limited.

***

The first request for help did not come from a failed station.

It came from a workshop that still had power.

Maelor Thane read the message while Vessa held the end of a telemetry strip to stop it rolling back on itself. At the Western Compensation Station, the main regulator had been operating for two shifts with a dissipation margin below the authorized level. The Annex's extraordinary demand had ended days earlier, but the stress had not returned to normal.

The workshop requested permission to keep a cutting machine running for another six hours. A batch of supports for water pumps depended on it. If the municipality ran out of those parts, the letter argued, an ordinary breakdown might become a flood.

Maelor laid the request beside the curve.

“How long can the margin hold?” he asked.

Talia Mer did not answer immediately.

“The question isn't how long we can maintain the present condition. It's how much further the margin can deteriorate before stabilization maneuvers become unpredictable.”

Three traces lay on the table: incoming load, dissipator operation, and residual stress on the regulation assembly. None showed a complete loss of containment. Talia was concerned by the growing separation between recorded stress and the load that should have produced it.

“Is the sealed testing bench drawing power again?”

“No. The testing branch remains immobilized. We have independent confirmation at the control and supply points. The additional load comes from branches sharing stabilization with the western node, and some stress remained stored after the earlier maneuvers.”

“Can we isolate those branches?”

“Some. Not all. The containment regulator needs a minimum load and gradual discharge to dissipate what it already holds. If we cut supply abruptly, we'll lose control capacity without eliminating the stress.”

Maelor ran a finger along the edge of the curve.

He had authorized a partial reduction after the Annex inspection. He had assumed the margin would recover if the tests stayed shut down. The system was under no obligation to meet his expectations.

“Prepare two plans,” he said. “One for a phased reduction. Another to maintain essential services while it lasts. And don't use an estimate without a source to tell me how many safe hours we have.”

Vessa finally released the strip, which snapped back into a roll.

“There's a third issue,” she said. “The municipality wants to know the cause of the cuts before accepting another timetable.”

“They're entitled to know about their supply,” Maelor replied.

“They want a seat at the load meeting.”

Maelor laid the strip down.

“That isn't a technical question anymore.”

“To them, it is.”

***

The municipal supply committee occupied the second floor of a building whose windows were always open.

Maelor entered without armed escort, accompanied only by Talia and Vessa. He had sent ahead the boundaries of the information he could share: service curves, effects on civilian branches, and operating restrictions. He had not brought the sealed bench's inventory or photographs of unauthorized tests.

Neris Daal, the Civic Council's representative on the supply committee, stood beside a distribution map. She carried a written mandate from the Municipal Services Directorate to negotiate priorities and notifications; execution orders would remain the Directorate's responsibility. Small wooden markers on the map represented pumping stations, workshops, medical services, and transport lines.

“Your last notice ordered three workshops to limit operations,” she said in greeting. “Five shut down.”

“The restriction affected a shared feeder,” Talia replied. “Two more workshops were connected to the same section, and their contracts hadn't been updated in our list.”

“That's exactly what we've been saying. You measure nodes. We find people.”

Maelor did not argue. He studied the markers.

“What needs to keep running?”

Neris pointed to a pumping station.

“This isn't negotiable: sanitation, two dispensaries, and water transport for the lower sector. Then the workshops capable of making parts for those services. If we disconnect them entirely to save energy, we'll spend the reserve repairing failures we could have prevented.”

“Part of the network can run on conventional power,” Talia said. “Not every machine needs compensation from the node.”

“The conventional alternative works, but not for the entire load at once. It needs shifts, fuel, and operators. It isn't a lamp you can switch on from an office.”

Maelor took notes.

At his desk, he had imagined a clear hierarchy of essential uses. The municipal map displayed chains that did not fit into a list: a pump needed a gasket; the gasket needed a lathe; the lathe needed workers who could not go days without wages.

“I can authorize a reduction in strategic supply,” he said. “I can't personally assign production to a municipal factory or change its service contracts.”

“And I can't order your regulator to sustain a load your engineers consider unsafe,” Neris replied. “That's why I requested this meeting.”

She unfolded a sheet.

“I need a written commitment: limits per feeder, a time for reassessment, advance warning, and an exception procedure if a water facility has an emergency.”

“An exception can't mean a manager declares his machine indispensable and forces us to raise the node's stress,” Talia said.

“That's not what I'm asking. I want to know whom to call before a breakdown forces us to choose between two services.”

Maelor read the proposal.

At the bottom was a field headed *cause of risk*.

It was blank.

***

Elian Voss arrived at the Custody office with an objection already drafted.

He had learned of the municipal meeting through Licensing's reporting system. His document did not dispute Energy's measurements. It challenged the participation of local administration in a decision that, in his view, could reveal the network's internal organization.

“We can't negotiate a strategic node's parameters with an urban distribution office,” he said.

Maelor offered him a chair. Elian remained standing.

“We haven't negotiated the node's interior.”

“They've requested a place at the load table.”

“They've asked which services will lose supply and what notice they'll receive.”

“When the margin is reduced, the two become the same issue.”

Talia, who had entered behind him, placed a folder on the desk.

“That's precisely why we need their data. If we schedule a reduction without knowing which demand shifts to conventional backup, we could overload a civilian feeder.”

Elian stayed on his feet.

“Licensing decisions need clear attribution. A safety interruption might be interpreted as an admission that we've been operating outside protocol.”

Maelor remembered the two versions of the Annex report preserved under the joint audit. One recorded a dangerous interval. The other had erased the warning. He did not know who made the alteration or why. But the altered document could no longer serve as the sole basis for restoring the load.

“The authorization review is still open,” he said. “We won't wait for it to establish authorship before preventing a containment failure.”

“If you declare a strategic reduction, other Seats will demand information about the room you found.”

“They'll receive what their responsibilities require. Audit already holds the versions and the original readings.”

Elian closed the folder.

“Some believe the institution loses authority every time it explains a restriction.”

Maelor looked at him.

“It also loses authority when a factory knows it cannot work but can't find anyone responsible for giving notice.”

That answer would please no faction of the Council.

It had not been intended to.

***

Ilse Var refused to discuss the margin until Maelor had seen the shift room.

The Western Annex's testing doors remained sealed. There was no sign the bench had been supplied again, and photographs taken after the inspection remained under Custody and Energy control. The containment machinery, however, had not rested for a single night.

On one wall hung a relief roster crossed out and amended so many times there was almost no space left to write.

“The earlier reduction took us out of the most dangerous interval,” Ilse said. “But we're still covering two posts with people who ought to be resting.”

“I authorized reinforcements.”

“They arrived. Then they were sent to cover another supply shift. The paperwork was right. The staffing wasn't.”

Maelor watched a technician washing her hands in a basin.

Reddish marks showed beneath her gloves.

“What do you need now?” he asked.

“A real relief crew, an order refusing further tests, and no one demanding that I sign a declaration that the margin is back where it was.”

Talia checked the log. The readings confirmed Ilse's account: the system was not on the verge of an immediate rupture, but stress accumulated when demand repeatedly exceeded the restricted operating level. A brief adjustment could look harmless on the input needle and leave work outstanding in the dissipator.

Maelor approached the courtyard window.

Outside, a driver waited beside a fuel cart. Conventional backup had been prepared for monitoring instruments and ventilation, not to power the entire compound. The reserve had limited volume, output, and operating hours.

“Could reducing the node deprive its own containment system of power?” he asked.

“If we did it all at once, yes,” Talia replied. “Our plan separates power delivered to external branches from the power needed for monitoring and dissipation. First we reduce outside consumption, maintain stabilization, and observe the margin. We proceed only if the readings behave as expected.”

Ilse pointed to a line in the procedure.

“This says an anomaly means the maneuver stops. I want it recorded that the technical shift supervisor makes that decision, not whoever calls from the capital.”

“It will be,” Maelor said.

She fell silent, perhaps surprised by the speed of his answer.

“Even if the instruction comes from you?”

“Even then.”

Maelor knew what he had just relinquished: a measure of immediate control over an installation his office was charged with protecting.

But commanding a machine from a distance had never been the same as understanding what it was doing.

***

The first municipal visit began in a pump room.

Neris had insisted on bringing him there before signing anything. Maelor had agreed on condition that they neither interrupt operations nor use the visit to order inspections outside his jurisdiction.

Water stained the floor, and a deep noise passed through his chest. A conventional pump worked beside a small regulator supplied by the network. When the regulator received less power, a valve had to be operated manually to maintain service pressure.

“We can do it,” the supervisor said. “But not six times in one night, without rest or knowing when the next variation will come.”

Maelor watched two workers tighten a transmission belt.

Neris led him to the shift board.

“Every change of supply requires someone who can recognize a real pressure drop. If you announce a half-hour cut and it becomes two hours, the operator can't leave his station or go home to look after someone who's ill.”

“How long does reconfiguration take?” Maelor asked.

“The maneuver itself, not long. Warning the lower sectors takes longer. And stabilization afterward depends on how far the reservoir has dropped.”

The supervisor did not give him one figure he could promise in every situation. Maelor appreciated it.

Next they visited a workshop manufacturing replacement parts for the pumps. The owner had shortened the afternoon shift. Six half-finished cylinders lay on a table. If he lost another supply window, he would have to pay overtime to complete them.

“They told me the reduction was for safety,” he said. “I'm not arguing against safety. But who decides whether my production is part of it?”

Neris indicated her supply committee.

“We set civilian priorities according to contracts and current needs. Custody won't decide on its own which parts get made.”

Maelor studied the cylinders.

No answer would erase the loss entirely.

“I can commit to no deliberate load changes outside the agreed timetable except in a documented emergency,” he said. “I can't guarantee the system won't suffer an unexpected failure.”

The owner leaned on the table.

“That's more truthful than the last circular.”

Maelor heard a tool strike the floor in the next room.

A containment policy could be technically correct and socially unbearable if it reached people only as an unexplained order.

***

The Council meeting began with three empty chairs.

Not every Seat had accepted the summons. Some sent delegates with limited powers; others asked to receive the outcome in writing before taking a position. Maelor could not manufacture unanimity by insisting the situation was urgent.

The table was divided in ways the regulations never showed.

One group wanted the restriction extended without telling the municipality more than the outage schedule. Another insisted on first determining whether the altered Annex report invalidated maintenance permits. A third accepted sharing operational information, provided supervision did not open access to restricted programs.

Elian took a chair near Licensing.

Talia presented the curves.

She did not explain the facility's entire history. She showed the loss of margin after the extraordinary demand, the limited recovery observed, and two reduction scenarios. Neither involved shutting down containment.

“The first protects critical services with a smaller reduction,” she said. “It takes longer to relieve accumulated stress and requires stronger monitoring. The second reduces the load delivered to the industrial sector faster, but affects shifts and requires the municipality to arrange backup.”

“Can you guarantee either will restore the margin?” a supervisory delegate asked.

“No. We can establish conditions for continuing and criteria for stopping. If the curves depart from expectations, the plan must be reassessed.”

“Then you're proposing economic disruption without any guaranteed repair.”

“I'm proposing that we stop making an installation work beyond its safe margin while we investigate how to stabilize it,” Talia replied.

Maelor saw the delegate glance at the empty chair of a usual ally.

He had heard that objection before, after accidents whose causes no one could identify. His own generation had defended the secrecy of incomplete procedures, convinced that disclosure could enable irresponsible use. It remained a legitimate concern.

But concealing the loss of margin would not restore it.

“The municipality will share a list of critical services, expected demand, and changes to its backup,” Maelor said. “Energy will share the supply limits affecting those services and the times for review. Protected information about installations will remain subject to the competent channels and, where appropriate, audit.”

Elian intervened.

“Who answers if a municipal office discloses a limit that a clandestine operator could exploit?”

“The agreement will prohibit distribution of internal parameters or reserve locations. It won't prevent a nurse from knowing whether her dispensary will have power.”

A voice from the far end asked whether there was reason to believe the failure would affect other nodes.

Maelor could not answer no.

He knew of old connections between installations and that some restricted programs sought to compensate for the loss of recalibration capability. He also knew the Seats did not share all that knowledge, and that certain investigations involving people, whose legitimacy he considered deeply questionable, had been justified as technical solutions when ordinary methods failed.

None of that allowed him to attribute the Annex incident to a particular commission.

“We know connected installations can transmit stress through their conduits and controls,” he replied. “We have no measurement demonstrating propagation beyond the sector examined. So this agreement will cover only the node and its verified branches. I won't let a hypothesis govern the entire network.”

Talia laid the final sheet on the table.

The margin remained reduced.

The Seats could delay a decision.

The machinery would not stop to listen to them.

***

Neris returned to the Custody building at dusk.

She brought a three-page proposal. Her office had drafted it with those responsible for water and workshops, not with the Council.

“We can't accept a timetable that changes by telephone,” she said. “Ordinary changes must be recorded and notified in advance. In emergencies, an identified technical supervisor must issue the instruction and explain the reason afterward.”

Vessa read the document.

“Internal network information will remain classified.”

“We haven't requested it,” Neris replied. “We need to know how much supply we can count on, and when we can't.”

Elian was present as Licensing's representative.

“I want a clause preventing this agreement from being used as a presumption that earlier operations were unlawful,” he said.

Neris looked at him.

“I can't sign a statement that nobody did anything illegal. Nor can you ask me to declare the losses normal.”

Maelor intervened.

“The clause will say that the agreement is preventive and does not prejudge responsibility. Audit reports will proceed separately. No signatory will surrender the powers of their office.”

Vessa recorded the wording.

Next they argued about cost.

The municipality could not sustain extra conventional generators indefinitely or keep paying standby wages in closed workshops. The Council had no budget to compensate every industrial loss and should not promise damages without authorization from its Seats.

Neris proposed a temporary fund for critical maneuvers, limited to fuel, pump relief crews, and transportation of essential replacement parts. Labor and commercial claims would remain open through separate channels.

Elian asked who would review the invoices.

“Civil audit for municipal expenditure, Custody oversight for strategic funds,” Neris answered. “Two signatures where a service is shared, without unnecessary access to one another's restricted information.”

Maelor checked the figures.

They were not enough to restore work to those who had already lost it. They were enough to prevent the reduction from leaving the people who maintained water and dispensaries without backup.

“I can commit Custody's extraordinary contribution up to its emergency limit,” he said. “Anything beyond that requires budget approval. I want that recorded, not concealed beneath a vague promise.”

Neris laid down her pen.

“Then write it.”

The room fell silent while Vessa prepared the final version.

A pact worthy of the name did not make its parties friends.

It forced them to state where their authority ended.

***

Ilse's call came before the documents were signed.

Vessa listened first, then passed the receiver to Talia. The engineer grew very still as the shift supervisor described a new reading.

“Did the external load hold?” she asked.

A pause.

“And the dissipator?”

Another pause.

Talia took a sheet and drew two lines.

“Don't make any maneuver outside the authorized operating range. I'll review the pattern and call you back with instructions.”

She hung up.

Maelor waited.

“Residual stress is rising at a shared junction,” Talia said. “It hasn't crossed the immediate-stop limit, but the rise repeated after two controlled discharges. The reserve isn't recovering margin at the expected rate.”

“Do we need to lower demand further?”

“Yes. I can't defend the smaller reduction schedule with these data. I recommend beginning the second stage and preparing a third, subject to readings at each step.”

Elian stood.

“Was the instrument checked?”

“Against two reference readings and an independent sensor. Ilse retained the strips.”

“It could be a thermal shift.”

“That could contribute. Which is why the plan includes temperature measurement. We can't use the possibility to disregard the recorded stress.”

Maelor looked at Neris.

She understood before he spoke.

“How much notice will the workshops receive?”

“We can start the reduction at the next shift change, not before,” Talia said. “If the present condition holds, that gives enough time to give notice and reorganize loads without waiting another day.”

Neris counted the hours silently.

“Water services can be ready. The parts workshop needs to finish the batch it started this morning. I can allow a limited window, but not if the risk is greater.”

“We won't raise demand to finish it,” Talia said. “It can use the ordinary margin available until the shift change. Then the reduction begins.”

Maelor understood the cost of that concession. It was not permission to disregard the risk, but the time the engineering assessment considered tolerable before the maneuver.

“I want a notice signed by the municipality and Energy,” he said. “Nothing promising normal service. The procedure must say what will be reduced, who will execute it, and what conditions require it to stop.”

Vessa opened the order book.

Maelor did not sign yet.

One confirmation was missing.

***

Before signing, Maelor did not call Ilse Var to read an order to her.

He asked to speak to the Western Annex shift supervisor so she could explain the procedure back to him.

Her voice arrived over the noise of ventilation fans.

“Our reference sensors are within range,” Ilse said. “The dissipation unit responds, but recovery is still behind schedule. We're fully staffed for a phased reduction. I won't carry out a total shutdown.”

“Who stops the operation if the response deviates?” Maelor asked.

“The technical shift supervisor. That's me until the relief comes. Talia validates Energy's procedure. Custody maintains the perimeter. No external service can ask us to raise the load beyond the plan.”

“Support personnel?”

“Present. Relief crew ready, first aid team on site. The conventional backup fuel is sufficient for the shift's essential instruments, not to replace all the regulator's power.”

Maelor closed his eyes briefly.

It was the answer he wished he had heard on his first visit to the room.

“Have the boundaries of the testing branch been verified?”

“It remains isolated and sealed, with an independent reading. It won't be used to compensate for this reduction.”

“Good. Tell the operator the stop conditions outrank any political instruction.”

“I will.”

When he ended the call, Maelor saw Elian across the table.

The secretary had heard the exchange.

“Licensing's record must show that the installation retains authorization for containment,” he said.

“It will.”

“I can't declare the review of the sealed room complete.”

“I'm not asking you to.”

Elian sat slowly.

Perhaps his earlier objection had not been solely an attempt to protect his Seat's reputation. Perhaps he also feared that a necessary operation would be read as an admission that the whole system was illegitimate. Maelor knew the temptation to confuse institutional continuity with technical continuity.

“Tomorrow we'll keep arguing about responsibility,” he said. “Today we have to prevent an accident.”

Elian did not answer.

But he picked up his pen to sign the licensing restriction.

***

The final order filled two sheets.

The first authorized Energy to reduce the load delivered by the western node in stages, with measurements of stress and dissipation before each change. It maintained the supply indispensable for containment and prohibited reactivation of the testing bench. Any deviation in margin would require the maneuver to stop and the procedure to be reviewed, not a desperate increase in power.

The second documented the agreement with the municipality: civilian priorities set by municipal authorities, notification windows, limited conventional backup, temporary financing for critical services, and a joint committee to review every shift. No clause authorized municipal inspections of restricted interiors or prevented the courts from requesting evidence through lawful process.

Talia signed the technical approval of the procedure.

Neris signed the Civic Council's agreement; the authorized head of the Municipal Services Directorate signed the civilian distribution and notification plan.

Elian signed acknowledgment of Licensing's restrictions, without validating any prior test.

Maelor reserved the last space.

He knew the workshops would close part of their shifts. There would be fewer spare parts, fewer wages, and more arguments about who should pay for negligence whose author had not yet been identified. He also knew some Council leaders would see the agreement as a humiliating concession, and the municipality might use it to demand greater accountability when evidence became available.

He could not avoid those consequences with a smaller signature.

“I want the text delivered to the people who must execute it before operations begin,” he said. “The order isn't fulfilled because it's in my book. It's fulfilled when it reaches the people who'll move the valves.”

Vessa checked the recipient list.

Maelor signed.

***

The first reduction began at the shift change.

Maelor did not go to the Annex. He remained in the monitoring room, where every call was logged and each operator had to acknowledge instructions by name. He had insisted that the technical supervisor retain the authority to halt the maneuver without waiting for his office to respond.

Across the city, part of the industrial sector fell silent at the announced hour. He knew from the municipality's confirmation, not from some privileged vision of every workshop.

One pumping station switched to conventional backup. The second dispensary maintained lighting and essential equipment on the agreed auxiliary supply. Two workshops suspended noncritical operations; a third finished storing its parts and shut down its larger machines.

The signal from the Annex arrived afterward.

“First stage complete,” Ilse said. “The regulator maintains containment. External input fell as planned. Residual stress decreased, but we still haven't recovered the target margin.”

Talia recorded the values.

“Hold that operating level. Don't proceed to the next stage until the observation window is complete.”

Maelor heard the dry sound of a sheet feeding into the recorder.

He had not repaired the node.

He had not established who altered a report or which permit had allowed a machine to be tested in a sealed room.

He had ordered an end to demanding more from an installation than it could provide without endangering those who depended on it.

Vessa approached with a new municipal report.

“Protests are planned for tomorrow,” she said. “People want to know how long the restrictions will last.”

“We don't know yet.”

“What should I tell them?”

Maelor examined the curve Talia had just drawn. For the first time in days, its slope was favorable. One reading did not prove recovery, and the reserve was still being depleted.

“The truth we can substantiate: the reduction has begun, containment remains active, and there'll be another assessment tomorrow. Don't announce a date for normal service to resume.”

Vessa put away the report.

Maelor remained before the three curves.

One had begun to fall.

The other two still demanded vigilance.

The pact had neither stopped the network's deterioration nor reconciled the institutions that administered it.

It had changed something less grand and harder to reverse:

**From that night onward, protecting the node would carry a cost everyone could see, and a signed order no one could pretend did not exist.**

***End of Episode 24.***
