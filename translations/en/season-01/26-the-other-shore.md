# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 26: The Other Shore

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, third-person limited.

***

The first indication that the station could be repaired was an invoice.

Kael Veyran found it on Ruva Neral's table beside two photographs of a cracked component. Thirteen days had passed since the inspection was halted. During that time, the High Signalers' Association had kept the bench sealed, without further tests. Safety personnel had confirmed that the stress from the previous test had finished dissipating before permitting any mechanical work.

The invoice covered dismantling the auxiliary coupling, two days of verification, and a return component certified for the same family of supports. It included neither an energy reserve for Transit nor permission to use one.

“Who paid?” Kael asked.

“The association advanced the diagnostic costs. Custody will cover the safety repair because it received our notice and agreed that the danger couldn't be left to an organization without a strategic budget,” Ruva replied. “I haven't received a single coin to send people through that thing.”

An open box stood at the end of the table. Inside, resting on wooden supports, lay the removed fragment: a conductor with a fine crack that had been hidden beneath a clamp. Kael did not touch it.

“Does the component explain the entire deviation?”

“It explains why some of the load wasn't returning along the intended path. Galen says the readings are consistent with it. That doesn't establish that the rest of the machine is safe.”

Ruva showed him the report. It bore the signatures of Galen Trev, Myra Sol, and an inspector dispatched by the technical safety office. That inspector had also established that the procedural supplement Kael discovered belonged to a later bench model. The difference in signatures was not evidence of fraud, but an adaptation someone had filed in the wrong folder.

“Then the older sequence was correct for this station,” Kael said.

“That's what the inspector certified after comparing the equipment plate. Not because you guessed it. They had to open the assembly, read the manufacturing number, and check it against the office's own register. The inventory error is still an error. Now someone is responsible for correcting it.”

Kael nodded.

He was pleased his discovery had proved useful. He was less pleased to remember how close he had come to treating it as evidence of deliberate manipulation.

“Why did you call me?”

Ruva moved the invoice aside. Underneath was another folder, bearing a white label and a case number that did not belong to the association.

“Because someone has requested verification of an arrival reference. The regional office has agreed to consider one supervised operation. And you're named in the application as a voluntary documentary observer.”

Kael recognized Lyra's handwriting in the margin.

He had not signed anything yet.

For the first time, the distinction felt like real protection.

***

The meeting did not take place in the Anchor room.

It took place in the guardhouse, where impatience couldn't lead anyone to touch a control. Galen sat beside Myra. Next to them was a middle-aged woman carrying a tool bag and an operating permit issued in her name.

“Sena Vard,” she introduced herself.

Kael knew her only through a note sent to Lyra, whose existence Lyra had explained without showing him the private message.

“Your warning prevented the use of an undated table,” he said.

“The warning was for Lyra,” Sena replied. “I can't claim it saved anyone. Other restrictions already prohibited that diagnostic procedure.”

She opened a small leather-bound book.

“I have the earlier edition and the record of its replacement. The supplement you found applied to equipment with an independent return. Here, the return reference belongs to the main bench. Applying the supplement would have reversed two checks.”

Myra indicated a line in the report.

“The repair has been verified by progressively increasing tests, all below the permitted limit. After the last cycle, the discharge left no accumulated stress. We aren't going to pretend that success eliminated wear on the support.”

“And the natural connection?” Kael asked.

Sena opened another envelope.

“Before anyone approached the installation, we tested two independent field references. Passive readings were repeated on different shifts. Then an information response came from the far end of a connection recorded in the continuity inventory.”

“An echo?” Kael asked.

“A coded information disturbance, yes. It transported no object and didn't let us see the other side. The receiving crew answered a new request, written after testing began, using a pattern authenticated by its custody records. That establishes communication and operational correspondence between two stations. It doesn't, by itself, establish that transporting someone is safe.”

Kael examined the list of questions and responses. The complete addresses were not shown to him. He was allowed to see transmission and reception times, seals from different operators, and independent checks. The remote crew had supplied a current measurement of its sky and the condition of its Anchor. Sena had compared it with the old table and the astronomical model Irena Dalce had documented for Kael, though the office had not asked Irena to disclose any route.

“Couldn't the matches come from the same copied table?” Kael asked.

“The historical table may have guided the identification,” Sena said. “That's why we requested a fresh observation, within an interval chosen after the case was opened and signed by another crew. The response matches within its tolerance. We haven't assumed an old signature is enough.”

Kael put down the sheet.

For the first time, there was an observation his question could not have anticipated.

It was more than five lights drawn on a private sheet.

And far less than a journey.

***

Dalia Ferren had received a message from Kael two days earlier.

He had explained that an organization outside the Archive was offering him voluntary participation in a strategic-risk assessment under specific authorization. He had told her neither the route nor the contents of Lyra's documents. Dalia was not his legal representative in that matter, but she needed to know her client was considering obligations that might affect his case.

“I'm not going to tell you how to live,” she said when he arrived at her office. “But your suspension doesn't vanish because another institution finds your reading skills useful.”

“I know.”

“Nor does permission to work for third parties let you consult Archive collections.”

“I haven't asked to.”

Dalia closed the disciplinary record.

“Your ruling is still pending. Tessa's statement and the preserved reproductions are where they belong. Don't introduce an adventure here that you later can't explain without breaching another restriction.”

Kael had to smile.

“I don't intend to use it in my defense.”

“Good. Now read this.”

It was a sheet describing the consequences of accepting a dangerous activity in a private capacity. If he was injured or became unable to appear, he could request a postponement and representation, but there was no guarantee the proceedings would wait indefinitely. Participation would neither make him an official nor excuse his own conduct.

Kael signed only to acknowledge that he had read it.

Outside, the city was adjusting to new supply shifts. A workshop announced it would open at dawn to finish work before its feeder was reduced. The tram office had changed two schedules. There was no total catastrophe. There were small, accumulating sacrifices he could see from the street.

He disliked realizing that the same network whose deterioration he had studied as a documentary sequence could determine when other people received water or earned wages.

The journey he was being offered must not be paid for with that load.

***

Sena insisted that the financial agreement be read in front of everyone.

They would not use the reserve of the western node operating under containment. The regional authorization provided that the Class V test would draw power from a strategic accumulator moved from a reserve station unconnected to restricted civilian feeders, previously charged by conventional generation. Its total capacity, peak discharge power, and wear were recorded. Galen had rejected a second battery whose condition could not be certified.

The accumulator would arrive under Energy custody. Technicians would hold back part of its charge for dissipation and contingencies. What remained for the operation allowed only two individual outward Transit cycles, separated by mandatory recovery. Any additional departure was prohibited until reassessment.

The installation at the far end needed its own reserve for the returns. It was not enough to keep an arch open and expect people to walk back through it whenever they wanted.

“And the license?” Kael asked.

Ruva showed him the document.

It was an exceptional, limited regional authorization. Obtaining it had taken days after the risk notification and technical checks. It permitted a supervised test involving consenting adults, to verify physical correspondence between two previously identified endpoints. It specified a maximum stay, mandatory stopping conditions, and restrictions on destination information. It neither restored old routes to service nor granted the association a permanent license.

“Why did they agree to let me travel?” Kael asked.

Sena did not pretend it had been an easy decision.

“Because of your report on the celestial description, and because you can document an observation without confusing it with its interpretation. The safety authority required a witness who wasn't part of the calibration team. The association put your name forward. If you refuse, no one will replace you at the last moment. That portion of the test will be canceled.”

“Wouldn't an operator be better?”

“The operators are assigned to maintain both ends. Lyra will make the first crossing. She has technical experience with routes and can verify the agreed protocol at the destination. You'll travel only if the first cycle closes without anomalies. We don't need a hero. We need two people who can state what they observed and record when they don't know something.”

Kael read the list of risks: injury, loss of load, return failure, and displacement of autobiographical memory. They were not universal to every act of travel; they increased with the condition of the Threshold and the stress of the maneuver. Nobody could promise the journey would be harmless.

“Can I withdraw even after signing?” he asked.

“Until the safety access closes and the cycle carrying you begins, yes,” Myra replied. “If you decide not to go, we'll stop that cycle. There's no clause permitting us to push you because we've already spent energy preparing the room.”

Kael was silent.

The form had a box headed *informed consent*, not *waiver of claims*.

It was an important difference.

***

On the eve of the test, Kael bought oranges.

At the stall, the seller offered the cheapest ones, their skins marked by rain. He chose three and took the longer route home to avoid the crowded trams at shift change.

The smell came before he peeled the first.

It carried him into an afternoon of his childhood.

He remembered a misted window and three separate sounds: rain against the wooden frame, a light tapping on glass, and a metal gutter striking the wall. The woman who had raised him sat beside him. He did not need to name her to remember how she listened. He told her that every surface made a different sound. She answered that if he learned to hear those differences, he would also learn when something was changing.

He remembered the relief of her not laughing at him.

He remembered the taste of an orange he had divided with his fingers.

It was not the most important event in his life. It was one of the few he could recall without wondering which document might certify it.

He opened a new notebook at his table, bought with his own money. He wrote the date and three facts he could verify without leaving home: his name, his address, and a small scar at the base of his thumb. Below he recorded the safety instructions he had been given. On the last page he made a different list.

*An afternoon of rain with the person who raised me. Wood, glass, metal. Orange segments. The feeling that I had time to explain.*

He stared at the last sentence.

He had read that an external record could describe something lost. He had never seen a sentence return to someone the experience of having been loved.

He wrote no more.

The next day he would carry the notebook in a sealed bag approved for travel and give the medical team a copy of his memory inventory. Not to use it as a key, but to check what remained his after the journey.

He put out the lamp.

He slept badly.

***

The station's access corridor was filled with workers who had not been there thirteen days earlier.

There was a regional safety supervisor, two containment specialists, a doctor experienced in Anchor accidents, and association personnel. Nobody wore a prosecutor's uniform. The operation had no established connection to the gallery beneath the square or the condemned man's judicial case.

Ruva checked each person's permit.

Galen displayed the replacement component and the phased-test report. Myra reviewed the stopping conditions: stable return, available dissipation, the remote reference revalidated immediately before energizing the bench, sufficient power after preserving the emergency margin, and authorization from the receiving operator. Without all five conditions, the cycle would be canceled.

Lyra stood beside an instrument table.

“You can still say no,” she told Kael.

“So can you.”

“I know.”

He watched her place her private document in an opaque sleeve. It had not become an operating credential. Valid references came from cross-checking inventories, the far end's current response, and operator verification. Lyra kept her sheet as the source of the questions that brought them here, not a reason to skip checks.

“What do you hope to find?” Kael asked.

“An observation that withstands being questioned again,” she replied. “And if there's a record of the operation I can't remember, I want to know who holds it. I'm not going looking through someone else's cupboards during a safety test.”

That answer seemed unlike the woman he had first met at the harbor.

Perhaps that was why he believed it.

Myra approached.

“Before going into the room, each of you will review your return plan. The receiving station has two authorized operators and an independent reserve. We must receive confirmation of the first arrival before the second Transit. If the first traveler arrives in poor condition, Kael won't depart.”

“And if she arrives but return becomes compromised?” he asked.

“There'll be no second traveler. The remote operators will care for the person who's already crossed and preserve their reserve. A return plan isn't an excuse to force another movement.”

Kael nodded.

It did not feel like the beginning of an adventure.

It felt like the moment an institution acknowledged in writing that it could fail.

***

The lower hall no longer smelled of dust.

The bench had been cleaned so inspections could be carried out visibly. New seals distinguished the containment circuit from the power circuit. The strategic accumulator was connected to an external distributor whose readings Myra could see. There was no new illumination behind the arch, no door that had materialized in the wall.

Kael remained in the observation area.

Galen announced input and reserve readings without treating them like a password. Sena confirmed them against the charge certificate. At another table, a communications technician sent a question through the information connection.

The answer arrived as a series of short impacts on the recording strip.

Sena compared the code with the remote authorization and the local time the receiving team had sent. Synchronization uncertainty was enough to require a safety margin; the plan accounted for it.

“Correspondence accepted,” she said. “Destination validated for this window, not for any future date.”

Myra surveyed the room.

“Containment?”

“Available,” Galen answered.

“Return reserve?”

“Confirmed at the receiving end, in its team's custody.”

“Outward power?”

“Within the certified limit, with separate dissipation reserve.”

“Authorization?”

Ruva raised the document. The safety supervisor checked it.

Lyra took her marked position. She carried no heavy luggage. She had signed her individual consent, including the warning about memory. Kael wanted to say something important and found only useless phrases.

“I'll see you on the other side,” he said.

She smiled tiredly.

“Only if Myra says we can try afterward.”

The operator did not open an arbitrary door. He activated the stabilizers around the identified natural connection using supplied power and the verified reference. The air became difficult to look at. The arch showed no landscape. It showed a depth without perspective, as though part of space had stopped allowing ordinary distance.

Kael felt pressure in his teeth.

Lyra stepped forward at the operator's direction. Her outline dissolved into the stabilized interval, and she was no longer there.

The recorder kept running.

Power draw fell, dissipation rose, and the crew closed the cycle according to procedure.

Nobody applauded.

They waited for confirmation.

***

The response took longer than expected, though it remained within the safety window.

Kael listened to the communications technician count intervals and watched Myra check the outlet temperature for a second time. The return curve remained stable. That did not guarantee Lyra was well.

Just as he decided he could bear looking at the strip no longer, the receipt arrived.

*Traveler identified. Conscious. Initial examination shows no acute injury. Memory data pending. Receiving station under containment.*

Kael let out his breath.

Then Myra shook her head in answer to a question he had not asked.

“That doesn't mean we can begin at once. The equipment has to recover margin, and we need to verify the support hasn't accumulated stress. If the next cycle exceeds the limit, we stop here.”

Time passed, impossible for Kael to measure without consulting the clock.

The doctor approached with his copy of the personal inventory.

“I can't promise what you'll remember after crossing,” he said. “If loss occurs, it doesn't necessarily select memories by importance. It can affect one particular scene while leaving knowledge about it intact. If you notice a gap, don't try to complete it by guessing. First record what you know and what you can no longer experience.”

Kael looked at his consent form.

He could still withdraw.

He thought of the years he had spent defending documents as more trustworthy than witnesses. Now, to distinguish a claim about another Domain from verifiable experience, he had to accept the risk of becoming a witness himself.

He would not do it out of heroism.

He would do it because he wanted to know whether the world Lyra described could be observed from somewhere absent from his maps, and because the decision was his.

“I still consent,” he said.

The doctor had him sign again beside the time.

***

The second cycle began more quietly.

The instruments had returned to their permitted bands, and the crew at the far end confirmed readiness to receive him. Myra read the stopping conditions one last time. Kael handed Galen items that could not travel and kept a small notebook inside a protected bag. A custody witness recorded its page count and the wrapping's seal.

He carried neither a secret sample nor a map of stations.

He carried his name and his question.

At the departure marker, the depth in the arch resembled a motionless surface. From outside it was still a metal apparatus anchored to stone. Only when the field was activated within the natural Threshold's tolerances did the surface cease to have a direction his eyes could follow.

“Walk when you receive the signal,” Galen said. “Don't stop inside the interval.”

The lamp changed.

Kael stepped forward.

There was no leap into the future, no city dissolving beneath his feet. He felt two incompatible forces acting on his body: one holding him within the measure of his own bones, another seeming to demand a reference he could not supply. His ears hurt. Breathing became clumsy. For an instant, he thought he heard three taps: wood, metal, glass.

He tried to remember where he had heard them.

The field released him.

Solid ground struck the soles of his boots.

Two human hands held him before he could lose his balance.

“Kael Veyran, can you tell me your name?” an unfamiliar voice asked.

“Yes.”

“Do you recognize your companion?”

Lyra stood across the platform, pale, with a blanket over her shoulders.

“I recognize her.”

The receiving operators helped him sit. A woman with a technical insignia recorded his arrival time; another checked his breathing, orientation, and pulse. They did not make him walk before the examination was complete.

Kael looked past their shoulders.

The room's windows were built differently. Low, broad frames were secured to dark stone. Through the gaps came salty air with a plantlike scent he did not recognize. Beyond a railing, gray plains ended in a line of mountains cut by pale bands.

It did not resemble Vael-Tir.

It might be somewhere far away on Eryndor, he told himself. The journey itself needed corroboration.

The operator pointed to an observation window.

“When you're cleared, you can look at the sky recorded in our latest communication. For now, remain seated. Receiving you has consumed part of our reserve, and we must maintain containment.”

Kael obeyed.

His heart was racing too fast to argue.

***

The receiving station was called the Port of Quiet Winds.

Its custodian introduced himself as Eren Mal, responsible for the Anchor and the observation notebooks on this shore. Kael had no way of knowing whether the name and office were legitimate without checking records. The crew permitted him to do so, but only after the doctor authorized a short visit to the reading room.

The receipt inventory bore a seal unused on Eryndor. It had been printed on reddish-fiber paper and numbered by an office Kael did not know. Its provenance was asserted, not established by those material differences alone.

Eren opened a folder in front of him.

“Here's the question we received before you left,” he said. “And our answer, with an observation of the sky made after its arrival. If you want to check from the window, the five reference lights will be visible by late afternoon. You needn't take my word for it.”

Kael examined the figures.

The relationships among the five lights corresponded to the family he and Irena had tested against Eryndor's catalogs. The new measurements had their own dates and tolerances. The school had found no configuration of that kind within the observations of his world. Here was a team claiming to have measured them, and a view that might let him check with his own eyes.

“What do you call this Domain?” he asked.

“Selivar,” Eren answered. “That's the name in our station register. Other languages use different names. I don't claim a word constitutes proof.”

Kael wanted to laugh.

It was exactly the answer he would have demanded.

He waited for the light to fade. Lyra stayed nearby without urging him on. The technicians had agreed on a maximum observation period before return preparations; none suggested they could disregard it if readings changed.

At the appointed time, Eren took him to a protected terrace.

There were no twin suns or impossible creatures. There was wind, a smell like crushed leaves, stone holding the day's warmth, and an ash-colored shore. Below the terrace, human workers gathered instruments beneath a canvas sheet. One sang while putting away ropes.

Kael looked up.

Five bright points appeared above the mountain. Two lay very close, two more were divided by a dark space, and the fifth stood exactly where the document's pattern required.

He opened his notebook.

He did not copy the shape from memory. He measured with an instrument supplied and verified by Eren, recorded uncertainties, and asked the operator to take independent readings. Their measurements did not match exactly; the differences lay within the expected margins. That was better than an inexplicably perfect agreement.

Eren signed the local record. Kael signed only to attest that he had witnessed the measurements and had been permitted to repeat them.

Before him was a sky he had never seen from Eryndor.

He could not derive it from a mistaken date or a misfiled book.

For the first time, his question about other Domains no longer depended on Lyra's voice.

***

The first sign of the cost came when Kael tried to describe what he felt.

He was still on the terrace, his notebook open on his knees. Eren had gone to report the results, and Lyra was discussing the return schedule with the crew. Wind struck the metal railing. Across the terrace, a glass window rattled against its wooden frame.

Three sounds.

Kael recognized that they meant something to him.

He searched for the memory he had written down the day before: a rainy afternoon, a table, the woman who had raised him, and orange segments.

He knew it had happened.

He also knew he had been able to recall the entire scene before leaving. Facts remained: the woman's name, the house where he grew up, even the knowledge that his childhood had been real and belonged to him. He could repeat the sentence from his notebook.

But when he tried to return to that afternoon, he could not find the window.

He could not find her face bending toward him, or the feeling of being listened to.

The words remained available as information. The experience was gone.

He turned to the final page. He read his own handwriting: *The feeling that I had time to explain.*

He recognized the sentence as his.

He felt nothing.

“Lyra,” he called.

She turned immediately. Seeing him grip the notebook with one hand, she knelt beside him.

“What happened?”

He showed her the line.

“Before we left, I could remember this afternoon. Not just know it existed. I could be there again.”

Lyra did not touch him until he looked up.

“And now?”

“I can read it. I can tell you the facts. But I don't have it.”

She looked at the paper without trying to fill his silence.

“We have to tell the doctor.”

Kael nodded.

Below, operators continued preparing their equipment. The return reserve still had to be checked, and the safety window was not infinite. Staying might cost more; returning meant another crossing.

The other world was real.

He had obtained evidence that could be examined.

And he had just learned that not everything lost could be preserved by writing it down in time.

***End of Episode 26.***
