# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 02: The Missing Signature

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran. **Format:** prose suitable for audio adaptation.

***

The Central Archive had one habit Kael considered healthy: it did not explain its mistakes.

A system might reject a request, a lamp might go out without warning, or an elevator might stop between two floors. None of those mechanisms tried to convince the user that the problem was his fault. Tonight, however, his desk terminal seemed to have developed an opinion.

**Request logged. Notification sent to the competent authority.**

Kael read the message for the third time. Then he closed the window, reopened the index of withdrawn series, and entered the same identifier.

The response changed.

**Access temporarily suspended.**

That was not a common variation. An invalid query should return a formatting error or no matching entries. A suspension meant the system had recognized the request and applied a rule.

He opened the audit log for his own session.

A line had appeared at eight forty-three that evening. It showed his username, the identifier, the failed operation, and an internal routing code. The recipient field was blank.

Kael printed the line.

The paper emerged warm, carrying the sharp smell of freshly fixed ink. He held it by the edges and wrote down the time by hand. When he checked again, the log entry was still there, but the routing field had vanished.

He liked that even less.

Not because someone had necessarily intervened. The Archive's older systems were an unhappy assembly of incompatible protocols. Sometimes their screens displayed fields their printers could not interpret; sometimes the opposite happened. He had learned to distrust interfaces as much as witnesses.

But a difference between two readings of the same entry was worth preserving.

He filed the paper in a technical incident folder and switched off the terminal. The room fell dark except for the city lights reflected in the windows.

Outside, the last tram on the eastern line crossed the bridge with a long hum. Kael wondered how long it would take him to get home if he missed it. The answer was enough to make him collect his coat.

When he opened the door, he found the night receptionist leaning against the frame.

“Veyran. Someone was looking for you.”

“Who?”

“A gentleman from the Delegation. Didn't give a name.”

“Did he leave a note?”

“Said he'd come back tomorrow.”

She glanced at the folder under Kael's arm.

“Did something happen with the execution?”

“I can't discuss it.”

“So that's a yes.”

“It means I can't discuss it.”

The woman smiled wearily and moved aside to let him pass.

***

The next morning, the director of Documentary Integrity was waiting in his office.

Aldren Meret was a neatly bearded man who had spent so many years behind case files that he had developed the habit of listening to answers before asking questions. His office was packed with small pieces of furniture, all labeled except for a wooden box he kept beside the window.

“Close the door, Kael.”

The director pushed a registry sheet toward him.

“Did you request access to a withdrawn historical series?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“It's referenced in the execution file.”

Aldren set his hands on the desk.

“Are you sure?”

“The reference is printed twice, on documents from different issuing bodies.”

“Could it be an inherited error?”

“Possibly. That's why I checked the index.”

“And what did you find?”

“A number the system recognizes well enough to notify someone, but not well enough to reveal where it came from.”

The director said nothing.

Kael noticed a small ink stain on his left cuff. It was unusual. Aldren had used sealed-reservoir pens for years and often mocked people who still preferred inkpots.

“I want you to stop that search,” Aldren said at last.

“Why?”

“Because the case is under judicial investigation, and any query outside the authorized process could compromise the evidence's admissibility.”

“My assignment authorizes me to verify documentary consistency.”

“It does not authorize you to consult withdrawn series without knowing their classification.”

“I can't know their classification when the index won't display it.”

“Then you submit a request.”

“I did.”

Aldren exhaled through his nose.

“A formal request, Kael. Through me.”

Kael looked at the sheet. A note had been added to the record of his query during the night.

**Supervisory review required.**

“Who added that?”

“I don't know.”

“Who received the notification?”

“I don't know that either.”

“Doesn't that concern you?”

“What concerns me far more is a capable analyst confusing curiosity with duty.”

The remark hurt more than it should have.

Kael trusted Aldren. He had supervised him since his early years at the Archive. He had taught Kael that no document was innocent of context, and that the worst forgery used a legitimate form to communicate a false fact.

“I'll register the query as a technical incident,” Kael said.

“Do that. And until further notice, limit yourself to the documents we've received.”

The director withdrew the sheet.

The conversation was over.

In the corridor, Kael stopped beside a window. The glass was fogged. He wiped a narrow strip clear with his thumb, looked out onto the inner courtyard, and remembered another, smaller window through which he had watched rain many years earlier.

The woman who had raised him used to set a plate of orange segments on the table whenever a storm kept them inside. She would ask him to distinguish the sound of drops striking wood, metal, and glass. “If you listen closely, everything tells you what it's made of,” she would say.

Kael could still hear her voice.

It was one of those small memories that returned effortlessly, as though time could never wear them away.

***

For the rest of the morning, he examined the ordinary paperwork surrounding the incident.

There was a court order, two receipts, a provisional medical certificate, and an inventory of objects confiscated from the condemned man. Most of the signatures were easy to verify. The dates agreed as well, except for a timestamp marking the body's departure from the execution apparatus.

One copy recorded the transfer three minutes earlier than another.

The clocks might simply have been out of sync. Kael noted the difference and requested the time reference used by each organization.

Then he turned to the witness list.

The first page held six names. The second held four. The signature form had ten lines, but only nine had been filled in.

The absence was not necessarily irregular. One witness might have left before certification. What was strange was that the empty box bore a validation mark.

Kael looked for the code identifying the person authorized to validate it.

There was no signature.

Only a stamp.

He took a magnifying glass and studied the ink. The stamp had been applied after the form was printed. The paper showed uneven pressure, as though someone had pressed it against a surface that was not entirely flat.

It could have been an overworked office. A hurried official. Any number of things.

But the chain of certification was beginning to accumulate too many small exceptions.

Around midmorning, a court messenger arrived with a request for cooperation.

Kael checked the signatures and handed the envelope to incoming records.

Daren Holt wanted a formal comparison of the condemned man's biometric records with those of the body presented afterward.

The judicial authorization was properly drafted. It neither requested classified information nor demanded a predetermined conclusion.

For the first time since the execution, Kael felt that someone shared his need to separate facts from interpretations.

He requested the original copies.

The reception officer returned with a small sealed package.

“There's a condition,” he said.

“What condition?”

“The Council Delegation wants to be present whenever it's opened.”

“On what grounds?”

“Arcane jurisdiction.”

“Did they attach a ruling establishing that jurisdiction?”

“No.”

Kael placed the package on the table.

“Then they cannot impose conditions on a municipal court order.”

“Should I write that?”

“Write that the claimed jurisdiction must be substantiated before the procedure can be changed.”

The man regarded him with a mixture of admiration and pity.

“One day you're going to get this whole building shut down.”

“That would be difficult. We have too many documents waiting to be processed.”

***

Daren arrived shortly after noon.

He sat across from Kael without removing his coat and set a bag containing two spiced rolls on the desk.

“They told me you skipped lunch.”

“Who did?”

“Half the building. You seem to have a remarkably efficient surveillance system.”

“Archivists have a habit of discussing people without leaving a record.”

Daren pushed one of the rolls toward him.

“I'm not going to argue with that definition.”

They ate in silence while waiting for the official authorized to open the package.

The verification was less dramatic than Daren had expected and more disturbing than Kael would have liked.

Both identification sheets used valid formats and authentic seals. There were no obvious signs of substituted pages. But the biometric measurements did not belong to the same individual.

“He could have changed physically,” Daren suggested without much conviction.

“Not like this.”

“Magic?”

“If a biological alteration produced these results, we need a laboratory report. I can't certify an explanation merely because it's extraordinary.”

The inspector leaned back.

“The magistrate wants to know whether there was an identification error.”

“We can establish a discrepancy between the records. We don't yet know where it originated.”

“In plain language, does that mean the corpse belongs to another man?”

“It means someone must explain why the data for the man who entered don't match the body that came out.”

Daren studied him patiently.

“Sometimes it seems like you're trying very hard not to answer a question.”

“Sometimes people ask questions that don't have answers yet.”

The inspector finished his roll.

“Well, if the dead man is someone else, the condemned man might still be alive.”

Kael did not answer immediately.

He had thought the same thing, of course. But hearing it spoken aloud turned the possibility into an obligation.

“It's a hypothesis,” he said.

“Investigating those is what they pay me for.”

“They pay me not to turn them into conclusions too soon.”

Daren got up.

“Then let's each do our job.”

Before leaving, he set down a card with his office address and contact number.

“If you find another discrepancy, call me directly. I don't need a three-page letter to hear about it.”

“Written records preserve details better.”

“And people preserve silences better.”

After Daren closed the door, Kael stared at the card for a moment.

He put it away.

***

At five that afternoon, a reply arrived from the systems department.

The previous night's technical incident had been classified as “expected behavior.”

No explanation was attached.

Kael went to the maintenance section in person.

The technician on duty, a very young man with tired shadows beneath his eyes, reviewed the request and shook his head.

“I can't open that series.”

“I'm not asking you to open it. I want to know what rule sends a notification when someone searches for an identifier that doesn't exist.”

“The routing tables for older archives are centralized.”

“Where?”

“Depends on the series.”

“The series isn't in the catalog.”

“Then I can't tell you who's responsible for it.”

Kael felt the conversation beginning to take on a circular shape.

“Can you at least tell me when the rule was created?”

The technician looked at the screen, typed a command, waited, and frowned.

“It has no creation date.”

“All rules have a creation date.”

“This one doesn't.”

“A migration?”

“Could be.”

The technician lowered his voice.

“There are old tables nobody touches. They copied them from earlier systems and left them running because no one knew what other processes depended on them.”

“Is there documentation?”

“In theory.”

“Where?”

The young man shrugged.

“At the Central Archive, I suppose.”

Kael closed his eyes for a moment.

“Naturally.”

The technician looked at the screen again.

“There's something else. The notification wasn't addressed to a person.”

“Then who was it addressed to?”

“A service address. I don't have access to the table that resolves it.”

“Can you print the identifier?”

The technician thought it over.

“I shouldn't.”

“It's information about an operation performed under my username, and it's part of the incident I reported.”

“That doesn't mean you're entitled to every routing path.”

“I know. That's why I'm asking for the identifier, not the recipient.”

The young man printed it.

Kael accepted the paper without comment.

It was a short string of letters and numbers, apparently unremarkable.

Until he recognized the prefix.

It matched the first characters of the seal he had seen in the case file.

***

The Archive closed its public reading rooms at six.

Kael stayed until the cleaners began moving through the corridors. Three copies from different sources lay across his desk: the execution identification sheet, the audit log of his request, and the technician's printout.

All three documents shared part of a code.

That did not prove they belonged to the same conspiracy. It could be a coincidence arising from inherited naming conventions.

But it was a specific coincidence, repeated across systems that were not officially supposed to be connected.

Kael opened the old manual and found the reference to series withdrawn by order of continuity.

The index pointed to a volume absent from the inventory.

He went to the historical catalog section and examined the records of collection transfers.

There he found an incomplete entry: decades earlier, a set of indexes had been sent to a preservation repository.

The repository had a name but no public address.

**Administrative Continuity Collection.**

Kael copied the reference into his notebook.

Then he checked the authorization signature on the transfer.

The box was empty.

There were no initials, no stamp, no validation mark.

A missing signature on an old document was hardly extraordinary. Incomplete case files filled entire rooms.

What was extraordinary was that the transfer had gone through anyway, been incorporated into a catalog, and later used as the basis for other operations.

A documentary chain could survive an absence if someone had decided to treat it as valid.

Kael returned to his desk, wrote a formal request to locate the collection, and submitted it through Aldren Meret, just as his supervisor had instructed.

This time he followed procedure to the letter.

He included the reason, the court case number, and the documents supporting the search. He did not omit the routing code or any of the discrepancies he had observed.

Before submitting it, he read the text twice.

He pressed confirm.

The terminal printed a receipt number.

For a moment, he felt satisfied.

Then the system displayed another line:

**Request rejected. Collection administrator not identified.**

Kael stared at the screen.

The request had been rejected because it did not identify the official who managed the collection.

But the entire purpose of the request was to discover who managed it.

He checked the authorization path again.

Instead of a name, he found an institutional reference.

It was the same incomplete code.

He stood, walked to the window, and looked at the lights of Vael-Tir reflected in the river. He thought of the wrong body, the missing signature, an archival collection with no known location, and a system protecting a record by insisting it had never existed.

For the first time, he felt something other than professional irritation.

He felt fear.

Not because he had uncovered a terrible truth.

But because he was beginning to suspect someone had built a procedure designed to prevent the truth from even being requested.

When he returned to his desk, he found a new notification.

**Your request has been forwarded for continuity review.**

Beneath it was a hearing date.

And the name of the person summoned was not Aldren Meret's.

It was his.

***End of Episode 02.***
