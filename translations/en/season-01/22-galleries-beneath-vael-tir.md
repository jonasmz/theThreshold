# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 22: Galleries Beneath Vael-Tir

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Daren Holt, third-person limited.

***

The stability report arrived with a smear of mud on its cover.

Daren Holt accepted it from the municipal architect who had examined the supports beneath the execution chamber. Her name was Elia Torset. She carried a folder of folding plans, three sheets of measurements, and a warning written in red ink.

“The ground can be inspected,” she said. “I didn't say the entire passage can be traversed.”

Daren cleared the correspondence from his desk.

The chamber remained under judicial seal. Odel Mar had shown that the loading mechanism beneath the platform could accommodate a person lying down, and the testimony gathered afterward had exposed an interval that nobody had directly observed. But proving an opening existed beneath the square was one thing. Entering it without causing a collapse was another.

“Which section do you consider safe?” he asked.

Elia unfolded the floor plan.

“From the municipal staircase to this first drainage chamber, with temporary reinforcement, controlled ventilation, and a guarded exit. Beyond it there's a stretch of damp masonry we haven't been able to measure from outside. If the displacement indicator moves, we get out.”

“And the connection to the platform?”

“Geometrically possible. We need to check where they meet from both ends. The modern construction concealed details of the old access.”

Daren studied the edge of the sheet.

The architect's approval had taken several days longer than the court expected. In the meantime, the city had continued paying for guards in the square, and a drainage crew had worked to keep a storm from flooding the preserved opening. There had been no heroism. There had been timber, props, wages, and someone measuring cracks in the morning.

“I'll submit a limited request,” Daren said.

Elia adjusted her coat.

“If the order permits entry beyond the area I've declared safe, it won't make the wall any stronger.”

“Then the order won't permit it.”

***

The magistrate read the application in front of Daren.

The earlier authorization had permitted only preservation of the service entrance and examination of the space beneath the platform. The new request identified a municipal corridor, a drainage chamber, and a length of gallery attributed to the Water Service in historical plans. It included the stability report and required the inspection to stop at any unexamined boundary.

“What do you expect to find?” the judge asked.

“Continuity of a physical route. The execution apparatus was aligned with an old opening. The security plans supplied for the ceremony didn't include it.”

“And if the gallery leads into someone else's property?”

“We'll stop at the boundary. We'll request ownership records and another authorization before crossing it.”

The magistrate held the sheet on which Daren had outlined the permitted perimeter in blue ink.

“The condemned man's defense counsel has requested access to findings relevant to his situation,” he said. “You can't reserve all the results indefinitely on the grounds that you're investigating third parties.”

“We'll provide the appropriate report through the court, without exposing witnesses under protective measures.”

“Have you located the technical assembly supervisor?”

“No. The company still hasn't fully identified its relief crews, and the person named in the contracts hasn't been interviewed. I can't claim he's hiding.”

The judge nodded.

“Don't turn a public-works inspection into a general search of Council installations.”

“I won't.”

The order was signed: inspection of the municipal gallery up to the documented limit of structural safety; sampling under chain of custody; the presence of Water Service personnel and experts; and a prohibition on opening areas under another jurisdiction without fresh authorization.

Daren left with less freedom of action than he would have liked.

For the first time in days, however, it was freedom he could exercise without disputing its legitimacy.

***

It was not raining on the day of the inspection.

That did not mean the ground was dry.

Elia Torset arrived before dawn with two structural assistants. Odel Mar inspected the chamber and the connecting point beneath the stairs. An air technician inserted a probe through the opening to determine whether the atmosphere was breathable. The Water Service sent Cira Venn, the supervisor responsible for the sector, who knew the sluices and could stop any maneuver if the canal level changed.

“I didn't come here to obey orders to walk into a flood,” Cira said, adjusting her helmet.

“Nobody's asked you to,” Daren replied.

“Yet.”

Elia handed her a paper to sign.

Cira read it.

“Limited inspection, drainage operating, no touching valves,” she summarized. “That I can agree to.”

Two workers remained on the surface beside the displacement instruments and a cable communication system. Elia insisted the exit be kept clear, with an assistant noting the time of every descent. A small rescue cart stood near the staircase but was not taken into the gallery; its width would have restricted the passage.

Daren had to change boots. His soles were not suitable for stone made wet by years of seepage.

Odel watched him struggle with the second buckle.

“I never thought the hardest evidence in this case would be an inspector fighting a boot,” he said.

“Write it down. It may be the only thing we can prove without an argument.”

The joke eased the assistants' tension for a moment.

Then they descended.

The first step was worn in the middle. The second dropped farther than expected. The air smelled of wet stone, rust, and stagnant water.

No tram sounds reached them from the square.

Only dripping.

***

The drainage chamber was larger than Daren had expected.

There was a central channel, an elevated walkway along one side, and two maintenance openings closed by grilles. A municipal pump kept the water level low through a conduit running west. The equipment did not operate through some mysterious device: the regular pounding of its pistons carried from the service enclosure farther above.

Cira rapped a wall with her knuckles.

“This gallery predates most of the square's paving. They reinforced it when they diverted an old canal. Since then it's been used to inspect drainage, not transport goods.”

Odel aimed his light at the ceiling.

“But someone left a guide.”

A horizontal recess ran along one wall, far too regular to have been worn by water. It continued from the opening beneath the stairs and disappeared into the passage at the back.

Daren approached without stepping onto the edge.

“A drainage channel?”

“It could support an auxiliary rail,” Odel answered. “Its width resembles the guide we found beneath the apparatus. I need measurements before comparing them.”

The expert marked two points on a ruler and asked an assistant to photograph the relationship with a scale. They called out the record numbers and copied them into the custody notebook.

Daren examined the floor.

Old wheel marks lay beneath mineral deposits. More recent boot prints crossed them. The Water Service had entered several times after the rain, so none of the prints could yet be attributed to an operator of the execution apparatus.

“I can't tell you which ones belong to my people,” Cira said. “We come down to check the pump and sluices. It'll be in the log.”

“We'll need it,” Daren replied.

“It's at the post, with the times. No need to seize it; I can supply a copy.”

He was surprised by the casual offer.

He had grown used to every document requiring three warnings and an unnamed requester. Here, the supervisor of a public pump knew exactly what her log recorded and who had been allowed in.

Odel finished measuring.

“The guide and tray could connect through an intermediate component,” he said. “But that component isn't here, and we still have to check their junction. Two sections having the same width doesn't mean they ever worked together.”

Daren looked toward the staircase.

It was a small but necessary advance.

The route was beginning to have dimensions.

It still had no person.

***

The first interruption came before they reached the passage at the rear.

One of Elia's monitoring rods vibrated as a heavy vehicle crossed the square above them. The movement was slight, but enough for the architect to raise her hand.

“Everyone still.”

The air technician switched off her auxiliary lamp to listen for the signal on the cable.

A surface worker confirmed the movement had stopped and the other markers were stable. Elia requested two further readings before allowing them to proceed.

Daren waited with his back against a wall that seemed to breathe damp.

Odel stood beside him.

“Want to go up?” he asked.

“If the architect orders it.”

“That wasn't the question.”

Daren looked into the dark corridor.

For weeks he had imagined the opening beneath the chamber concealed a simple answer: a passage, an exit, a name. The actual place had cracks, mud, and the possibility of collapsing on anyone who mistook curiosity for permission.

“I don't want anyone hurt because of my case,” he said.

Odel was quiet for a moment.

“Then you've understood the difference between the investigation and the ground.”

Elia authorized them to advance as far as a reinforced brick arch. No farther without checking the support.

They reached a space where the gallery divided. One branch descended toward the pump. The other ended in an iron door with no visible lock on their side and an almost-erased identification plate.

Cira tilted her head.

“We don't use that,” she said. “That branch isn't on my section's current routes.”

“Since when has it been closed?” Daren asked.

“Since before I took over the post. I'll have to find the date. My memory isn't a closure order.”

Daren wrote down her words.

The underground city was beginning to resemble the one above it too closely: every door had an administrative history that no intuition could replace.

***

Odel asked them not to touch the door.

First he photographed the plate, frame, hinges, and mortar joint where iron met stone. Elia measured the distance to the channel and confirmed the arch could not be opened without placing a load on part of the vault.

Three words remained legible on the plate:

*Regulation. Junction. Service.*

Below them, an old inscription had been replaced by a modern inventory number.

“Water regulation?” Daren asked.

Cira examined the valve symbol.

“It doesn't look like a hydraulic sluice mark. The old Water Service symbol wasn't written like that. I can look for it in the municipal plans. That doesn't mean it's connected to anything else today.”

Odel opened his folder and unfolded the historical plan included in the order. It depicted the square before the execution chamber was built and marked an auxiliary gallery ending in a rectangular room. The far end showed no owner's name.

“The direction matches,” he said.

Daren traced the permitted route to the arch with his finger.

“How far does it go on this plan?”

“To a room called the *compensation chamber*. But the term could refer to water pressure, machinery weight, or energy supply. The drawing doesn't say which.”

Cira shook her head.

“If it belonged to the Water Service, it should be in our books. The plans I work from stop here.”

Daren felt no triumph.

They had found a door outside the ordinary maintenance routes and a designation insufficient to identify any institution. It was more than a coincidence of brickwork. Less than proof of a destination.

Elia marked the permitted floor.

“This is where we stop.”

“We haven't opened anything,” Daren said.

“And we won't today.”

She ordered photographs of the boundary and a preservation marker placed on the municipal side, without disturbing the hinges or the old seal. Then she told everyone to retreat.

On the return journey they found a second guide, this one beneath the channel, interrupted by a recent cement repair.

Odel crouched.

“Now that I do want to measure.”

The repair had covered part of the horizontal recess. Two parallel friction marks ran across the cement, still free of mineral deposits.

“When did you lay that material?” he asked.

Cira knelt beside him.

“It's mortar from a drainage job. But I'm not giving you the shift until I've checked the work order. It could have been this month or last.”

The expert shifted his light to photograph it without reflections.

“The marks are on top of the mortar,” he said. “Whatever made them passed after the repair.”

Daren felt a pressure in his chest.

For the first time they had a physical before-and-after sequence that did not depend merely on how new a component looked.

But they still did not know what had made the marks.

***

When they returned toward the surface, water began rising at the foot of the stairs.

Cira noticed before anyone else. The level was not yet dangerous, but the pump's rhythm had changed, and the wet edge had reached a line that had been dry when they descended.

“A relief gate is opening upstream,” she said. “It may be an ordinary maneuver, but I don't want anyone below while we check.”

Elia ordered them to collect the tools.

Daren saw disappointment on one assistant's face as he looked back into the gallery. They had worked all morning to get in and now had to leave without opening the door they had found.

“The investigation ends here for today,” Daren said.

Nobody argued.

They climbed with their hands free to grip the railing. The surface crew counted each person, checked the entry records, and closed the opening with the temporary cover required under the judicial seal.

Cira went directly to the Water Service post to verify the maneuver.

The report arrived before lunch: a scheduled change in level, communicated late to the sector. There was no indication of sabotage. The gate had operated within its limits, although the lack of timely notice had forced the team to evacuate.

“Can I put that in the record?” Daren asked.

“Put that we suspended the inspection because the flow rose, and afterward a routine service maneuver was confirmed,” Cira replied. “If you write ‘suspicious incident,’ I'll sign a disagreement.”

“I'll write what happened.”

Cira studied him.

“Then maybe next time they'll send the warning before we go down.”

***

The work order was recorded in a clothbound ledger.

Cira allowed Daren and Odel to inspect the original page at the municipal post, in the presence of an official who drew up a record. They neither removed the page nor transferred it to a judicial office. They obtained a reproduction identified by folio, date of entry, contractor, and work.

The mortar repair had been completed after the ceremony and after the first judicial closure of the chamber, in a drainage section that had remained under municipal administration.

That did not mean the court had authorized freight movement through it. Nor did it mean no pump maintenance had taken place.

The work report listed crack sealing, a replacement gasket, and cleaning of the channel. It mentioned no rollers, trays, or platforms.

“Did the crew carrying out the repair use carts?” Daren asked.

Cira called one of her workers, who was still in the yard. He examined the order and described their tools: buckets, a small mixer, and a wheelbarrow too wide to fit down the stairs. They had carried the materials by hand.

“Could a heavy component have been wheeled through afterward?” Odel asked.

“Not during our job,” the worker replied. “I don't know what happened once we left.”

That answer was better than an absolute denial.

Odel laid a photograph of the marks over the mortar beside the reproduction.

“They're parallel, but I don't yet know the weight or hardness of the object that made them. They could come from maintenance equipment not listed in this report. I can't identify a tray from two lines.”

Daren felt the story resist closure again.

“Then we need to establish who entered after that work.”

Cira found the access log.

There were two ordinary visits: a pump inspection and a dampness check. Both bore names, signatures, and times. There was also an extraordinary entry recorded against a service-order number that the supervisor had not issued.

“Who authorized it?” Daren asked.

“I don't know. It names another unit, with a shared-infrastructure maintenance code.”

Daren compared its format with documents he had seen during the chamber investigation.

It was similar. Not identical.

“We're not going to assign it to a person because of the form type,” he said.

Cira closed the book.

“What I can certify is that someone used that order to request access to the sector after the repair. The office ought to be able to identify who was responsible.”

“Then we'll ask them.”

***

The historical plans were held by the Public Works office.

They were not a secret collection. They were working copies used to resolve boundary disputes, drainage problems, and repairs. Some sheets were drawn on brittle paper and could only be examined in an official's presence. Others had been reproduced for municipal use.

Daren requested the plans for the square, the sluice system, and the corridor behind the iron door.

The clerk found two editions.

The first showed a rectangular room adjoining the old edge of the depots. The second, drawn up after the network reform, classified it as a *transferred compensation connection*. It recorded no demolition, but rather a change in the organization responsible.

“Transferred to whom?” Daren asked.

The woman opened the transfer ledger.

“The folio refers to an administration responsible for strategic infrastructure. We retain the reference and boundaries here, not the interior inventory.”

“The Council?”

“The receipt seal identifies a supranational body. I can't certify that it still owns the place or retains the same jurisdiction.”

Daren studied the plan.

The route from the execution chamber to the boundary of the old room had geometric continuity, provided the corridor beneath the first vault was used. The plan did not establish that the passage had been clear during the ceremony or that anyone had reached its far end.

But the municipal system no longer ended at a wall without a history.

It ended at the administrative boundary of an old regulation facility.

“I need a copy of the transfer sheet,” Daren said. “Only the reference and the boundaries.”

The clerk prepared a partial certification.

Daren paid the reproduction fee from funds authorized for the investigation and kept the receipt.

The bureaucracy had not opened the door.

It had told him whom to ask about it.

***

Odel's new report did not use the word *escape* in its first paragraph.

It described the physical findings: a guide beneath the chamber, a verifiable alignment with the municipal gallery, geometric capacity to move a human-sized load, a recently repaired section bearing wheel marks made after the repair, and a branch ending at a historical entrance under another jurisdiction.

The expert included alternative explanations.

The marks might have been made by unidentified maintenance equipment.

The guide might belong to an older loading system reused without any connection to a criminal act.

The compensation chamber might be inactive, with the corridor blocked beyond the boundary.

The extraordinary order number might belong to a legitimate service whose documentation had not yet been produced.

Nothing established the destination or present condition of Taren Oss.

Daren read the last page while Odel brushed dried mud from his instruments.

“How long would a tray take to travel the distance we saw?” he asked.

“I'm not giving you a time from a distance and a slope,” the expert answered. “We don't know the pulling force, the condition of the rail on the day of the ceremony, or where the load was handed over. A reconstruction would require a model and controlled tests.”

Daren nodded.

“What can I ask the judge for?”

“Preservation of the access and a request that the owner of the infrastructure beyond it identify the connections and their records. Comparison of the work orders and subsequent entries. If someone wants to examine the interior, let them do it with technicians who know its hazards.”

“And the municipal gallery?”

“We don't need to shut down Water's pump to preserve our evidence. We can protect the friction marks with a reversible cover and leave maintenance crews a passage. All documented.”

Daren remembered Cira's expression when she saw the water rise. They had reached a physical boundary because other people had done their jobs carefully. If the investigation now prevented drainage maintenance, the court might end up causing the very harm it was supposed to avoid.

“Propose the cover,” he said. “I want every subsequent access documented, and I want the city to keep functioning.”

***

The judge received the report at a closed hearing with a prosecution representative and the court clerk.

Daren explained where the expert findings ended and inference began. The access from the square could not be considered a public exit. It had been concealed by the chamber's installation and coincided with a service route absent from the municipal police's security plan.

“Does this prove the condemned man escaped?” the prosecutor asked.

“No. It proves the chamber allowed a physical transfer toward a corridor whose existence and partial continuity we now know. Alongside the nonmatching corpse and testimony about the interior phase, that provides grounds to investigate the hypothesis further.”

“And the extraordinary access number entered after the repair?”

“There's an order in the municipal log. Its author and purpose haven't been established. The wheel marks postdate the mortar; we can't yet attribute them to that particular visit.”

The magistrate looked at the Public Works plan.

“The historical room lies beyond the municipal boundary.”

“Yes.”

“Then I won't authorize opening a Council installation under an order issued to inspect a Water Service gallery.”

Daren was grateful he said it before some official suggested otherwise.

“I request a preservation and identification notice to the body named in the transfer, and a coordinated inspection if it confirms its jurisdiction and the technicians consider access safe. If it objects, the issue will have to be resolved through the proper channels.”

The prosecutor took notes.

“We also need to identify the maintenance order that permitted the later entry,” she added.

“And preserve its original history,” Daren said. “Not a list reconstructed from statements.”

The judge signed the limited measures.

The investigation into Taren's whereabouts would continue.

No definitive death certificate would be issued for him on the basis of the body presented.

And the end of the gallery would be protected while its administrator was identified.

***

Back in the square, Daren found Cira beside the stairwell opening.

Workers had installed a safety barrier and a notice explaining why part of the paving remained closed. A boy tried to drag a wheelbarrow along the work's edge; Cira made him go around the square.

“If we don't close it like this, we'll have another injured person tomorrow,” she said.

Daren showed her the preservation order.

“We're not stopping the pump. The expert will install reversible protection over the marks, and you'll keep your maintenance access, with notice to the court whenever you enter the protected section.”

Cira read the text.

“That I can comply with.”

“We also need the extraordinary order identified in the access log.”

“I've already asked the liaison service for its complete number. They haven't answered.”

“When they do, send a certified copy. If they don't, make a record of the refusal.”

The supervisor folded the sheet.

“Inspector, one thing.”

“Go ahead.”

“I've worked here since before they remodeled the square. I always thought the old works were closed because nobody had the money to repair them. Now it turns out some have owners I don't even know how to contact.”

Daren looked at the service door exposed behind the dismantled partition. During the ceremony, a temporary wall had concealed something as concrete as a staircase.

“Not knowing who's responsible doesn't mean there isn't someone,” he said.

Cira nodded.

“Then I hope they answer you. I still have to pump the water out.”

Daren could not tell whether that was a farewell or a warning.

Probably both.

***

One last communication was waiting at the courthouse.

It did not come from the Council. It was a certified copy of the Water Service's visitor log, sent by Cira with a note from the sluice keeper. After examining the original page, they had established that the extraordinary number belonged to the same day an unidentified crew requested access to the drainage sector.

Daren compared the times.

The visit took place after the mortar repair, well after the execution. The register showed entry and exit, but described neither tools nor any object transported. The sluice keeper did not recall seeing cargo. He had admitted the crew because a valid service number existed in the municipal system.

Daren could not claim that crew had made the marks.

He could say someone had retained the administrative ability to request entry into a gallery supposedly no longer used for moving loads.

He opened a new investigation folder and separated three questions:

*What was transported?*

*Who authorized access?*

*Where did the gallery continue?*

The first remained unanswered.

The third led to a historical installation whose responsible authority still had to be identified.

The second had just become urgent.

Daren returned to Odel's report. Two clean, parallel lines stood out over the fresh mortar. Nobody could yet say what mechanism had left them.

But they could no longer be attributed automatically to builders from long ago.

The gallery had been used after the city believed it had closed.

And whoever entered had not needed to break down a door.

**All they needed was a service order nobody could yet explain.**

***End of Episode 22.***
