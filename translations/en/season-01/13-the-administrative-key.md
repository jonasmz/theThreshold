# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 1 · Episode 13: The Administrative Key

**Editorial status:** English literary translation, draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, third-person limited.

***

The letter bore three seals, two signatures, and no answer to the question Kael had asked.

It arrived shortly after the Central Archive opened. The Delegation's courier demanded proof of delivery and refused to leave the envelope at the counter. Kael signed the receipt, compared the number on the outside with that of his request, and sat by the window to read it.

The Continuity authority acknowledged that there was a procedure for requesting a provenance review. It also stated that the Administrative Continuity Collection could not be consulted with ordinary clearance. It did not identify the person responsible for the series, but this time it included an appeal procedure and a deadline.

Someone had corrected the most obvious defect in the previous response.

They had left the problem itself untouched.

Kael marked the last sentence:

*The appellant must establish the relevance of the reference by means of a document issued by the authority responsible for the collection.*

He needed the signature of an authority he could not identify until someone allowed him to consult the very record he was looking for.

A second page offered an alternative: the Archive could certify the connection between two references without releasing their contents, provided the request came through the analyst's supervisor and the provenance unit accepted jurisdiction.

It was not access. It was an administrative key that only opened another door.

“Do you call that an improvement?” asked Tessa Ord, who had come to leave him a guide to the indexes.

Kael showed her the second page.

“There's a procedure we can try.”

“That doesn't answer my question.”

“Compared with a refusal that doesn't name the responsible authority, yes.”

Tessa checked the clock.

“Aldren expects you at nine. He asked me to bring him a copy of this response, too.”

“Did he say why?”

“No. And even if I knew, I'd tell you to discuss it with him.”

Kael folded the document along the marked filing line. He had two hours to prepare a request that did not confuse three different things: the recent maintenance document, the technical signature that had validated it, and the historical series that might identify the issuing authority.

If he mixed all three together, someone could reject all three with a single sentence.

***

Aldren Meret did not look up when Kael entered.

His office was arranged with almost offensive precision. Correspondence folders formed one stack; procedural files another. The wooden box by the window still had no label.

“Your audit of the issuing authority can no longer proceed as an ordinary inquiry,” he said.

“That's why I requested a provenance review.”

“You requested it after seeking an outside technical verification.”

“The Authentication Service verified only the structure of a signature. It didn't examine the restricted collection.”

Aldren held the report by one corner.

“Where did you get this copy?”

“The Technical Section provided it when the procedure closed. It's certified as an extract of the response.”

“I know. I want you to explain why it isn't among the working copies you registered when your assignment began.”

Kael looked at the date of receipt.

“Because it didn't exist then. It was added afterward.”

“And you didn't update the list.”

It was true.

Kael had added the report to his correspondence folder and logged its arrival in his work diary, but not in the controlled-reproductions register. Until then, every office had treated the document as though responsibility for filing it belonged to someone else.

“I should have,” he said.

Aldren looked at him for the first time.

“You're not the only one who made a mistake. The difference is that you know how to reconstruct a chain of custody.”

“I can correct it with a late-entry notice. Without changing the original date.”

“That would be proper. What I don't know is whether you'll be allowed to continue working on the case.”

Kael felt his back stiffen.

“Why?”

Aldren pointed to a new official letter. The Delegation had requested an examination of inquiries linked to the Continuity Collection. Until it was complete, it recommended restricting access for the user who had attempted to establish connections between a reserved series and maintenance documents.

“My account,” Kael said.

“Your name isn't in the recommendation.”

“An exact description of my operations is.”

“That doesn't automatically make it a disciplinary measure.”

“But it could become one.”

“Yes.”

Aldren closed the letter.

“If you continue, I need everything to go through the review channel. No more side requests to units outside our authority.”

“The units I consulted were authorized to answer.”

“The problem isn't only jurisdiction. It's that each unit sees one part, and none will accept responsibility for the whole.”

Kael remembered the three copies he had spread across his table at home. Three sources and three different purposes. Now someone was trying to treat them as if they had all come out of the same envelope.

“I want to file the appeal,” he said.

Aldren rubbed the bridge of his nose.

“I'll authorize you to submit it as the assigned analyst, subject to my review. If I receive a formal order suspending your permissions, I'll have to comply.”

“Understood.”

“Really?”

Kael took a moment.

“I understand that you'll obey an order you're required to obey. That doesn't mean I agree with the grounds for it.”

Aldren almost smiled.

“At least you're still distinguishing those two things.”

***

The Archives comparison room had a single table and a window of frosted glass.

Kael arrived with Tessa, who had been assigned to witness the consultation. The authorization permitted a comparison of the headings of two instruments: the historical manual published for inspectors and the technical validation extract added to the investigation. It did not permit opening the restricted series or duplicating its contents.

The Delegation official introduced herself as Ardis Ren.

Kael had seen her signature on an earlier reply. They had never met.

“I can't guarantee that the office responsible for the collection will accept this correspondence,” Ardis said as she checked the folder's seals.

“I'm only asking that we record whether the identifiers are compatible.”

“Compatibility doesn't establish an issuing authority.”

“I haven't claimed otherwise.”

Ardis looked at Tessa.

“Does he always answer like that?”

“When he's in a good mood,” the archivist replied.

The official did not smile, but she moved to the table.

The procedure was brief and more physical than Kael expected. Ardis placed the open manual on a clean surface. Tessa verified the reproduction number and the extract's seal. Kael read aloud the sections he proposed to compare: the validator family, its declared period of use, and the structure of the response sequence.

He was not looking for identical codes. Systems that had survived several migrations might have changed their identifiers.

He wanted to know whether the modern signature referred to the same technical family the manual placed before the Great Silence.

“The headings belong to compatible families,” Ardis said when they finished. “That can go on the record.”

“And the issuing authority?”

“The extract doesn't identify it. Neither does the manual. I have no authorization to resolve that by inference.”

Kael had expected that limitation.

“Then I'd like the classification discrepancy recorded: a recent operation uses a family described as retired in a publicly accessible manual.”

Ardis held his gaze for several seconds.

“It can be recorded as a documented discrepancy. Not as proof that an ancient authority remains operational.”

“Agreed.”

Tessa wrote down the exact formulation.

Ardis lifted the cover of the comparison file and removed a separator sheet. Beneath it was a preprinted form with four boxes.

**Inquiry accepted. Inquiry restricted. Inquiry referred. Security incident.**

The third box had been checked before the comparison began.

Kael pointed to it.

“When was the decision made to refer this?”

“When the session was scheduled,” Ardis replied. “It doesn't mean a conclusion was reached in advance. It means our office lacks jurisdiction to decide the substance of the request.”

“Who will receive the referral?”

The official indicated the field below. It contained a service address, not a person's name.

Kael recognized the prefix.

It was the same one that had appeared in the first notification on his terminal.

The persistence of a code did not prove that it belonged to the same present-day unit. Routes could preserve their names even when the people responsible changed. But it compelled him to ask.

“May I request confirmation that the recipient received it?”

“You can request it,” Ardis said. “I can't promise they'll give it to you.”

Kael signed the comparison record.

Before he left, Ardis handed him a certified copy of the unrestricted section. Her signature appeared at the bottom beside Tessa's.

For the first time in days, he had obtained a new and strictly limited finding through a procedure the offices themselves were willing to recognize.

He had not obtained the main answer.

***

At noon, Daren Holt called from the courthouse.

“I have a question about your technical report,” he said. “The copy you gave me for the square case doesn't include the authentication appendix you're mentioning now.”

“That was added later. The appendix wasn't part of the original criminal-case documentation.”

“The judge wants us to clarify exactly what was obtained and under what authorization.”

Kael leaned back in his chair.

“I can send a record identifying the comparison procedure. I can't send a restricted collection.”

“I'm not asking you to.”

“I know. I want to avoid turning a limited certification into a shortcut to something neither of us is allowed to touch.”

Daren let out a short breath.

“For once, I'm grateful you corrected me before I finished asking. My question's simpler: is the report in the Archive's custody, or do you have it?”

Kael looked at his working folder.

There was an authorized copy of the extract; another was entered in the technical receipt register. The first lay on his desk because he had used it that morning.

“The working copy is my responsibility,” he said. “The original receipt copy remains within the Archive's records system.”

“Is your possession recorded in a register of copies?”

Kael let a second pass.

“I'm correcting an omission in the register.”

The inspector's silence was different from Aldren's.

“Do that before you move anything,” Daren said. “And if someone asks you to hand over documents relevant to the court, insist on a written record. Don't turn my office into a hiding place.”

“I won't.”

“Seriously, Kael. If you lose access over an administrative matter, that doesn't give me the right to receive material I can't lawfully add to the case.”

“I understand.”

“Then we agree on something. Write it down.”

Kael ended the call with a smile that did not last long.

He opened the controlled-reproductions register and prepared a supplementary entry: actual date of acquisition, purpose, current location, copy seal, and the omission discovered. He did not alter the original receipt date. When Tessa reviewed it, she noted that the entry had been made after the document was used.

“I don't like seeing you file a formal observation against yourself,” she said.

“It's better than having someone discover the omission later and guess what I did.”

“That's very you.”

Kael waited for her signature.

Then he took the form to the internal registry.

***

When he left the office, he found two identification cards on his desk.

His own, blue, and a new gray one granting provisional access.

The second could not be his. He had not requested a different clearance.

The personnel officer was waiting by the door.

“The register received an instruction to review your permissions,” she explained. “Your current badge is now valid only for entry to the building. To work with assigned collections, you'll need Integrity to approve each consultation.”

“Since when?”

“Fifteen minutes ago.”

“Whose name is on the gray badge?”

“It's a temporary authorization. It lets you collect personal belongings from work areas and attend administrative meetings. It doesn't permit reading historical series.”

Kael took both cards.

“Does the instruction give a reason?”

The woman showed him the notice.

**Preventive compliance review.**

It did not say disciplinary suspension. Nor did it say he could continue working.

On the floor below, a bell announced the beginning of the lunch break. People passing in the corridor kept talking about schedules, duty shifts, and rosters. Nothing about the building seemed to have changed.

Kael tried his blue badge at the consultation repository door.

The light turned red.

He stood still long enough for an assistant to offer to fetch the shift supervisor.

“No need,” he said.

He had asked that every decision be verifiable.

This one was.

***

Tessa accompanied him to the work-document conservation room. It still housed sturdy cardboard boxes, mechanical seals, and a lending ledger that had survived three technological reforms.

“Aldren wants you to inventory your reproductions before leaving,” she said. “They'll revoke access to your desk when your shift ends.”

“Was he here?”

“A little while ago. It looks as though he received the order at the same time you did.”

Kael did not know whether to believe it. Mentally, he recorded it as a statement from Tessa, not an established fact.

They laid out the technical extract, the unrestricted copy of the comparison record, the public equivalence references, and the consultation notes. They separated institutional originals from his personal notes. Everything received through a formal channel would remain traceable within that channel.

The problem appeared with a small green-covered folder.

It contained two copies of technical correspondence that Kael had retained for comparison with the indexes. They had been legally reproduced and registered as working documents at the time; they were still copies belonging to the Archive. His clearance to use them had expired when his access assignment changed.

“These have to stay here,” Tessa said.

“Yes.”

“Then why are you looking at them like that?”

Because the lending record showed an anomaly.

The green folder was authorized for internal comparison, but the returns system had generated a transfer instruction to the same service address as the other documents. There was no responsible person or physical repository listed. The transfer date was that very morning.

Kael compared the identifier with the notification.

“Can a service order automatically claim a folder that's out on loan?”

“It can generate a movement request,” Tessa said. “It shouldn't be executed without a handover record.”

“And if it's processed as a return?”

The archivist checked the auxiliary electronic ledger.

“Then the counter operator could accept it as an ordinary return and leave it in a distribution tray.”

Kael looked at the two copies.

He did not know whether the referral was legitimate. Nor did he know whether someone had tried to remove the contents. The irregular transfer case he had heard rumors about at the Delegation gave him no license to accuse another office.

But the instruction had an immediate consequence: the documents could leave their known location before the review justifying their retention had ended.

“I want a custody incident entered in the record,” he said.

Tessa frowned.

“That will mean halting the return. The room supervisor may consider it a refusal to comply with the order.”

“I'm not refusing to return the copies. I'm refusing to accept a request with no physical recipient as proof of return.”

“There's a distinction. I can't promise they'll recognize it.”

Kael wrote his reservation in the lending ledger. He recorded the two document numbers, their condition, the time, and the destination field that failed to identify a physical custodian. Tessa signed as the receiving witness, without endorsing any theory about the reason for the order.

The green folder remained inside a sealed box in the conservation room, available to the competent supervisor and subject to transfer review. Kael kept only his personal acknowledgment of the incident and the identification details the form expressly allowed him to retain.

He had not removed documents from the institution.

He had prevented their return from being confused with their disappearance.

***

Rain began in the late afternoon.

Kael walked home because it was still a while until the next tram, and he did not want to sit down again with an imaginary case file between his hands.

On the corner near his house, a woman sold fruit beneath a red awning. The sliced orange segments gave off a sweet, almost bitter smell that stopped him before he had decided to buy anything.

He asked for two oranges.

In his kitchen, he lit a lamp and took out a chipped porcelain plate. It was not the plate from his childhood; that one had broken years ago. He cut the first orange into uneven pieces and arranged them around the rim.

Rain struck the glass.

He remembered a little table beside a window lower than his own. He remembered the hand of the woman who had raised him pushing the plate toward him, the peel curling onto a cloth, and the sound of the drops: the deep percussion on the veranda's wood, the rattle in the metal gutter, the soft taps on the glass.

“If you listen carefully, everything tells you what it's made of.”

Kael had tried to guess without looking. She had laughed when he mistook the gutter for a cooking pot and had him close his eyes to listen again.

He could not remember how old he had been that afternoon. He did remember the smell the orange left on his fingers and the small, comfortable embarrassment of getting something wrong when it did not matter.

The scene returned so completely that he wanted to stay beside the window until the storm ended.

Instead, he sat down to write.

He did not copy any restricted documents. He drafted a personal record of events he had witnessed: who had given him the receipts, which procedure he had requested, when his permissions had been restricted, and where the copies remained deposited under a signed record. For every statement, he added the source that could confirm it, even if that source was beyond his reach.

He might be wrong about his suspicions.

He did not want someone else to have to guess what he had done.

When he finished, half the orange pieces still lay on the plate.

The rain had eased.

***

Daren answered the call from the judicial office.

“I'm listening,” he said.

“My permissions have been restricted. The Archive is holding the institutional copies under a custody-incident record and has acknowledged receipt.”

“Were you ordered to give them to me?”

“No. And I won't. I only want you to know where they are in case the magistrate requests their preservation through the proper channels.”

Daren was silent for several seconds.

“Is there a concrete risk they'll disappear?”

“There's a transfer instruction with no identified physical recipient on the working copy I examined. That's all I can state.”

“Then if appropriate, we'll ask the competent authority to preserve the materials and document every movement. Not to send us the entire collection.”

Kael was surprised by how grateful he felt that the inspector made no attempt to simplify a difficult situation.

“I also want it on record that I corrected an omission in my copies register,” he said.

“Your omission?”

“Yes.”

“Did you correct it before they removed your access?”

“Yes. The entry is dated as a late addition.”

“Good. That doesn't absolve you of every possible breach, but it makes investigating the matter possible.”

“That's what I hope.”

“Will you keep working on it?”

Kael looked at his hands. They still smelled of orange.

“I can't do so as the assigned analyst anymore. I can testify about what I saw and ask for the records to be preserved.”

“That distinction matters.”

“I know.”

Daren exhaled.

“And Kael, don't come back tomorrow as though nothing happened. If they've suspended you, don't enter using someone else's clearance.”

“I won't.”

He hung up.

It was strange that the warning sounded more like friendship than the inspector's invitations to dinner.

***

The next morning, a representative from Personnel received him in a windowless room.

There was an empty table, two chairs, and a jug of water. Aldren arrived shortly afterward, carrying a folder he did not set on the table until all three were seated.

“You've been notified of a precautionary suspension from documentary duties,” the Personnel representative said. “It is not a final sanction. A procedure will be opened to determine whether you breached your recordkeeping and preservation obligations.”

Kael asked to read the resolution.

It cited his appeal request, the comparison of headings, and the late addition of a technical report to the register of copies. It did not accuse him of falsifying anything. It added that he had retained institutional reproductions after being notified of a return order.

“The materials were held in the conservation room,” Kael said. “With a custody-incident record and a witness's signature. The copies are still there.”

“That is your response,” the representative said. “We'll include it.”

“I also request that the lending register and the transfer instruction be preserved unchanged while the review is resolved.”

Aldren made a note.

“I'll demand that in writing,” he said. “I can't promise every movement will be suspended, but I can insist that its audit trail and responsible parties are preserved.”

Kael read the document again.

The suspension affected work with collections and documentary systems. He retained the right to respond, obtain a copy of his own disciplinary file, and be represented at hearings. He was not stripped of his status as a witness to events he had already seen.

“I'll sign to acknowledge receipt, not agreement,” he said.

The representative nodded and showed him where.

When Kael put down the pen, Aldren handed him another sheet.

It was not included in the precautionary resolution.

It was a summons to a disciplinary hearing on an approaching date. The legal office had asked to examine whether his conduct amounted to irregular removal of copies and refusal to comply with superior instructions. The wording was more serious than the measure he had just received.

Kael looked up.

“Who signed this expansion?”

“The compliance directorate,” Aldren replied. “It's being handled separately. You'll have a chance to answer.”

“And the documents they say I removed?”

Aldren slowly closed his folder.

“That's what they'll have to prove.”

The Personnel representative held out a hand for the blue badge.

Kael placed it on the table.

For the first time since he had started working at the Archive, the room's door opened for him as a visitor.

***End of Episode 13.***
