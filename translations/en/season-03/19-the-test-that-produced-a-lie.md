# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 19: The Test That Produced a Lie

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Sera Nhal, limited third person.

***

The report accused a seal of having been altered.

Sera Nhal received it with a red mark beside two Resonance curves. One came from an old reproduction preserved by an archive. The other came from a later reading taken on a reference support. Both referred to the same descriptive collection, but they had not been produced with the same instrument or at the same temperature.

The author had written: “The deviation confirms interference with the document.”

“The deviation doesn't confirm that,” Sera said.

Nel Or studied the lines.

“They're different.”

“They'd also be different if someone had changed the support or the reference. Who recorded the temperature?”

No one had recorded it for the earlier reading. The modern one included a number but did not specify whether it referred to the instrument, the room, or the plate being measured.

An audit commission asked Sera to assess whether the report could be presented as technical evidence. Her authorization covered the method and low-power instruments, not the restricted originals. She could not state what had happened centuries earlier. She could, however, test the rule the report used to turn a difference between curves into an accusation.

The author of the opinion arrived at the workshop, his expression closed.

“Are you planning to prove the curves are identical?”

“No,” Sera replied. “I want to test whether your criterion distinguishes between alteration of the support and an ordinary difference in measurement.”

The man set a notebook on the table.

“People are waiting for answers.”

“Then we shouldn't give them one the instrument can't support.”

***

The bench was prepared with three modern plates, not historical documents.

Two had been stamped with the same die. The third had received a deliberate, documented modification so it could serve as an intervention control. An external laboratory retained the identification key until the experiment was complete. The reader remained isolated, powered by a workshop accumulator and fitted with the independent cutoff used in earlier calibrations.

Sera established two measurement conditions: complete thermal rest and residual temperature within a known safe limit. No one would run the equipment outside its protocol. Repetitions with residual heat would be performed only after the authorized recovery interval had elapsed.

The report's author set out his comparison criterion in writing before seeing the keys.

“If the deviation exceeds this band, I'll say the material has been altered.”

“And if it doesn't?” the independent expert asked. “Will you say it hasn't been altered?”

The man hesitated.

“I'll say we can't detect it.”

Sera had the distinction entered in the record. A negative test was not proof of absolute integrity.

In the first series, the instrument separated the two untouched plates with slightly different readings because of their surface roughness. The differences fell within the measured error. The third produced a distinguishable signal, although its value depended on the supporting surface.

In the second series, changing only the mounting arrangement within the permitted conditions, one of the untouched plates produced a deviation greater than the threshold the opinion treated as proof of forgery.

Nel looked at the author.

“This one hasn't been altered.”

“That's what the key you're keeping says.”

The external expert opened the identification envelope in front of them all. The laboratory that prepared the plates certified the marks and initial condition; a second technician had inspected their surfaces without knowing the order of the test.

The criterion had produced a false positive.

***

The argument continued in a room without instruments.

The report's author wanted to keep his opinion with a footnote. Sera requested that the causal conclusion be withdrawn and that the observed result be described instead: different curves obtained under conditions that were not fully comparable.

“You can't rule out that the old document was tampered with,” he protested.

“I'm not ruling it out,” she answered. “I'm ruling out the claim that this deviation alone proves it.”

The legal observer asked about the scope of the test. Sera explained that a reproducible modern experiment refuted the proposed universal rule; it did not reconstruct the particular surface of the historical material. That item would require a chain-of-custody history, a manufacturing reference, and permission for physical examination. Some of those things might never exist.

The author closed his notebook.

“The press already knows there are two curves.”

“Then it will also need to know that two curves can differ for different reasons.”

The commission decided to remove the allegation based solely on that comparison from its next report. It would retain the readings as data with uncertainty and record the methodological correction. It did not declare any old document authentic.

Sera stepped into the workshop yard. The municipal station had sent a maintenance notice: the backup bearing was still wearing down, even though the startup schedules had been corrected. The audit was consuming working hours and money that did not appear in its curves.

Nel caught up with her.

“Did we just tell the people waiting for an answer that we have nothing?”

“We have something. We know one explanation was false as evidence. That keeps us from making decisions based on a lie.”

Nel stood beside her as a cart unloaded crates of spare parts.

“It sounds less heroic than it ought to.”

“Perhaps that's why someone has to do it.”

***

The corrected copy was sent to two archives and the editors of the public report.

Sera insisted on attaching the experimental conditions: modern standards, workshop energy, temperature, supports, uncertainty, and the distinction between a false positive and the authenticity of any particular document. The information approved for release excluded settings capable of interfering with nodes and operational Transit references.

The commission also received the results of two comparisons made by independent custodians. Those comparisons did not depend on the rejected curves. They described dispatch numbers, receipt records, and administrative dating rules. Sera took no part in their archival validation, but checked that the technical report no longer purported to discredit them on the basis of poorly explained instrumental incompatibility.

“Does that mean the dates can be compared after all?” Nel asked.

“It means we can't use our curves to declare them fraudulent. The custodians will have to do their work with their own originals.”

Before Sera returned to the station, the author of the opinion approached her.

“My report is going to be cited as a mistake.”

“Your method was corrected with evidence,” Sera said. “You can put on record that you accepted the review, or challenge it with other experiments.”

The man looked at the powered-down bench.

“I was afraid that if I didn't say something definite, no one would listen.”

Sera thought of the technicians who had to go on containing an installation without promising to open it, and of the families still paying for the closed roads.

“People deserve a useful answer,” she said. “Not necessarily a comfortable one.”

Back at the station, she checked the flow records first. The operators had maintained essential service without exceeding the reserve. Only afterward did she file the calibration data.

One separate sheet remained: the documentary comparison of the old dates was ready for public review by the people who held the sources. The two archives had not placed their records on the same table, but they had agreed how to explain the equivalence of their calendars.

Sera added no historical conclusion.

She turned off the bench light.

The machine had already said everything it could.

And for the first time, its silence made the documents easier to hear.

***End of Episode 19.***
