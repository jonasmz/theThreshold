# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 05: The Road That Wasn't on the Record

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Lyra Saren, limited third person.

***

The stamp said “delivered.” The woman who was supposed to receive the cargo said otherwise.

Lyra Saren held the manifest up to a window at the dock. The shipment contained bandages, dried food, and water filters for a valley community that had lost its usual supply route. Since the restricted corridors ceased operating, carts had been using a two-day detour and relying on a bridge that could only carry light loads.

The receiving signature came from the city's transport office, not the destination.

Ema pointed to the name.

“That belongs to the intermediate depot. The contract says the first carrier's responsibility ends there.”

“And another carrier's begins, one who hasn't signed anything yet,” Lyra replied.

The woman in charge of the valley's medical post spoke over the messaging line, her voice rough and her patience thin. They had received some of the food from an earlier convoy, but neither the filters nor the bandages. The community well needed repairs, and their reserve supplies would last only a few days.

“I can't treat people with a copy of a receipt,” she said.

Lyra couldn't send a traveler through a closed Threshold, either. There was no authorized destination, no reserved energy, and no approved crew. Her experience with routes granted her no exception.

“I'll locate the physical cargo,” she replied. “Then I'll tell you what we can transport, when, and under what conditions. Not before.”

The woman went quiet.

“I've already been given two dates that weren't met.”

“That's why I won't invent a third.”

***

The cargo was under a leaking roof.

The manager of the intermediate depot opened the doors to Lyra, Ema, and an inspector hired by the senders. Three boxes of bandages still had their seals. The bags of dried food were on raised pallets. The filters, however, stood in a damp corner of the floor.

“The connecting vehicle never came,” the manager explained. “We had everything ready.”

“When did you report the delay?” Ema asked.

The man showed her a message without an acknowledgment of receipt.

Lyra examined the outside labels. One box of filters had a dark streak along its base. She couldn't be sure its contents were ruined without opening it under the proper procedure. Nor could she treat it as undamaged after seeing it sitting in moisture.

The inspector photographed the condition, location, date, and seals. The compromised units were set aside for the manufacturer to assess; the others were moved into dry storage. The depot acknowledged that its receipt meant arrival at an intermediate point, not final delivery.

“If you publish it that way, my boss will say we lost the goods,” the manager protested.

“If we sign as the destination for something that never got there, the people waiting for it won't be able to claim it,” Lyra said.

The man moved out of their way.

The inspection took all morning. The connecting carrier still hadn't answered. At midday, news arrived: its vehicle had been reassigned to another load following a verbal instruction, with no amendment to the contract.

Ema wanted the facts recorded before they argued over blame. Lyra agreed. They could seek reimbursement later; the filters couldn't wait for the dispute to end.

***

There were two alternatives, and neither was good.

One company offered to take the boxes as far as the bridge and transfer them to light carts. They would have to unload in two stages and pay for overnight security. Another proposed using a riverboat, departing at dawn with an uncertain arrival time because of the water level.

Lyra had traveled that river years before. She remembered the currents along its banks, the type of hull that could carry the load, and the price of a delay. She had also learned that remembering a route was not the same as having a current inspection.

She requested the boat's operating permits and the latest navigation notice. The upper stretch was clogged with sediment, and the permit allowed loads lighter than the full shipment.

The cart operator demanded payment in advance. Ema calculated the shelter's reserves.

“We can pay for the bandages and some of the filters. The food would have to leave tomorrow with the regular convoy.”

“Who guarantees unloading?” Lyra asked.

“The valley cooperative, if they agree to receive it at night.”

The medical-post supervisor listened to the plan. She agreed to organize volunteers to unload a small vehicle, but asked that they not be sent anything requiring technical handling without safe instructions.

Lyra ruled out the damp filters until they could be assessed. The manufacturer, consulted by the inspector, could not approve them over the telephone. It authorized only the separation of a batch showing no signs of moisture, with the rest retained for examination.

“So we're sending less than they need,” Ema said.

“We're sending what we know they can use.”

Ema looked at the expense sheet. The new transport costs left a planned repair at the shelter unfunded: an exterior shutter that protected the shared dormitory from the wind.

“We'll feel that this week.”

Lyra felt an impulse to pay the entire difference herself. Her own resources had dwindled after months of journeys, treatments, and commitments. A heroic gesture without real money behind it would only postpone the same debt.

“We'll take it to the shelter assembly,” she said. “Let them decide whether to accept the expense and how to replenish the funds. I'm not going to turn their budget into my excuse.”

***

The assembly was not friendly.

Two people argued for keeping the money for the building. Another recalled that when the shelter had needed supplies, the valley cooperative had lent them vehicles without charging. Ema presented the accounts, including the extra fee for security and the amount that might be claimed from the carrier that had failed to provide the connection.

Lyra explained that no one could guarantee a prompt reimbursement. Nor did she claim that the shipment would repair the supply system.

The assembly voted to cover the urgent transport of the verified boxes and postpone a noncritical portion of the repair, provided temporary protection was installed and a date set to review the budget. One person voted against. Their objection was entered into the record.

That night, Lyra accompanied the first cart only as far as the checkpoint. Her physical limitations prevented her from making the full journey into the valley, and she needed the driver not to depend on her presence. She checked the manifest, insurance, weight, seals, and recipient. The second vehicle was prepared with unloading tools, not with more cargo than its authorization allowed.

At dawn, the valley cooperative's acknowledgment of receipt arrived.

It bore three signatures: the driver, the medical-post supervisor, and a community representative. The document listed every box received, a discrepancy in weight compared with the point of origin, and two seals with scratches already noted at the depot. No one wrote “all in order” before inspecting them.

The medical-post supervisor called again.

“We have bandages and some of the filters. Today's care can continue.”

Lyra rested her forehead against the window frame.

“There's another delivery still outstanding.”

“I know. But now I know exactly what's missing.”

It was not gratitude for a rescue. It was the possibility of making a claim backed by a real fact.

Ema appeared with a new letter. It came from another community once supplied through the same restricted corridors. Its food depot was running low, and the alternative road route passed through a civilian station whose capacity had been reduced.

Lyra read the station's name, then the quantities requested.

She could organize one more shipment.

She could not promise one for everyone.

***End of Episode 05.***
