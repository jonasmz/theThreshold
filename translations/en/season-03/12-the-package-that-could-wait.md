# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 12: The Package That Could Wait

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Lyra Saren, limited third person.

***

The message came from a transport office Lyra Saren had sworn never to use again.

It wasn't a threat. It was a commercial offer with far too many conditions left unstated.

A carrier licensed for overland consignments could collect a certified copy of an old index in an outside jurisdiction and deliver it to Vael-Tir. The document had been requested by an audit office, but the custodian's reply required controlled physical delivery: the communications link could not transmit a complete certification, and the original was not to leave the archive.

Ema read the proposal.

“They're charging for a journey we don't even know they can finish.”

“And using the word 'urgent' to push up the price,” Lyra replied.

The past few weeks had used up much of the shelter's margin. They still needed to assess the filters left at the damp depot and repair the shutter in the common room. Ema had not authorized spending a single coin on a historical investigation.

Lyra kept the matter separate. She could help evaluate routes under a contract funded by the audit. She had no right to use the shelter's budget or transport a record whose provenance she did not know.

She asked the bidder for a current license, itinerary, preservation conditions, and liability terms in the event of loss. The man suggested an alternative.

“I know a much faster connection. An old stretch. It avoids the bridge inspections.”

Lyra recognized what he was hinting at before he said “Threshold.” The connection he was suggesting had no authorization and no verified destination. There wasn't even evidence of a stabilized Anchor.

“Then you don't have a route,” she said. “You have a promise without an installation.”

The man ended the call.

Ema put the paper on the table.

“Do you still remember how to reach that stretch?”

Lyra thought of the spaces in her memory that wishing would never restore.

“I remember hearing about it. That's not the same thing.”

***

The office holding the index agreed to speak with Lyra over an official line.

The archivist explained that the source material was a catalog of referrals, not a traveler register or a sequence for operating equipment. They could issue a certified copy of the authorized headings. The complete version remained restricted because it contained references to other series held under different custodianships.

“If we send it, it has to be kept dry and sealed,” she said. “And whoever receives it must sign as an institutional recipient, not as the owner of the contents.”

Lyra asked for the dimensions, the ink's sensitivity, and the period for which the certification would remain valid. The archivist had those details in a written procedure. None of the conditions meant the copy held evidentiary weight that had not yet been established for the original.

A second company offered to move it by ordinary road and river transport, with two verifiable custody checkpoints. It was slower than the first offer and required an inspection when changing vehicles.

Its invoice was also clearer.

Lyra forwarded both quotes to the audit coordination team with a warning: the requesting body had to approve the contract. She could only recommend a route she could describe and supervise.

A technical representative asked whether the delay might prevent the report from meeting its deadline.

“Then change the deadline or explain the limitation,” she answered. “Don't turn the urgency of reading a piece of paper into permission for a crossing no one has demonstrated is safe.”

When they finished, Ema received news from the valley community. The filters held back after the previous incident were still waiting for the manufacturer's assessment. Someone had asked to use them anyway.

Lyra called the depot. She confirmed that the seals remained intact and the decision to hold the filters still stood. The historical shipment would not displace work that continued to have consequences for living people.

***

The boat departed with a manifest longer than its cargo.

The certified envelope traveled inside a rigid box bearing the archive's seal and a simple humidity indicator. It carried no accumulators, Anchor components, or destination references. The driver of the overland leg signed for receipt and recorded a bent corner on the outer cover, without claiming the contents had been damaged.

Lyra supervised by communications from the dock. Her physical condition did not allow her to endure long days of travel, and she had no need to pretend otherwise. An independent inspector verified the second custody checkpoint.

In midmorning, an alert arrived: rain had raised the river level. The navigation authority temporarily suspended the passage of documentary cargo on small boats.

The captain proposed waiting for the water to recede. The audit representative asked them to use another boat that could still cross a smaller channel.

“Is it licensed?” Lyra asked.

“The owner says he knows the passage.”

“I didn't ask what the owner remembers.”

The boat had no current inspection permitting it to transport that box. Lyra recorded the refusal and notified the sending custodian. The delivery deadline was revised by formal agreement; the envelope stayed sealed in an authorized depot.

Ema listened to the conversation from the next table.

“They're paying you to solve problems, not to find reasons not to leave.”

“Solving one doesn't mean creating another.”

The delay had a cost. The depot charged for additional custody, and the audit demanded an explanation for every day. Lyra sent the weather notices, suspension order, and signatures. She didn't need a tale of heroism to demonstrate why they had waited.

***

Two days later, the copy arrived without anyone crossing a Threshold.

The receiving officer compared the seal, humidity, weight, and number of pages against the manifest. The indicator showed no exposure outside tolerance. The outer cover had suffered another blow, noted during the transfer, but the inner box remained intact. Receipt was accepted with that observation.

The material was not handed to Lyra. The document coordination team placed it in official custody and sent the originating archive a signed acknowledgment. The receiving office noted that its legal validity depended on the comparison to be undertaken by the competent authorities.

The audit representative thanked Lyra for arranging the route.

“We might need another copy.”

“First check what you actually need. Every transfer costs money and leaves a trail.”

She returned to the shelter with an invoice for her fees separate from Ema's accounts. Part of the income could pay for the shutter repair if the assembly accepted an unconditional donation. Ema reminded her that the claim against the carrier over the supplies was still unresolved.

Life wasn't waiting for a catalog's findings.

Before going to sleep, Lyra received a communication from the sending archive. An old receiving note indicated that a reference copy of the index had been sent, decades later, to another depot. The archive could not guarantee that the two versions were identical. Nor did it know the condition of the distant copy.

The technical representative wanted another expedition immediately.

Lyra looked out at the dark river.

“First ask that depot to confirm what it actually holds,” she said. “Don't pay for another journey just to discover the door is still closed.”

She hung up.

The new lead had traveled along ordinary roads. Its next step would have to justify its own cost, too.

***End of Episode 12.***
