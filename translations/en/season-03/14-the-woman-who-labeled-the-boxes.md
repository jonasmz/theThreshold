# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 14: The Woman Who Labeled the Boxes

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel, limited third person.

***

The woman did not want to talk about the Fracture.

“I worked in a storage depot,” she told Inara Vel. “I never saw a door to another world. I saw boxes, labels, and men who didn't know where to put them.”

Her name was Oria Cenn. She had worked at a successor secretariat during an archive reorganization, many years after the historical disaster. Her name appeared in a public staff directory Joren Mal had consulted for an article about the new audit.

Inara called before visiting her home. Oria agreed to speak but did not authorize photographs or publication of her address. She also asked not to be presented as the keeper of secrets she had never read.

The house stood beside a bookbinding workshop. The sound of a hand press came through the window and made a row of glasses tremble.

“They say there were nine versions,” Oria remarked, still standing. “People hear nine and think it means nine witnesses.”

“The audit has only references to possible sources,” Inara replied. “Do you remember a particular transfer?”

Oria opened a personal notebook. Years earlier, she had drawn the shelving arrangement of the depot where she worked. The lettering was hers, without any official seal.

“I remember when we changed the labels. One inventory counted packages. The other counted case files. We were told to give them a common reference, but we weren't allowed to open them.”

Her account fitted an ordinary explanation for the discrepancy the archives had detected. It did not demonstrate that every item had arrived or that anyone had falsified a date.

Inara asked who had given the order.

“The section chief. His surname will be on the payroll records, if they survived. I signed for moving the shelves, not for the contents of every box.”

Oria looked at her hands.

“That's all I can swear to.”

***

Oria's account included a detail absent from the official statements.

As she remembered it, during the reorganization some books were separated for conservation reasons, not because of their subject matter. Pages needing repair were sent to an outside workshop while the closed case files remained in storage. The office then declared the transfer complete without waiting for every reference item to come back.

“Does that mean documents were missing when the inventory was closed?” Inara asked.

“It means they were missing from that room. I don't know if they were missing from the archive.”

Oria remembered an argument over damp boxes, a trolley stuck on a ramp, and a signature given at the end of a shift. She remembered no page numbers. After decades, two different working days might have blended together in her memory.

Inara avoided suggesting dates. She first asked Oria to describe the route, then the documents she had signed. The personal notebook contained a task list but no mark that could date the movement of the books precisely.

“I might have written it that week,” Oria said. “Or when I retired. I kept it to show my nephew how a depot was organized.”

The nephew appeared with a cup of tea. He offered to show an old letter thanking Oria for her work. The letter bore a date and letterhead, but referred to a shelving renovation. It made no mention of the safety series.

Inara photographed only the letter, with its owner's permission, leaving out the address.

“This establishes that you worked on the reorganization,” she explained. “It doesn't establish everything you remember about that day.”

Oria smiled for the first time.

“I'd rather you put it that way. I don't want someone accusing me ten years from now of inventing an entire world because I mixed up two boxes.”

Inara left with a list of new questions, not a revelation ready for the front page.

***

The old directory of workshops was still held by a commercial archive.

Joren found a paper-restoration firm contracted during the period Oria had described. The company had closed long ago, but its liquidation had left a service index in public custody. Inara requested access to the available entries and received a reproduction of the lines relating to archival work.

There were invoices for restoration, conservation materials, and the transport of packages between offices. None identified the books' contents. One carried a shipment number resembling the one used in the successor catalog.

Joren leaned over the table.

“So Oria was right.”

“About a transfer for repairs, perhaps. We still haven't linked this invoice to the series we're studying.”

The commercial archive's clerk pointed out that the numbering was annual and repeated across contractors. Inara noted the warning. A coincidence that looked extraordinary was commonplace under the rules of the period.

They asked to see the workshop's receiving register. Only a partial index remained. It confirmed that packages from an administrative agency had returned weeks later, without individually identifying each book.

The archivist agreed to certify the consultation and describe the lack of detail. She did not allow them to take the original.

Back in the newsroom, the editor suggested a headline: “The Documents Came Back After the Closure.”

“We don't know whether those were the documents we're looking for,” Inara replied.

“But a witness says some came back late.”

“And we have a commercial record of returns, not identification of each item. We can report both and explain the distinction.”

They sent a formal inquiry to the successor secretariat. They wanted to know whether a receiving inventory of restored materials existed and who held its copies. They did not request general access to restricted files.

***

Oria received a verification call before a single line was published.

Inara read aloud the statements she intended to attribute to her. Oria corrected one point: she had said the books were moved out of the room, not taken off the premises. The distinction could completely change the route others would later try to trace.

Inara amended her notes and kept the corrected version with the recording made by consent.

The newspaper article presented a hypothesis about the physical reorganization, partially supported by commercial records. It separated Oria's words from the public entries, explained that the memories were retrospective, and requested a response from the present custodian. It attributed no forgery and named no one responsible for the Fracture.

The official reply arrived shortly after publication.

The archive confirmed that it held a restoration service ledger, but said a series of return acknowledgments still needed checking. It offered to perform that examination under the audit and provide a summary without naming users of other collections.

Oria called to thank Inara for not turning her work into a legend. Then she added something Inara hadn't asked about.

“When they gave us the new labels, some packages kept their old numbers inside. The outside number could change, but the slip attached to the papers didn't.”

“Did you see that yourself?”

“I saw it happen with ordinary boxes. I don't remember one of the ones you're looking for now.”

Inara recorded the qualification and closed her notebook.

The official history of a transfer could be incomplete without being false in every line.

And a witness's account could be valuable without becoming the whole truth.

***End of Episode 14.***
