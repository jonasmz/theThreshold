# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 34: The Price of a Copy

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, limited third person.

***

The petition had more signatures than pages.

Kael Veyran found it on his desk at the Archive, beside a lending register Tessa Ord had left for review. A collective of affected people was demanding public access to the entire historical file. It had gathered signatures from workers, families, and people living outside municipal jurisdiction. The request was legitimate. One sentence, however, demanded that the operating annexes also be published without redactions.

Rena Tor read over his shoulder.

“If the documents explain what they did, why not release them?”

“Because an explanation of a decision and instructions for repeating a procedure can occupy the same physical record,” Kael answered.

The question hurt. He had spent too long demanding access to files administered by others without giving him a voice. He did not want to become the gatekeeper who responded to every request with an automatic refusal.

Tessa showed them the historical custodian's communication. It offered a public version of the ruling, with its sources and votes redacted where appropriate. The strategic operating fields would remain subject to specialist review, with a right to challenge restrictions. No Archive employee, Kael included, was authorized to copy the complete annexes.

Rena put down the bundle of signatures.

“Someone will say we've chosen to protect the same people as before.”

“Then we have to explain what we're protecting, who decides, and how that decision can be reviewed.”

Kael drew three columns on a sheet. The first identified established historical facts. The second listed data needed to attribute responsibility. The third described instructions that could jeopardize current installations if released without control.

He did not know the details in the final column.

He did not need to know them to defend independent review of its boundaries.

***

Someone was waiting in the consultation room after closing time.

He was a maintenance worker who had signed the petition and knew some of the consequences of the closed corridors. He had heard that Kael worked with indexes and wanted to see the original document before somebody altered its description again.

“I'm not asking you to give me a machine,” he said. “I'm asking you not to hide the procedure behind technical words.”

Kael led him to the public desk. There they could consult the dispatch and receipt certifications, the corrected chronology, and the extract of the historical decision. Kael could not open the restricted depot on his own initiative.

The visitor listened to the explanation and pointed to a reference.

“This number appears twice. How do I know they aren't hiding another warning?”

Kael explained that both covers belonged to the same notice and had left traces in different custodianships. He showed him the identifiers on the public certifications and the procedure for requesting review of the link between the copies.

“And if they say no?”

“They have to give reasons for the refusal. You can appeal to a body other than the office that holds the series.”

The man looked at him distrustfully.

“You talk as if you trust institutions.”

Kael thought of the papers that could define his origins without his ever having consented to their release. He did not tell the visitor about them. His personal history was not evidence he had to supply in defense of a general principle.

“I trust the requirement that people show what they've done more than I trust anyone's goodwill,” he answered.

The visitor asked for an appeal form.

He left without a copy of the annex.

But he had a procedure he could use without knowing anyone inside the Archive.

***

The conflict intensified when an incomplete reproduction appeared on the building's door.

Someone had pasted up a sheet from a technical file, accompanied by the word *censorship*. Some lines were blacked out; others displayed marks resembling equipment positions. People gathered around, saying that at last someone was beginning to reveal how the ancient operation had worked.

Rena moved to tear it down.

Kael stopped her before she touched the paper.

“First let's notify the custodian and document what's been exposed. We don't know where it came from or whether the image has been altered.”

Tessa called the documentary incident channel. Accredited staff removed the material from public view and recorded its condition and location. Kael did not photograph it for circulation or attempt to reconstruct the concealed fragments. The Safeguards office would determine what could be discussed publicly and what investigation was warranted into the possible leak.

A Safety official arrived proposing that all historical consultation be suspended until the sheet's origin was established.

Kael objected.

“Exposing a restricted record doesn't make public indexes dangerous. Closing everything would prevent people from checking facts already cleared for disclosure.”

The official argued that any copy might fall into the wrong hands.

“Then define the risks document by document,” Kael replied. “Don't turn one specific failure into permission to hide everything.”

Tessa supported his position. She suggested keeping catalogs and certifications open, suspending only reproduction of the affected material while it was examined, and publishing a reviewable explanation for the measure.

The Safety officer agreed to put the proposal before the competent authority.

Kael felt tempted to consider it a victory.

It wasn't one yet. People who needed information were still waiting, and someone had circulated a document of uncertain provenance.

He had defended two limits at once.

Neither was comfortable.

***

The meeting the next morning began with a question Kael hadn't expected.

A Safeguards representative asked whether they would consider shared custody for versions cleared for release: one archive would preserve originals, another would register certifications, and an independent office would hear access appeals. The proposal did not automatically transfer restricted records into volunteers' hands.

“That way no one would have to trust Safety alone,” the representative said.

Rena looked up.

“And could we check when someone changes a description?”

“Every revision would have a history and an accountable person. Access to the contents would still depend on their nature and the custodian's authority.”

Kael requested that affected people be able to challenge indexing errors without first proving a specialized technical interest. He also asked that nobody lose rights over their own information because they had cooperated with a public investigation.

The representative recorded both conditions for debate. She did not declare them approved.

Tessa reminded them that the budget was limited. Maintaining verified copies, answering appeals, and auditing changes required staff who could not simply be improvised. They would need to choose which series came first and how the work would be funded without neglecting essential services.

After the meeting, Rena asked whether he feared the shared system might reproduce the same authority under another name.

“Of course I do,” Kael answered. “That's why I want to know how a wrong decision can be corrected.”

The room was empty when the response arrived about the sheet that had been displayed. Its authenticity and provenance remained unverified; the recovered material was being preserved under a formal incident record. Consultation of public indexes would not be suspended.

Kael filed the requests register and closed his desk.

He had not managed to open every archive.

He had prevented fear from closing those that ought to remain open.

The next argument would be harder.

They would have to decide who watched the custodians without giving anyone a key that opened every door.

***End of Episode 34.***
