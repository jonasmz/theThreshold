# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 23: The Name Beneath the Seal

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Daren Holt, limited third person.

***

The signature belonged to someone who could not have made the decision everyone attributed to him.

Daren Holt had seen the name in a public reproduction of the historical receipt record. The signature was clear. What was missing was the mandate of the office whose seal surrounded it. Since the newspaper published the corrected chronology, several petitions had demanded the identity of the “person responsible for receiving the warning.”

The hearing secretary placed a copy of the regulations from that period on the table.

“Here's the signatory's position,” he said. “Registration officer of the coordination committee.”

“Did he have a vote?” Daren asked.

“The index doesn't say.”

A Safeguards delegate was present to supervise the handling of historical names and any personal information belonging to living witnesses. A representative of the sending archive certified only that the reproduction matched the entry it held. She could not speak on behalf of another administration.

Daren remembered Bren Tal, the employee involved in a much later reorganization, whose receipt had said less than others wanted to read into it. He would not make the same mistake with a far older signature.

“Before we attribute a decision to him, we need to establish what he had the power to decide,” he said.

The room was full of stamped pages and a question that sounded simple only from outside: who had authority to accept the risk?

He requested the statute in force on the exact date, together with its amendments and the registers of replacement officeholders.

Without that context, a signature might indicate receipt, certification, transfer, or approval.

The paper was under no obligation to explain which one.

***

The first statute arrived divided into two booklets.

The general volume established the committee's powers. Another, held by a different office, described substitute officials, secretaries, and technical staff summoned to appear. An amendment adopted in a later cycle had extended signing authority for certain procedures. The chronology of each version mattered.

An assistant had prepared a table of the names printed in the margins of the documents. Daren asked her to replace them temporarily with official functions.

“We're trying to reconstruct powers, not choose suspects.”

The archivist in charge read out the old regulation. The registration officer could receive communications, certify their arrival, and distribute them to the competent bodies. He could not approve strategic maneuvers by himself or substitute for a vote of the committee.

“So his signature doesn't establish authorization,” Daren summarized.

The Safeguards representative added:

“Nor does it establish that he didn't read the document. Only that his signature in this field has no such legal effect.”

Daren had both qualifications entered into the record.

Another sheet bore the initials of a secretariat empowered to organize the day's files. A councilor wanted to classify it as a technical decision. The statute said otherwise: that office could place a matter on the agenda, but could not certify the safety of a test.

Their powers formed a chain with missing links.

The greater problem emerged when they consulted the amendment to the statute. The published version carried a date later than the technical test Kael had identified, but a marginal note stated that certain provisions had been applied provisionally before publication.

“If we accept that note, anyone can move authority backward through the calendar,” the delegate objected.

“We won't accept it without the act establishing when it took effect,” Daren replied.

The request went to another custodian.

Every seal added another question. None yet justified a conviction.

***

The third session was held with formal opportunity for challenge.

A historical advisory body argued that the secretariat for deliberations had been entitled, under delegated authority, to commission preliminary tests. The safety office disputed the interpretation: the statute distinguished workshop tests from maneuvers on active nodes, requiring different authorizations.

Daren asked both sides to cite the exact rule. The first institution produced a general delegation for studies; the second, an express reservation covering strategic intervention. The texts were not incompatible so long as their respective scopes remained separate.

“Is there an order directing anyone to act on a node?” he asked.

Neither side had produced one.

A civilian authority present reminded them that the investigation should not be paralyzed by the absence of a document that might have been destroyed. Daren agreed that facts could be reconstructed from independent evidence, provided gaps were not filled with convictions.

“There's a difference between being unable to produce an order and proving that no order ever existed,” he said.

The hearing admitted a limited finding: the functions attached to two signatures had been established. One concerned receipt, the other the scheduling of matters for consideration. Neither alone identified who had decided to carry out a risky maneuver.

Daren ordered the objections preserved in full and absent custodians given an opportunity to respond. The historical file was not the criminal case in which he was investigating possible experimentation on people. An institutional coincidence could not transfer evidence between proceedings without legal authority and safeguards.

As he left, he found a letter from Lena Vard.

The association asked the hearing not to forget the three municipal series whose preservation had set this inquiry in motion. The depot needed funds to complete its inventory while the public was still waiting for answers.

Daren approved forwarding the notice to the competent authority.

An archive investigating the past could not destroy the present for lack of shelving.

***

The pending amendment arrived with a certificate of enactment.

The provisional provision had come into force for contracts and bench tests but expressly excluded the use of strategic Anchors. The copy was complete in the statutory archive, and its date had been checked against an independent bulletin.

Daren updated the table. Now they could establish something useful: a historical office could commission certain studies without being empowered to authorize the operation that would later have required a decision involving risk. A receipt signature should not become a command signature through sheer inertia.

The secretary showed him two empty columns.

“Shall we put the names of the people who did have that authority here?”

“First we'll enter the positions defined by law. When we find a specific act and establish its authenticity, we'll check who held those positions at the time.”

He did not want to absolve anyone before knowing the facts. Nor did he want a history of negligence to end up blaming whichever official had left the most legible name.

At nightfall, a custodian sent a reference to a separate register of extraordinary authorizations. There was a public index containing a file number and date, but it did not disclose the object of the authorization. The date fell after the old test and before the Fracture.

A plausible sequence was beginning to take shape.

Daren did not call it proof.

He requested preservation of the series and verification of its jurisdiction, integrity, and relationship to the documents already certified. A future hearing would need to show not only what the paper said, but who could act on it and for what purpose.

Before leaving, he saw a line of applicants at the conservation desk. A woman was holding a family letter so worn that its edge had begun to crumble.

Daren understood the fragility of the investigation.

Great events could depend on a tiny inscription.

But a signature, even after surviving for centuries, must not acquire authority it never possessed.

***End of Episode 23.***
