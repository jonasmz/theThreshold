# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 33: The Visitor on the Other Side of the Line

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Maelor Thane, limited third person.

***

The Western Annex elevator stopped one floor below the control post.

Maelor Thane had ordered visitor access to remain separate from the active sector. Even so, hearing the mechanism stop reminded him how often he had been the one to decide just how far an inspection could go.

The external delegate was waiting with two assistants and accreditation issued by an independent oversight body. Her name was Ilya Sen. Her mandate covered records of decisions, containment expenses, and compliance with safety conditions. It did not authorize her to operate regulators, examine personal information unrelated to the purpose of her visit, or enter a strategic installation without specific technical clearance.

“I'm here to verify obligations, not direct your work,” she said.

“Those obligations also include not altering the Annex's load,” Maelor replied.

Talia Mer checked their credentials and led the group to an isolated room. Verified copies of shift rosters, maintenance decisions, and aggregated readings had been prepared there. The originals remained with their custodians; a technician could explain how the data had been extracted without handing over operating sequences.

Ilya looked first at what was missing.

“I don't see the record for the meter removed during the last inspection.”

Maelor explained that the component remained in technical custody pending a wear assessment. His office had recorded its replacement, but the transfer certificate for the meter had not yet been included in the oversight packet.

“Then I can't certify that part today,” Ilya said.

The words hurt less than an accusation and more than a courtesy.

Maelor asked Vessa to locate the certificate without manufacturing a backdated copy.

For the first time, a gap in a present-day folder could oblige him to answer to someone who did not depend on his signature.

***

Ilya's first objection concerned the workers.

The budget table listed hours of guard duty and relief shifts, but did not clearly distinguish mandatory training from actual service time. Talia produced the shift sheets; two operators had attended a nighttime inspection after an extended workday.

“Did they receive the prescribed rest?” Ilya asked.

Maelor checked the records. One had. The other had returned early because of an unexpected staff absence. The hours had been compensated, but that did not replace the rest he had lost.

Talia looked at Maelor.

“I told him to go home. He said the post couldn't be left empty.”

Her account showed no lack of loyalty. It showed a shift system that had turned individual responsibility into undue pressure.

Ilya asked whether the incident had been entered into the risk register and whether measures existed to prevent a recurrence. Maelor acknowledged that it appeared only in a personnel note, not the safety report.

“We'll correct it,” he said.

“With the correction date visible,” Ilya answered. “And with workers empowered to challenge the plan, not merely comply with it.”

Maelor authorized an expansion of the temporary relief team within the permitted budget and postponed a nonessential inspection. The cost would be made public in aggregated form. He would not offset it by reducing the energy essential for containment.

At the exit, the affected operator was waiting for a copy of his employment record. Maelor did not approach him demanding gratitude.

He delivered the certificate through the prescribed channel and left the worker to decide whether to submit observations to the inspector.

Oversight had produced a concrete measure before any historical report.

***

The second objection concerned the archive reform Maelor had authorized years earlier.

Ilya laid out the chain of custody for a reclassified series. The comparison begun in Episode Fifteen was still open: a physically preserved sheet appeared only as “restricted” in the digital edition, and some old batches lacked individual descriptions. The historical decision about the Fracture had been established through other custodians, but that did not erase Safety's contemporary failures.

“Can you personally certify the integrity of that transfer?” Ilya asked.

“No. I approved the procedure under examination.”

“Will you accept an external review of your administrative conduct as well as your technical results?”

Maelor could have offered a full defense of his reasons. He had acted because of insufficient space, damp conditions, and the need to protect originals. Those reasons mattered, but they did not relieve him of proving that the method preserved traceability.

“I accept an examination with defined jurisdiction, access justified by its purpose, and an opportunity to challenge findings,” he answered.

Ilya noted that acceptance did not imply guilt. She would request originals when necessary and refer restrictions to a Safeguards body for review. No observer would thereby become an operator of the Network.

During a break, the maintenance alarm sounded on an auxiliary pump. It was not an Anchor failure. Talia received the report and decided to keep a reserve circuit operating without increasing demand.

Ilya did not ask to enter the active sector to see what was happening. She requested the subsequent incident record and the procedure supporting the response.

Maelor caught himself waiting for an order from her.

The delegate gave none.

Her task was to verify afterward whether the competent team had made a safe, documentable decision.

That distinction made oversight possible without replacing the operators' expertise.

***

By the end of the visit, the table was covered with documents returned to their folders.

Ilya issued no absolution. She identified three outstanding matters: the documentary transfer of the removed meter, operators' rest, and the traceability of the archive reform. She also acknowledged that the current containment margins had been maintained without introducing any new demand during the inspection.

“I'll need written answers and a realistic deadline,” she said.

Maelor agreed to a schedule with responsibilities assigned separately. Safety staff would handle technical records; Safeguards would review access permissions; the labor unit would examine shifts. No team would be required to surrender restricted information without its custodian's authorization.

Vessa accompanied him to the door.

“After all this, they'll say we're being investigated because we hid something.”

“We're being audited because we exercise authority that can affect other people,” Maelor replied. “They should be able to audit us even if we'd made no mistakes.”

That night, the meter's transfer certificate turned up in the proper office. It had been registered on the day of removal and left out of the packet because of a classification omission. Maelor did not order it quietly added to the original submission. He included it with a note of later discovery, date, and custodian, so Ilya could distinguish a missing document from a procedure that had never existed.

The shift worker's report arrived as well. It requested one specific change: no emergency replacement should require extending a workday without independent authorization.

Maelor signed for receipt, not automatic acceptance. He forwarded the proposal to those empowered to assess its safety and labor implications.

Looking at the Annex from the gallery, he remembered the historical decision Daren had established. They had known that an assessment was missing, and they had chosen to proceed.

Maelor could choose differently.

Not to obey the data less.

To listen also to those entitled to examine it.

The next visit would no longer depend on his invitation.

***End of Episode 33.***
