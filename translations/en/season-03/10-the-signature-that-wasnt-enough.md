# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 10: The Signature That Wasn't Enough

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Maelor Thane, limited third person.

***

Maelor Thane had not left the observers' field blank.

He had asked that no one write down a name until they had decided who was entitled to choose them.

The draft audit proposal lay on a table that was far too long. At one end sat delegates from Safety and Licensing. At the other were representatives of the civilian administration and a safeguards office. A Licensing councilor pushed the proposal toward Maelor.

“If you sign, we can begin tomorrow.”

“My signature only reaches installations under my authority,” he replied. “It doesn't open municipal archives or grant access to personal data.”

“People want a unified answer.”

“An answer can be coordinated without pretending there's a unified authority.”

The safeguards delegate pointed to a paragraph authorizing examination of “any record related to the closure.” That phrase could cover private notebooks, medical histories, and irrelevant correspondence. She asked to replace it with identified series, a defined purpose, authorization from each custodian, and a record of access.

A civilian representative then demanded that the technical side be just as open to scrutiny.

“We can't accept an audit where you choose which data to show us and then tell us to trust you.”

Maelor looked at the Western Annex report. It contained no recipe for opening the facility, but it did contain enough information to demonstrate a measured recovery problem. There was a way to allow independent scrutiny without publishing the regulators' configuration.

“I propose two levels,” he said. “A public report on decisions, costs, methods, and limitations. And a restricted technical review by accredited observers who do not answer to the office carrying out the operation.”

The Licensing councilor set down his pen.

“Who accredits them?”

Maelor gave the one answer that would not turn the meeting into a ceremony celebrating his own power.

“The authorities that share the risk, through a procedure that can be challenged.”

***

The first candidate for an observer declined the appointment.

She was an expert from a laboratory that had participated in the isolated measurements the previous season. The committee considered her suitable to review calibration methods, but an ongoing contract with a supplier created a conflict that had to be declared.

“She could abstain from evaluating that component,” an official suggested.

“Then we'd have an observer who couldn't examine part of the core material,” Maelor said.

The expert asked to serve as a background consultant instead, with no vote on the findings.

The second candidate was a municipal maintenance technician experienced in shift safety. He had no authorization to examine strategic configurations, and he should not receive it automatically merely because he understood a civilian system. He could assess operating costs and occupational risks, not Anchor parameters.

Talia Mer, called in as the safety lead, presented a task-specific authorization framework: who would witness measurements, who would review redacted documents, who could question custodians, and who must remain outside active sectors. Credentials would be temporary, recorded, and revocable for stated reasons, with the option of challenging a revocation.

“We can't put a visitor beside a regulator to demonstrate transparency,” she said.

“Nor can we ask that visitor to certify data they've never seen,” Maelor replied.

They agreed that independent expert analysis would use verified copies and instrumental references outside the installation, while a limited number of qualified technicians would observe the data-extraction procedure without altering loads. Each task would require its own authorization.

The budget list made no provision for relief operators who would maintain containment during the review.

Maelor added them. An audit depending on exhausted workers would not be safe, however immaculate its forms.

***

The discussion broke down when they reached funding.

The municipality was paying for food shipments, electrical reinforcements, and staff hours. Licensing offered to fund the equipment review, but not the transport of spokespeople from communities outside the municipality. Safety could maintain the Annex's meters and guard shifts, but not pay for a traveling civilian hearing.

The safeguards delegate asked for funds to be reserved for advising people who needed to testify without revealing protected information.

“If you remove that allocation, the only people able to participate will be those who already have resources,” she said.

Maelor examined the table. His own office could give up a nonessential purchase planned for the quarter, but that would not cover every expense. Some reserves were also earmarked by regulation for maintenance that could not be sacrificed.

“I'm not going to fund observers by shutting down containment,” he said. “I can defer furniture replacement and provide recordkeeping staff under an authorized budget.”

Each administration would have to publish the source of its funding and any outstanding obligations. A budget review was scheduled for the audit's midpoint: if security expenses increased or affected communities lacked representation, the program would have to narrow its scope or seek supplementary authorization, not invent money.

One councilor refused to sign the full version. He accepted reviewing current safety, but believed examining correspondence from before the Fracture might reopen disputes unrelated to the present closures.

Maelor did not attribute bad faith to the objection.

“The historical request has its own custodians and conditional access,” he said. “We won't presume guilt. But neither will we forbid questions about the origins of restrictions we continue to invoke.”

The table agreed to a separate path: first inventory the historical series that existed, identify their custodians, and establish what could be certified without exposing operational contents. Only then could specific consultation requests be submitted, with safeguards and an opportunity for objections.

The councilor had his vote against that path entered in the minutes. The disagreement did not disappear from the record.

***

By the end of the day, the signatures covered three pages.

Safety approved a review of containment criteria within its jurisdiction. Licensing ratified the information rules for workshops and permitted methods, without reinstating the expanded intervention circular. The civilian administration agreed to document losses and facilitate testimony. Safeguards validated the data-protection mechanism and the right to challenge access. Historical archives would receive specific requests and retain the authority to decide over their own collections.

There was no order compelling every Domain to open its documents, and no authorization to use a Threshold. Nothing in the agreement lifted the restrictions on the Western Annex.

Observers would be nominated by different bodies and selected according to their declared qualifications, with challenges to their participation allowed. The first public report would also have to state access limitations and expenses. The audit would have a review date and a condition requiring suspension if it endangered containment or personal rights.

Vessa laid the final copy in front of Maelor.

“They'll accuse us of hiding things even if we do all this.”

“Perhaps,” he replied. “The procedure doesn't require them to trust me. It requires them to be able to check whether we've done what we signed up to do.”

Talia called from the Annex. The latest measurement showed recovery consistent with the morning's reduced band. It did not authorize new demands. The relief team had arrived, and the exhausted operator had finally left his post.

Maelor had both facts recorded. The agreement had just improved one concrete working condition; the Network remained as fragile as ever.

He thought the day was over when an archivist from the successor secretariat requested a documentary observer to accompany the review of a historical inventory. She was not asking to open it without permission. She had discovered that two custodians described the same batch using different criteria: one counted envelopes, the other assembled case files.

The explanation might be entirely ordinary. Checking it would require delivery inventories, classification dates, and witnesses to changes in custody.

“How many series have you identified?” Maelor asked.

“Nine references of possible provenance. Not nine confirmed documents, or nine independent testimonies.”

Maelor looked at the still-empty fields on the review form.

For years, he had believed that keeping a secret meant knowing where the door ended.

Now his first obligation was to allow others to establish what was really behind it, without turning the examination into another disaster.

He signed the receipt of the request.

The audit was authorized.

The truth was not. Not yet.

***End of Episode 10.***
