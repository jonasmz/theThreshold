# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 15: The Page We Were Keeping

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Maelor Thane, limited third person.

***

Maelor Thane had signed the regulation that now made it impossible to find a page.

He remembered it because, years earlier, Safety had reorganized its storage facilities. The measure transferred frequently consulted inventories to a restricted reference office and left older originals in historical custody. It was meant to prevent operators of strategic installations from handling fragile documents whenever they needed to look up a reference.

The new audit report showed an uncomfortable result: the historical office maintained that it had forwarded an auxiliary index, while Safety's receiving list made no mention of it.

Vessa spread the two documents before him.

“The package is recorded as delivered to our office, but it doesn't appear in the inventory we kept.”

“Is the delivery signature valid?”

“The custodian considers it authentic. They haven't yet certified what was inside the package.”

Maelor checked the date. The reform had been carried out during his administration. He could not blame all its gaps on centuries of vanished institutions.

He remembered meetings about lack of space, cataloging shifts, and pressure to clear boxes from a room that needed protection from damp. He also remembered approving the postponement of detailed inventories for certain series until the transfer was complete.

He did not know whether this had been one of them.

“We aren't writing 'lost document,'” he said. “We'll write 'receipt not located in the series consulted.'”

Vessa nodded.

“Do we tell the audit team?”

The question irritated him for a reason he did not want to hide from himself.

If the omission turned out to be a failure of his own administration, Safety's custody would no longer appear more reliable than anyone else's.

“Yes,” he answered. “And I won't be the only one validating our office's explanation.”

***

The Safety depot smelled of metal and damp cloth.

Maelor entered with the head archivist and a documentary observer accredited to examine inventories, not containment parameters. Her credentials allowed her to inspect shelves, transfer records, and cataloging rules. The Western Annex remained outside her authorization.

A receiving register from the year of the reform was brought to a table. Its pages were sewn together, with shift stamps and a column for the number of containers received. On several lines, the description was simply “mixed batch.”

“That's the problem,” the observer said. “A signature for a batch doesn't establish what's inside each item.”

The archivist showed them an order to preserve the original packaging so discrepancies could be examined later. The order existed, but some of the packaging material had been destroyed after the retention period expired, according to a routine certificate.

Maelor felt an impulse to ask who had authorized its destruction. Before doing so, he checked the date. The destruction preceded the audit request and complied with the rules then in force. That was no evidence of a cover-up. It also meant his administration could no longer fully reconstruct part of the chain.

“Is there an inventory of what was discarded?” he asked.

“By volume and type of material,” the archivist answered. “Not by each label inside.”

The observer requested copies of the relevant columns, accompanied by a comparison record. She did not request access to other custodians' collections or add any new marks to the originals.

On a side shelf they found a box cataloged twice: once under the old office's reference and again under that of the reorganized office. It contained a set of empty folders and dividers. It was not the auxiliary index they sought.

“That might explain some of the discrepancy in the package counts,” Vessa said.

“Or it might only show how dividers were counted,” Maelor answered. “Let's record what we found and no more.”

The responsibility for approving that reform weighed on him in a way less dramatic than a confession.

It was also harder to leave behind.

***

At the audit session, Licensing requested that the historical review be suspended until the discrepancy was resolved.

“We can't use indexes whose own custody has gaps,” its delegate argued.

Maelor acknowledged the problem without accepting the automatic consequence. A missing receipt in Safety did not, by itself, invalidate documents preserved and authenticated by another office. It did require limiting any conclusion that depended on the missing item.

The observer presented three categories: preserved originals, references with documented transfers, and references with unexplained gaps in their custody. The disputed series belonged in the third.

“Who will investigate the gap?” a civilian representative asked.

Maelor had prepared an administrative answer. He placed it on the table without reading it.

“Our office carried out the reform. I propose that the comparison be led by someone appointed jointly by the custodians involved and the independent representation, not by me alone.”

The Licensing delegate looked surprised.

“That doesn't absolve you of having approved the system.”

“I haven't asked to be absolved.”

The safeguards representatives demanded that the examination not expose private correspondence accidentally included in the mixed batches. They agreed to inventory the physical records first and separate any protected information before requesting substantive access.

Maelor handed over a copy of the regulation he had signed, the reform budget, and the modification records that still survived. His name appeared on the general authorization, not as the recipient of every package.

The committee did not remove him from his containment duties. It did record that he should not independently certify the integrity of the transfer he had ordered.

It was a small reduction in his authority.

It seemed necessary to him.

***

When he returned to the Annex, Talia Mer was waiting with the recovery report.

Containment remained within its reduced operating margin. Relief shifts had returned to normal, although wear on the meters required maintenance. No documentary audit could replace that work.

“I heard they found an omission in our archives,” Talia said.

“We found an interval without individually documented receipt.”

“And what are you going to do?”

“Preserve what exists, document what's missing, and let someone else review our explanation.”

Talia did not look reassured.

“If we need an old manual to repair a component, how will we know we have the correct version?”

Maelor looked at the meters, the guards, and the accumulators sustaining containment. Her question connected two risks he had tried to keep separate: historical truth and present-day operations. It did not mean a missing document had caused a technical failure. It meant no maintenance decision should depend on a reference whose edition had not been verified.

“Until we've checked, we won't use that reference to authorize a maneuver,” he said. “Containment will continue under the current, verified procedure.”

The order left one team unable to proceed with a scheduled review. Maelor arranged for their hours to be paid and the material held in reserve, without dismantling active systems out of curiosity.

Vessa arrived soon afterward with a response from the successor secretariat. The archivist had found the name of the person who had certified the batch during the original transfer. That did not prove the index had been inside. The former certifying officer no longer worked there, but an institutional address for formal notifications was available.

“Shall we request a statement?” she asked.

“Through the proper procedure, with a chance for that person to consult their own records. We won't treat someone as a suspect for signing a receipt.”

Vessa set down a second sheet: the Safety catalog had a page numbered between two surviving leaves, but the digital edition showed only the notation “restricted.” No one had yet checked whether the physical page was still bound into the book.

Maelor remembered approving that word as a temporary measure.

He had considered it sufficient.

Now he understood that a restriction without a verifiable inventory could, over time, become an unintended form of disappearance.

He ordered the book preserved and requested a physical examination by its competent custodian.

He did not know what the page contained.

For the first time, he could not even be certain that his own office knew where it was.

***End of Episode 15.***
