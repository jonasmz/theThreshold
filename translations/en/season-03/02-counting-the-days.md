# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 02: Counting the Days

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel, limited third person.

***

The most frequently repeated figure in Vael-Tir had no source.

“Hundreds of families without supplies,” declared sheets pasted at the market entrance. A merchants' association spoke of irreparable losses. The municipal office insisted it had prevented a collapse. All three statements could refer to the same day and still describe different things.

Inara Vel arranged her notes on her desk.

Joren Mal had brought a summary from the station where Sera Nhal worked. It included aggregated consumption, hours under restrictions, and the cost of transporting sensitive goods. The businesses were not named. There was a case number and an identifiable custodian.

“This is the story,” Joren said. “They closed the routes, and now a pump nearly stopped.”

“A pump operated with less margin,” Inara corrected him. “'Nearly stopped' isn't in the report.”

“It sounds better.”

“And proving it would take longer than printing it.”

She called the technical office. Sera didn't grant an immediate interview. She told Inara where to consult the municipal resolution and warned that the original records were still subject to review. Inara appreciated the precision. Then she called the cooperative that had requested emergency refrigeration support.

Mara Lis answered from the dock.

“We lost a loading shift and paid for a repair we hadn't budgeted for. The medicines were preserved. I don't want you writing that we lost the entire depot.”

Inara wrote down both points. She didn't ask for photographs of what was inside the crates or for patients' names. Mara gave permission to identify the cooperative, but not its private contracts.

When she hung up, Joren pointed to a column in the municipal register.

“There are twelve delayed deliveries here.”

“Twelve consignments,” Inara said. “We don't know how many recipients there were, or whether all of them arrived late. Let's find the other ledger.”

***

The second ledger was at the transport office.

Inara obtained a copy of the public receiving schedules. The list included vehicles that had arrived by road during the week and the docks where they had unloaded. Some consignments appeared twice: once when they reached an intermediate depot and again when they left for their final destination.

Joren had counted both movements as separate deliveries.

“So there aren't twelve,” he said.

“There are twelve entries in that column. We have to match waybill numbers before we talk about distinct loads.”

They spent the morning comparing documents. In five cases, the movement was a transfer within the city, not a new supply delivery. In three others, arrival had been recorded after midnight, so the departure date didn't match the receiving date.

The office agreed to explain its recording method but would not certify the number of households affected by the corridor closures. Such an estimate would require final-distribution data that no authority was yet collecting consistently.

An official offered her a cleaner table.

“It was prepared for tomorrow's political meeting. Everything is summarized there.”

Inara noticed the table carried neither a method nor the signature of anyone responsible.

“Can I see how you put it together?”

“I don't know where the original spreadsheet is.”

“Then I'll quote it as an estimate from your office, if you can specify its scope. Not as a census.”

The man seemed offended, but he gave her a contact in the statistics department. The analyst confirmed that they had extrapolated from three districts to the whole city using average consumption figures from before the closures. They had not accounted for changes in routes during the last week.

Inara kept the table. She also kept the explanation that prevented her from treating it as the final truth.

***

The workers' association refused to speak at the newsroom.

They met in a dining hall near the docks, with the doors open and the noise of carts passing in the street. A delegate explained that several maintenance shifts had been extended to cope with demand. Another worker noted that failure reports recorded machine hours but not canceled rest periods.

“Can I publish your names?” Inara asked.

The delegate shook her head.

“Our company is still negotiating who pays for the extra shifts. We want the problem on record, not to be taken off the roster.”

Inara asked for receipts and minutes. They showed her two overtime records and a notice rescheduling a rest period. The documents could establish those cases, not support the same conclusion for every workshop.

One of the workers opened a folder containing a photograph of a control panel.

“If you publish it, you're going to have to explain why the Council won't reopen things once and for all.”

Inara recognized a delicate detail: beside the panel was an installation label with an operational reference.

“I don't need that picture to report the harm,” she answered. “And we have no proof that publishing it would make reopening any safer.”

The worker put the photograph away, irritated. His anger did not erase the value of the records he had brought.

As they left, Joren asked whether they weren't being too cautious.

“Leaving out one name might protect a person,” Inara said. “Inventing one figure could harm thousands of people who need to claim compensation for a real loss.”

***

The newspaper came out with three figures and no promises.

The first covered restriction periods documented by municipal resolutions. The second recorded extraordinary transport expenses supported by actual invoices. The third described labor cases verified through records shared with consent. Each figure carried its scope, dates, and limitations.

Inara requested responses from the transport office, the energy administration, and the authority responsible for strategic containment. The responses appeared together: the municipal office defended its service priorities; the security authority reiterated that there was no authorization to reopen the corridors; the merchants' association demanded compensation. None could confirm the total number of people affected.

Joren left a copy beside the window.

“There's no big number for the front page.”

“There's an account we can correct without erasing what we already know.”

In the middle of the afternoon, Mara called. The cooperative had secured a date to discuss emergency expenses and wanted a copy of the methodology. Inara provided it. The article was helping people negotiate something verifiable.

Then a letter arrived from the Civic Council. It invited citizens and technical specialists to submit reports on the consequences of the closures, but also urged them to preserve original records. Several departments were approaching their scheduled disposal of old shift ledgers and correspondence under ordinary retention calendars.

Inara read the planned-destruction notice twice. It did not prove a cover-up. It could be legitimate routine. It could also eliminate information people needed before those affected had an opportunity to request it.

She called the secretariat and confirmed there was no general suspension of disposal. Requests to preserve records had to be submitted to each custodian or the competent authority, with a justified scope.

As the edition closed, she wrote a brief update explaining where to file claims and which records people should identify before their retention periods expired. She included neither workers' names nor installation numbers.

A response came from Inspector Daren Holt's office. It wasn't an order.

It was an acknowledgment that a civic association had filed a petition for document preservation.

Inara laid the paper on her desk.

Some figures could not yet be finalized.

And someone had just asked that the ledgers needed to count them not be allowed to disappear.

***End of Episode 02.***
