# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 01: The Station That Stayed Awake

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Sera Nhal, limited third person.

***

The station had stopped sleeping before its operators did.

Sera Nhal found the pump room lit up in the middle of a shift change. A needle hovered near the yellow limit on the panel. It wasn't a catastrophic alarm, but neither was it a reading they could ignore. Beyond the wall, water in the distribution system struck a valve at irregular intervals.

Nel Or handed her the overnight log.

“Three surges in demand and not one complete lull.”

“Which consumers came online?”

“The market's cold-storage facilities and two canal districts. Road transport has brought in goods that never used to pass through here.”

Sera checked the log against the mechanical recorder. The electrical gauge's curve matched the pressure markings, except for a brief stretch when the discrepancy exceeded tolerance. That meant they had to check the sensor, not dismiss the increase in load as false.

For weeks, the municipal distribution network had been absorbing some of the work previously shared among restricted corridors and auxiliary services. The containment measures at strategic installations remained in force. No authority had promised to reopen them. Ordinary vehicles, docks, and neighborhoods were paying the price of that caution.

The shift supervisor showed her an authorization for overtime.

“We can hold out another five days if we don't stop.”

Sera read the bearing's temperature report.

“We're not going to call something endurance when we haven't even measured it yet. Reduce nonessential demand and prepare the backup circuit for a controlled interruption.”

“Do we cut power to the cold-storage depots?”

“First, let's find out what's inside them and how long they can preserve it without our supply.”

The man opened his mouth to object, but Sera had already picked up the telephone. The station had limited reserves. So did the people who depended on it.

***

The cooperative's representative arrived with wet sleeves.

Her name was Mara Lis, and she carried three delivery receipts. Her depot held food intended for a community kitchen and medicines that required constant refrigeration. The partial outage announced by the station could ruin the reserve before a vehicle became available to move it.

“The office said civilian power was independent of the Council's closures,” she said.

“The installation is under municipal control,” Sera replied. “The load placed on it isn't independent of how the city functions.”

Mara pointed to the receipts.

“Then don't make me pay twice for a decision I never made.”

Sera didn't argue with the accusation. She asked for the depot's temperature readings and the equipment's backup endurance. The manager showed her a figure provided by the manufacturer and admitted the accumulator had lost capacity through use.

“When was the last actual test carried out?” Sera asked.

No one could give her a date.

That answer changed their priorities. They couldn't plan a shutdown around a nominal endurance figure without verifying it. Sera called in an independent electrician to check the reserve and requested an on-site measurement, without disconnecting the cold rooms while they held temperature-sensitive goods.

Nel suggested increasing the station's output during hours of lower consumption.

“It would buy us a night.”

“It would sell off the next one,” Sera said. “That bearing hasn't cooled down between peaks.”

Mara looked at the team's grease-stained hands.

“So what can you offer me today?”

Sera asked the transport office for a list of alternative cold-storage facilities and available transfer slots. The cooperative could move the medicines first in a temperature-controlled vehicle. The food would stay in the depot while the auxiliary ventilation circuit was repaired. It was more expensive than turning up the power, and less convenient than announcing that everything was back to normal.

“I need the office to guarantee that in writing,” Mara said.

“So do I.”

The woman thanked no one. She signed the priority request and returned to her depot. Sera watched her leave across the yard. Mara had obtained a procedure, not immediate relief.

***

By midafternoon, a councilor had asked for the restrictions to be lifted during the supply week.

The meeting took place at the station itself because Sera refused to leave the instruments during the most demanding shift. Neris Daal arrived with an expense statement and two workers' representatives. No one present had the authority to activate an Anchor or revoke the Network's containment measures.

“People need a date,” the councilor said.

“Then give them the date of the next review,” Neris replied. “Not a promise to reopen something that isn't ours to decide.”

Sera laid out three documents: the load register, the mechanical temperature report, and the quote for a replacement rotor. The part was available, but installing it required a maintenance shutdown and personnel already covering additional hours.

“Could it fail tomorrow?” one representative asked.

“I don't have enough data to name that day. I do have enough to say that continuing to load it the same way increases wear. I propose a demand ceiling below today's level, verified rest periods, and a daily review.”

The councilor studied the figures.

“That means reducing lighting on two commercial streets.”

“At announced intervals, with safety lighting maintained. Cold storage and water will receive temporary priority, subject to public review.”

Neris asked that the minutes distinguish municipal responsibility from the strategic decision concerning the routes. She didn't want the Council excused from explaining the harm that continued to accumulate. Nor would she attribute a bearing failure to it without evidence.

The workers requested mandatory shift rotation. Sera included it. She had come close to measuring only the machine's fatigue.

When the provisional reduction came to a vote, the councilor abstained. The resolution passed with a forty-eight-hour limit, funding for refrigerated transport, and a requirement to publish actual consumption on the first day. No one recovered their trust through a vote.

***

At nightfall, the station sounded different.

The bearing was still hot, but the needle had dropped. The electrician certified that the depot's reserve lasted less time than the manual claimed. Municipal medical transport collected the medicines with a temperature record. The food remained refrigerated following a minor repair paid for by the cooperative with an emergency advance.

Sera reviewed the final figures. The reduction had worked for four hours. It didn't prove it would be enough the next day, when another convoy arrived by road.

Nel found a notice among the municipal circulars calling for documentation of the impact of the closed corridors.

“They're asking for testimony,” he said. “Should we send our report?”

Sera looked at the authorization field. The report contained information about installations and private businesses; not all of it could be disclosed. She prepared a public version with aggregated consumption, verified decisions, transfer costs, and the limits of the measurements. The originals remained in the station's custody, available for inspection by the competent authority.

“This isn't a request to reopen the route,” Nel warned.

“No. It's a request to stop making the cost of keeping it closed invisible.”

Before she sealed the envelope, a call came in from another civilian station. They had received the same load-priority instructions, but their instruments showed a different response: the backup pump had failed to start.

The operator wanted to know whether they could copy Sera's procedure.

She looked back at the panel.

“Not until we know why yours failed. Send the report and keep the load reduced according to your own safety protocol.”

She hung up and left the envelope open.

Her station had gained a few hours.

The city had just demonstrated that not every station could buy them in the same way.

***End of Episode 01.***
