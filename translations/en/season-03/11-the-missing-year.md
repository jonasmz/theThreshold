# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 11: The Missing Year

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, limited third person.

***

The impossible date wasn't written in the old document.

It was in the index that purported to describe it.

Kael Veyran placed two public catalogs beneath the Archive's reading lamp. The first was a dispatch register dated before the Fracture. The second, published many decades later, summarized the collections held by a successor secretariat. Both mentioned a series of safety warnings. But in the modern edition, receipt was dated a year after the administrative closure of the committee to which the warnings had supposedly been addressed.

Rena Tor turned a page.

“Perhaps the committee kept operating under another name.”

“Possibly. Or it could be a reclassification date,” Kael said. “We don't know what each column represents.”

Beside him, Tessa Ord reviewed the powers of the newly authorized audit team. Kael could work with metadata and public editions; he was not accredited to read the restricted annexes. That separation also protected his decision not to turn private information about his origins into a credential.

The question was more modest and more troubling: why did a printed date describe something as received when another edition showed it had been sent years earlier?

Kael consulted the legend of the modern catalog. Two symbols looked very similar: one indicated receipt and the other transfer to storage. The print was worn. Without the original typographic version, no conclusion would be safe.

“What if it's a printing error?” Rena asked.

“Then we need the correction, not a culprit.”

Tessa handed him permission to consult the original public catalog, kept in another room and subject to handling restrictions because of its fragility. They could examine it without opening any historical case files.

As he stood, Kael saw a line of workers waiting for transport outside the window. The world demanded swift decisions. Documents continued to impose their own pace.

***

The original catalog smelled of damp paper and dry wood.

The conservation officer placed a protective sheet beneath its pages and forbade them from opening the leaves beyond their support. Rena read the symbols while Kael took notes without touching anything.

The ambiguous mark clearly meant transfer, not receipt. The modern catalog had mistranslated the column in an abridged edition. For a moment, the entire anomaly seemed to dissolve.

“It was a mistake,” Rena murmured.

“It was one of the mistakes,” Kael corrected her.

The transfer date was still later than the committee's official closure. That might be perfectly normal: an office that had ceased to exist could send its papers elsewhere years later. But the old index carried an abbreviation Kael had already seen in the previous season's messenger registers. It referred to the date an entry was registered in its original custody, not the subsequent transfer.

He asked to see the administrative rules from that period. The conservation officer brought a public cataloging instruction. It distinguished three moments: creation, receipt, and referral to another office. It did not claim the entries were complete or that the rules had always been followed rigorously.

The old catalog card recorded receipt before the closure. The later edition retained the same series number but listed as its origin an office that, according to the public directory, did not yet exist in that year.

Rena frowned.

“Two different mistakes?”

“Or two different operations later grouped under a new name. We need to know which office created each entry.”

The conservation officer pointed out that the card bore a small control number. Her provenance ledger might establish when it was added to the catalog without revealing the series' contents.

Kael requested that ledger through the ordinary procedure. An answer would not arrive that day.

They left with a certified reproduction of the headings, not a secret document or the alleged historical warning.

***

At the audit's classification meeting, a civilian delegate wanted a headline.

“If the official date is false, we have proof of a cover-up,” he declared.

Kael had brought a sheet with four columns. He explained what they had read directly, what appeared in a later edition, what the classification rules said, and what still needed comparison. The faulty translation of the legend was already established. The creation date of the later office remained an apparent contradiction.

The delegate struck the table.

“People have been paying more for supplies for months. How many discrepancies do you need before you admit someone lied?”

Kael did not ignore the cost. He had seen the municipal station's notices and the invoices for overland routes. But those expenses could not identify whoever might have altered an ancient index.

“We need the provenance ledger and a response from the successor office,” he said. “An editorial error may conceal another error, not necessarily a crime.”

A safeguards observer asked whether the control number identified restricted content.

“It's published only in a finding aid,” the catalog's custodian answered. “By itself, it gives no access to an annex.”

The committee agreed to a limited request: certify the administrative dates and equivalence rules without opening strategic papers yet. Kael handed his work notes to Tessa for custody and declined to sign as a historical expert. That wasn't his role.

Rena came out frustrated.

“They want us to prove even the ink of a symbol.”

“They want us not to confuse a door with what's behind it.”

She looked at the copy.

“Sometimes it feels like no one is going to let us get behind it.”

Kael took a moment to answer.

“Then we document who denies the examination and why. But they haven't denied it yet.”

***

The response from the provenance ledger came at the end of the following day.

The conservation officer had located an old entry bearing the correct control number. It confirmed that the card existed before the modern abridged edition. Its description named the originating secretariat but not the warning's title. An attached note added that, during an administrative reform, the files of a closed office had been regrouped under the name of its successor.

That explained part of the contradiction. Not all of it.

In the same ledger, the card was recorded as having been added on a date earlier than the official directory gave for the reorganization. The entry might have been backdated, the reform might have begun before publication, or the series might have been transferred in stages.

Kael wrote down those three hypotheses without ranking them by suspicion.

“Does that give us grounds to request the reorganization order?” Tessa asked.

“Yes, if an accessible version exists, or if its custodian will certify the relevant administrative facts.”

They prepared the request with the public identifier, both dates, and the specific reason for comparison. It contained no Transit destination reference.

Before the Archive closed, Rena found a mention in an old guide of the transfer of an auxiliary index, separate from the main case file. The guide did not say it had been sent to the same place. It identified only a receiving office and a chain of custody that did not pass through the coordination committee.

Kael studied the office's name.

For the first time, the anomaly could be checked without asking a single custodian to explain its own history.

A second documentary route had appeared.

That did not yet make it a second truth.

***End of Episode 11.***
