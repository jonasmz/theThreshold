# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 04: Three Kinds of Absence

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, limited third person.

***

The new index did not contradict the old one.

Kael Veyran had spent an entire morning comparing the two versions before writing that conclusion on his work sheet. In one, the supervisory secretariat recorded sending safety observations to a coordination committee. In the other, the committee's archive acknowledged an inventory of annexes but showed neither their contents nor a resolution.

Both statements could be true.

Tessa Ord placed a recent reply to the request filed weeks earlier on his desk. The custodian of the successor secretariat confirmed that the acknowledgment of a historical transfer had been placed on hold while provenance and access restrictions were reviewed. It did not authorize removal of the originals. Nor did it confirm that the package contained precisely the warning Kael was looking for.

“Should we add it to the public list?” Tessa asked.

“As a record of preservation. Not as proof of what it contains.”

Beside him was a folder of recent statements about the harm caused by the closures. The Civic Council wanted the services affected to be linked to decisions about containment. Kael understood why so many people wanted the two inquiries combined: one concerned old warnings, the other present-day losses. But their moral resemblance was not enough to turn them into a single case.

He drew three columns on a sheet of paper.

The first was headed “observed.” There he noted the authenticated dispatch and receipt records, cross-references in the messenger ledgers, and the existence of a preserved acknowledgment. In the second he wrote “stated by custodian”: the restricted series might contain related annexes that had not yet been examined. The third, “hypotheses,” was reserved for questions no one had answered with evidence: who read the warning, who assessed the risk, and who signed an order.

Tessa read the third column.

“That's the one everyone will want to print.”

“That's the one we still can't present as an answer.”

***

A young assistant was waiting in the public room with an expense notebook.

Rena Tor had compared two editions of the coordination catalog. The older edition listed an annex series using letters; the later one grouped it by numbers. She had found a printed concordance table available for public consultation.

“I thought the committee had changed the number to hide the file,” she said.

Kael examined the publication dates. The table covered every series from the period, including agricultural supply records that had nothing to do with strategic security.

“It may be a general reclassification,” he said. “If we say that, we'll have to check other cases.”

Rena pointed to a handwritten note beside one reference.

“But this line does look as though it was corrected later.”

“The ink looks different. That doesn't tell us when it was written or who wrote it.”

They requested a certified reproduction of the public catalog and checked the editorial instructions. The handwritten entry had not changed the correspondence between series. It pointed to a later errata notice available in another folder.

Rena let out a breath.

“So I spent two hours discovering that nobody was hiding anything.”

“You discovered that an ordinary explanation could be verified. That keeps us from using a false suspicion against a real custodian.”

Even so, the exercise had revealed a new obstacle: the secretariat's catalogs and the committee's did not use the same terms for received packages. Some references could be matched by date and department, but not by an individual item identifier. The preserved acknowledgment might help clarify a transfer; it could not guarantee that every page had arrived intact.

Kael wrote a question on the review sheet: had a contents inventory been kept at the time of delivery?

He did not ask to open it. First, he needed to know who had the authority to answer.

***

By midafternoon, an official from the Civic Council arrived with a proposal for a joint report.

He wanted the Archive to present the historical chain alongside the figures for losses that municipal stations were collecting. The cover already had a title: “What They Knew When They Left Us Without Roads.”

Kael read it standing up.

“We haven't established that the people who made the current closure decisions knew what that correspondence contained.”

“But we know warnings were issued before the Fracture.”

“We have evidence they were issued and received by institutions, yes. That isn't the same as knowing who evaluated them or attributing the current situation to a particular person.”

The official looked tired.

“People can't go on living with distinctions between words.”

Kael remembered the requests from people who had waited years to access documents about their own lives. He knew institutional precision could become an excuse for inaction. He also knew that confusing receipt with a decision could ruin an investigation.

“We can prepare an explanation of the documentary stages,” he proposed. “And we can say what records we would need to move from one stage to the next. But the losses report has to retain its own chain of provenance.”

Tessa stepped in.

“Kael's contract covers voluntary work on public indexes. It doesn't authorize him to examine restricted material or represent the technical authority at a hearing.”

The official withdrew the proposed cover and asked for a methodological note. That did fall within Kael's work, as long as it was identified as an archival contribution and reviewed by his supervisor.

When the visitor had gone, Tessa placed the proposal beside the entry ledger.

“You could have accepted a more visible role.”

“I'd have made it look as though I'd accepted more authority.”

The distinction mattered to him.

***

At the end of the shift, Kael wrote a two-page note.

It explained four levels that should not be confused: the existence of a series, receipt of an item, reading by a person with the relevant authority, and a decision made on the basis of its contents. For each level, he identified what evidence would establish it and which alternative explanations would have to be excluded. He published neither strategic dates nor references that could reveal the locations of active equipment.

Rena prepared the copies. Tessa reviewed them before sending them to the Civic Council, on the condition that they be cited as methodology, not as a judgment about responsibility.

A second reply arrived as they were closing the cabinets. It came from the historical archive holding the committee's catalog. The archive agreed to check whether an old contents inventory existed, provided the request specified the series and the security body reviewed the restrictions.

Kael compared the series number with the corrected concordance table. It matched.

That didn't mean the inventory contained the warning. It meant they could now request a specific check of an identified transfer.

Tessa made the outgoing-register entry.

“Shall we request an examination?”

“Let's request that the custodian preserve the inventory and determine what portion can be certified without exposing restricted contents. If authorization from another authority is needed, they should identify it.”

Rena returned the public ledgers to their shelves. Kael took no reproductions home.

Outside, he saw a notice from the municipal station: load restrictions had been extended pending another measurement. There were people who needed answers faster than a historical series could provide them.

The inquiry had to respect both kinds of urgency without pretending they were the same.

Before he left, Tessa showed him the final line of the custodian's notice. The inventory had been preserved, but its description recorded a discrepancy between the number of packages dispatched and the number entered as received.

Kael read it again.

It could be an accounting error, a package combined with another, or a historical loss.

He didn't know which.

For the first time, they had a concrete discrepancy to examine, not a culprit to accuse.

***End of Episode 04.***
