# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 07: The Cost of Returning Too Soon

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Sera Nhal, limited third person.

***

The reopening proposal arrived in an envelope without any consumption figures.

Sera Nhal read it beside the pump that had forced them to reduce service two days earlier. It came from a merchants' association, backed by a technical adviser from Licensing. They wanted authorization for a nighttime passage window to ease congestion on the overland roads.

The document contained no Anchor configuration, energy budget, or verification of the destination. It simply asserted that brief use would cause less wear than keeping the route closed.

“They've confused time with load,” Sera said.

Nel Or looked up from a dismantled bearing.

“Transit isn't ours to decide.”

“No. But they're asking us to certify that the civilian utility network can absorb any auxiliary maneuver. We do have a duty to check that.”

The station Sera managed had been operating under restrictions for forty-eight hours. The bearing was running cooler now that they had reduced peak loads. The second station, the one that had called at the end of the previous episode, was still waiting for a part for its backup pump. The municipality had no spare margin to offer a strategic corridor additional energy automatically.

Sera asked that the proposal be treated as a hypothesis, not a work order. The adviser protested.

“A window of just a few hours would allow supplies to recover.”

“Perhaps,” she answered. “Before we discuss benefits, I need you to tell us what load you're requesting, where it will come from, and who takes responsibility if it has to be interrupted.”

They did not receive that information. They did receive permission to use the civilian records already available and the low-load bench, disconnected from every active installation, to examine a narrower question: what happened if a service that had not fully recovered was subjected to two closely spaced demands?

***

The test opened no doors.

Sera had learned to distrust demonstrations that began with spectators and ended with people searching for a reason to applaud. Nel's isolated test bench had a workshop accumulator, a dummy resistor, and the independent physical cutoff verified in the previous season's trials. An external expert would record the initial condition. The power would come nowhere near that of an urban station, let alone what an Anchor required.

“We aren't reproducing the Network,” Sera explained to the adviser. “We're checking whether the recovery model you've used to justify a second demand is valid even at the scale you've stated.”

The adviser agreed to watch from behind the safety barrier. He had brought a curve that assumed complete recovery between short pulses.

The first demand was applied within the established protocol. The cutoff activated on time, and heat began to dissipate. The measurement fell within the expected range. So far, nothing was new.

The difference emerged when they assessed the proposed second demand. The approved protocol did not permit it to be carried out before full recovery. Sera did not break that rule. She took the residual temperature and the measured curve, calculated the starting state a second pulse would encounter, and compared it with the bench's permitted maximum.

“According to his curve, we'd be back at rest by now,” Nel said.

The expert showed them the independent reference reading. Thermal energy was still stored in the support.

The adviser leaned over the paper.

“If we adjust the interval—”

“Then it isn't the window you requested,” Sera replied. “And even with the interval adjusted, a workshop measurement doesn't authorize strategic intervention.”

The representative put away his original curve without destroying it. The minutes would preserve both the failed hypothesis and the data that refuted it at that scale.

***

When they came out, the civilian station was sounding a pressure alarm.

Sera heard the mechanical shutoff strike before Nel reached the panel. It was not a failure of the test bench; that circuit remained isolated. A change in demand on the market branch had coincided with the scheduled startup of a secondary pump. The system had reached its protective limit and automatically reduced flow.

“The medical priority supply is stable,” an operator reported. “The commercial district has lost pressure.”

Sera ordered them to keep the affected branch isolated, check the valves, and notify the depots. No one was to restore flow simply because the alarm had stopped.

A merchant entered the yard waving a receipt.

“We've had restrictions for two days. Now you're leaving us without water too?”

Sera went to meet her without removing her gloves.

“The protection activated on one branch, not across the entire station. We're checking why two startups coincided. The office must tell you the actual schedule for reduced service.”

“You always have a reason to make us wait.”

“And you have the right to have that reason measured and reviewed.”

Sera did not offer an impossible date. She asked the office to install a temporary service tap at the approved point, with supervision and a flow limit. The staff carried out the work without raising the station's overall output.

Half an hour later, the register confirmed that the secondary pump had been programmed without accounting for the market's revised hours. The incorrect setting came from a shift-schedule update, not tampering with instruments or a deliberate act against the city.

The coincidence mattered: two demands that each fell within a procedure had occurred at the least convenient moment.

“Does that prove opening the corridor would cause the same thing?” Nel asked.

“No,” Sera answered. “It proves that our civilian system can't promise to absorb an additional load that no one has even quantified for us.”

That distinction was going to cost her a long argument.

***

The argument came that same afternoon.

The Licensing adviser insisted that the low-load bench could not represent an Anchor. Sera agreed. The merchants' representative maintained that the municipal incident had nothing to do with reopening. She accepted that the circuits were separate, too.

Then she laid three items on the table that could be considered together: a demand proposal without a budget, the certified condition of the civilian infrastructure, and the recovery requirement ignored by the proposers' own model.

“I don't need to blame you for a breakdown to reject a guarantee that doesn't exist,” she said. “Before authorizing extraordinary consumption, you have to show where the supply will come from without depriving essential services.”

The municipal office maintained the provisional restriction and required the startup schedules to be corrected. The strategic authority kept its independent prohibition in place. The two decisions were not conflated.

The adviser requested a copy of the test. Sera provided the redacted report and the bench data that could be reproduced without dangerous access. She had no restricted Annex parameters in her possession.

At nightfall, Mara Lis called from the cooperative. She had received the explanation of the latest incident and wanted to know how much more the merchants would have to pay for transport and refrigeration.

Sera looked at the growing pile of receipts.

“I don't know yet. What I do know is that the repair budget won't sustain this arrangement indefinitely.”

Mara fell silent.

“Then saying you can't reopen it isn't enough.”

Sera took off her gloves. That was the part the measurements could not resolve on their own.

“No,” she said. “We also have to show who's paying to keep it closed and decide how that cost should be shared.”

When the call ended, Nel placed a new file on the table. It came from a community absent from the municipal demand tables, but it had started receiving supplies through the station operating under restrictions.

The load had been recorded.

The people affected had not.

***End of Episode 07.***
