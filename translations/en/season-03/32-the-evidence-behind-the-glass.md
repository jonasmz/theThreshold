# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 32: The Evidence Behind the Glass

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Daren Holt, limited third person.

***

Daren Holt recognized the protest before he heard the speaker.

The line filled the corridor of the Safeguards office. Some demanded complete publication of the historical documents; others feared that a hearing naming individuals would harm families who had never been involved in the Fracture. Between the two groups, a woman held an envelope in both hands and kept repeating that she had brought evidence.

“I can't accept originals without a record of provenance,” Daren explained.

“My grandfather kept this because no one wanted to listen,” she answered.

It wasn't a statement he could dismiss. Neither was it a chain of custody.

From a distance, the woman showed him a household notebook containing copies of family transcriptions. She maintained that an ancestor had worked for the body that authorized the test. Daren offered her a receipt for submission, a voluntary interview, and the option to withdraw the object until the authorities assessed how to preserve it. He did not promise that the notebook would be sufficient evidence of a vote.

That day he was to determine the admissibility of the material supporting the previous episode's historical ruling. The general finding had survived adversarial scrutiny, but opening a proceeding about individual responsibilities required a more precise question: which document proved each action, and by what right could an affected party examine it?

A delegate from a successor office proposed excluding all redacted reproductions.

“The defense can't challenge what it can't see,” she argued.

Daren acknowledged the difficulty without agreeing to automatic exclusion.

“If an extract doesn't allow its integrity or context to be challenged, it can't support an accusation by itself. That doesn't invalidate originals that an accredited expert has compared.”

Someone in the corridor shouted that they were protecting the guilty.

Daren opened the hearing-room door.

The problem was not hiding the file behind glass.

It was deciding who could examine what, and how to demonstrate that nobody had changed it.

***

The session began with a sealed box.

An archivist representing the historical custodian checked the seal, catalog reference, and inventory of pages before placing the original on a conservation support. A Safeguards expert could verify pagination, erasures, and correspondence with the extract; the parties would receive a reasoned description of the restrictions and controlled access to whatever affected their defense.

It was not unlimited permission. Some marks could allow the operating sequence of a system with dangerous present-day equivalents to be reconstructed. The safety agency had to justify each restriction and provide a version in which the historical decision could be challenged without exposing instructions.

A family representative objected that the redactions might conceal names.

“That's possible,” the expert replied. “For that question, we need an authorized identity examination and a way for the parties to challenge it. We won't presume the hidden text confirms a hypothesis merely because we can't display it.”

The successor office then challenged the calendar. The original document used administrative cycles rather than civil dates. The independent dating expert explained how the equivalence had been checked against two unrelated records and made the margin of uncertainty visible. Even under the interpretation most favorable to the objection, the decision record still came after the acknowledgment of reading.

Daren separated three questions in his procedural ruling: authenticity of the physical record, its capacity to establish a fact, and the lawfulness of access. An authentic document might be irrelevant to a particular person; a useful copy might be inadmissible if obtaining it had violated someone's rights.

When the session ended, the archivist sealed the original again.

No one had been allowed to take it away.

And no one had been forced to rely solely on Safety's word.

***

The family notebook was examined in another room.

The woman agreed to a recorded statement and allowed two reproduced pages to be compared. According to her, her ancestor had carried out secretarial work and kept notes about a disagreement. The pages bore no institutional seal. The paper and ink might be old, but the date of the copy had yet to be established.

“My family never received any remedy,” she said. “I want them to acknowledge that he tried to stop it.”

Daren heard the need behind her request. The historical minutes already recorded a dissenting vote. He did not know whether it belonged to her ancestor or whether the notes were contemporary. Similar contents did not prove authorship.

He asked her permission to request the period's staff register from the statutes office, limited to the relevant position and substitution. She agreed but refused permission to publish household details about the rest of her family.

An assistant reported that the notebook would have to wait for an expert examination slot. There wasn't enough funding to examine every object received at once. Daren assigned priority based on the risk of physical loss and relevance, not the emotion of each applicant.

The woman returned the notebook to its envelope.

“What if it deteriorates while we're waiting?”

“We can document its condition and offer preventive conservation without requiring you to surrender ownership,” he replied.

Outside, a siren from the civilian station announced a change of shift. Daren remembered that the city was still being kept alive through shifts and repairs while they debated an ancient catastrophe.

His own criminal proceeding, open since the previous season, was waiting in another office. He would not use historical testimony to introduce material into that case without jurisdiction and safeguards.

Each case had its own victims, evidence, and rights.

***

The admissibility ruling took fewer pages than the objections.

Daren authorized the use of archival certifications and expert reports to reconstruct defined institutional facts, subject to challenge. He ordered the register of restrictions preserved, the redactions justified, and specialist review permitted whenever an accusation depended on material that could not be disclosed. He left the family notebook under evaluation and rejected the idea that a photograph of unknown provenance could suffice to attribute a vote.

The successor office's delegate approached him afterward.

“Can you call it a trial when not everyone can read the same things?”

“Only if the restrictions are necessary, reviewable, and don't prevent a person from defending themselves against the specific accusation,” Daren said. “If they do prevent it, the evidence mustn't be used that way.”

The representative of the outside communities requested a turn. They had traveled for the hearing and did not want to return without understanding how to submit the costs of the present closures.

Daren asked the secretary to prepare a clear sheet describing two channels: the historical procedure, concerned with proven responsibility, and the civilian one, concerned with current losses and its own custodians and deadlines. A person could take part in both without either proceeding absorbing the other.

The woman with the notebook was still waiting near the exit. Daren gave her the receipt and the preservation status she had requested.

“Will you notify me before examining the original?”

“Yes. And agreeing to its preservation doesn't mean agreeing to every question someone might ask you.”

She nodded and left.

As he closed the room, Daren saw the control ledger recording every access to the historical original. Each signature identified a specific function, a time, and a reason.

He thought of the ancient registration officer so many people had mistaken for a decision-maker.

He would not let the present investigation repeat that injustice.

The next hearing would have admissible evidence.

What remained was ensuring that the accused and the affected could hear it on equivalent terms.

***End of Episode 32.***
