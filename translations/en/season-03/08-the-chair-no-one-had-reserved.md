# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 08: The Chair No One Had Reserved

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel, limited third person.

***

The claims room had seventeen chairs and a pigeonhole for each district.

Inara Vel saw it in a photograph Joren Mal had taken during the information session. On the reception desk lay a form intended for registered businesses, workers with a municipal address, or recognized associations. The notice spoke of people affected by the closures, but said nothing about what to do if someone depended on the corridors and belonged to none of those groups.

“The office says everyone can submit evidence,” Joren said.

“That's not the same as being allowed to speak at the hearing,” Inara replied.

The difference seemed minor until a woman called from a roadside establishment. She introduced herself as Ada Venn, temporary manager of a dining hall near the valley road. She did not want her name published. She had twice submitted invoices for extraordinary transport expenses, and both requests had been returned because she had no business address inside Vael-Tir.

“I don't want a vote on the city's budget,” she said. “I want someone to hear how much it cost us to bring in water when they closed the corridor.”

Inara asked whether she had receipts. Ada kept copies of freight bills, depot receipts, and minutes from the dining hall acknowledging the debt. They documented an expense; they were not proof of the Council's responsibility or of anyone's legal authority to represent others.

“Can I show you the receipts without saying whose meals they paid for?” Ada asked.

“Yes. Cover the families' details. If we publish anything, I'll explain first what information could identify you.”

Joren brought the claims form closer.

The “district” field was mandatory.

Some people's losses fit into the accounts, but not onto the paper.

***

Inara traveled to the municipal boundary on a delivery cart, without requesting access to any restricted route.

Ada received her beside a kitchen that smelled of onions and hot water. Three women were dividing loaves into bags. On one wall hung a calendar covered in crossed-out shifts and alternative routes written by hand.

The dining hall served passing workers and families from a hamlet beyond the council's jurisdiction. Several cooperatives provided its funding. None could appear on behalf of everyone without a mandate.

Ada showed her two invoices. The first covered road transport during a documented closure; the second covered an extra journey after a bridge imposed a weight limit on carts. Inara acknowledged that the second cost could not be attributed entirely to the strategic decision: the bridge inspection had its own cause.

“Then they'll deduct it again,” Ada said.

“No. We'll distinguish why you paid for each stretch. Fair compensation depends on that distinction.”

One of the cooks, Nela, joined in.

“The city saves money when we feed the people waiting here. But when we ask to speak, they tell us we're outsiders.”

Inara asked whether Nela was authorized to speak for the dining hall. Nela looked at Ada, then shook her head.

“I can speak for my own shift and my unpaid hours. The rest is something we all have to decide together.”

Inara wrote down the sentence but did not publish it. Specific permission would come, if it came at all, after a meeting of those involved.

As she left, she noticed a folding chair beside the door. Ada took it on deliveries because some recipients could not remain standing through hours of waiting.

The image struck Inara as better than any chart.

She did not take a photograph. First, she wanted to know who might be recognized in it.

***

The second voice came by telephone and asked never to appear in the newspaper.

An older man had missed a medical appointment when his transport was suspended. The health center later offered him a new appointment, but he had to pay for a companion's journey overland. He still had the receipt. He did not want his diagnosis or his name disclosed, and he had not authorized his daughter to speak for him without consulting him.

Inara verified only that the center had a rescheduling procedure, without requesting clinical information. To document the expense, she needed permission to use the receipt and a clear agreement on its scope. The man consented to having the amount included in aggregate figures with other evidence, not to having his personal case published.

Back in the newsroom, Joren had prepared a headline: “The City's Forgotten.”

“That makes it sound as if we discovered them,” Inara said. “They aren't our characters.”

The new article explained a verifiable problem: the call for claims provided no clear channels for people outside the municipality or for those unable to attend. It included consented expense figures, with qualifications, addressed the municipality's arguments, and asked the organizer to specify how third-party statements would be received without granting anyone universal authority to speak for others.

The council secretariat replied that the form had been designed for municipal budgets and was not intended to exclude other affected people. It offered a written submission channel but acknowledged that the speakers' list was closed.

Inara published both positions.

By midafternoon, a message arrived from Ada. The dining hall had held an assembly and appointed two spokespeople to explain its shared expenses, without authorizing them to reveal the names of people receiving meals.

“We don't want you calling us victims while there are still things we're discussing,” Ada said.

“I can call people making claims claimants, and people who can document the harm they've described affected parties,” Inara answered. “I don't need to invent a verdict.”

***

As they closed the edition, an assistant from the council called the newsroom.

The hearing being prepared by Inspector Daren Holt would accept documentation about preservation and losses, but it was not yet clear which authorities could hear witnesses from outside the city. The office had not asked Inara to select spokespeople. It wanted to know where to obtain the public minutes of the assembly, if those entitled to authorize their release consented.

Inara consulted Ada before replying.

“They can receive the version without distribution details,” the woman said. “But they'll have to invite us directly. I don't want the newspaper speaking for us.”

The journalist passed on the condition and a contact authorized to receive notifications. She sent neither personal invoices nor lists of diners.

Joren looked at the blank space he had left in the article for a photograph.

“We could use the chair.”

“An empty chair has no right of reply,” Inara answered. “Ada does.”

That night they printed a service notice explaining how to file claims and the limits of each jurisdiction. It did not promise that everyone would receive compensation, or that the council could order the Council to reopen a corridor.

The official response arrived before dawn: the spokespeople's documents would be accepted, and the authority presiding over the hearing would consider allowing them to participate remotely. The conditions were still unresolved.

Inara filed the acknowledgment and called Ada.

“There's a way to submit your assembly's minutes. They still haven't given you a voice in the room.”

“I already knew that,” the woman answered. “What I need now is for them not to decide who gets to speak again while I'm not looking.”

Inara left the newspaper open at the announcement.

That claim needed neither a secret witness nor an impossible machine.

It needed a place in a procedure where no one had reserved one in advance.

***End of Episode 08.***
