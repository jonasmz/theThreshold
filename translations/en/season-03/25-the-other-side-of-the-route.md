# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 25: The Other Side of the Route

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Lyra Saren, limited third person.

***

The document they needed had a corner torn away.

Lyra Saren saw it in the copy sent to her by the audit's logistics coordination office. It was a historical manifest of transfers between offices, reproduced from a reference collection. It documented a partial delivery of technical papers before the Fracture. The field that should have described the attached annex was cut off by an old tear.

“They want me to find another copy,” she told Ema.

“Does one exist?”

“There's a reference to an outside depot. No one has confirmed what it holds.”

The requesting office proposed sending an expedition immediately. Lyra had learned from the first document shipment that traveling without confirmation could mean paying to visit an empty shelf. She had no intention of repeating that mistake.

She asked the receiving custodian to verify the index and conditions of access before anyone booked transport. The reply came by administrative message: an archive beyond the valley held a series of transport ledgers, but its staff would have to conduct an on-site search before it could confirm the identity of any particular page.

The journey was an ordinary one, with a bridge subject to weight restrictions and a river section dependent on the rains. There was no authorized Threshold, no verified Anchor, and no budget for inventing one.

Ema checked the shelter's calendar.

“The cooperative needs another supply delivery this week. The cart they want to hire is the same one that carries the parts.”

Lyra put the manifest on the table.

That missing corner had been waiting for centuries.

The valley's spare parts had not.

***

Negotiations with the outside archive were shorter than the argument over transport.

The depot director rejected a proposal to send an entire box to Vael-Tir so the audit could examine it there.

“Our jurisdiction doesn't permit originals to leave in response to a consultation request,” she explained. “We can examine the series ourselves and, if appropriate, issue a certified reproduction of the fields we're authorized to release.”

Lyra accepted. She had no authority to demand anything else.

“What information do you need to locate it?”

“Provenance reference, equivalent date, and the sender's reference. The number on the torn copy isn't enough. It recurs in several series.”

The audit office could supply two of the three details. The third required consulting the sending archive's index, whose custodian maintained restrictions on the names of strategic equipment.

Lyra arranged for the two archives to speak directly through institutional channels. Each would decide which fields it could communicate and what needed to be redacted. Her role was limited to arranging the communication and calculating the cost of a possible shipment.

The director called back the next day.

“We found a receiving entry that can be linked to the indicated interval. It's an entry for a batch, not an individual document.”

“Is there a description of the annex?”

“An abbreviation. It might mean 'operating conditions' or 'conservation conditions,' depending on which set of rules was used.”

The difference was too great to resolve by intuition.

The director held a manual of abbreviations from the period, but it belonged to a different collection and required permission before it could be consulted for comparison. The audit agreed to pay for the necessary staff hours without buying preferential access to the archives.

Lyra communicated a realistic schedule.

The coordinator was impatient.

“We're close to finding out which warnings accompanied the test.”

“We're close to finding out what an abbreviation means,” she answered. “That's not the same thing.”

***

The cost arrived before the document.

A bridge inspection announced a new restriction during repairs. Heavier vehicles would have to take a detour, adding a day. The valley cooperative had just reported that some of the delivered parts had been installed, but water-quality checks were still needed before service could be expanded.

Ema had assembled funds for a second light shipment of conventional supplies. The audit offered to pay the same carrier to collect a certified reproduction from the outside archive. Combining the two loads might reduce costs, provided their weights, custodial requirements, and destinations were compatible.

Lyra brought both contracting parties together.

“I won't put archival material on a medical supply cart if that compromises its chain of custody or delays the supplies people are waiting for,” she warned.

The carrier outlined the transfer points. The cooperative approved a vehicle for supplies. The archive, however, had not yet issued the reproduction: there was no documentary package to collect.

The audit coordinator proposed reserving an empty space for it.

“If it isn't ready, you'll still pay for that space,” Lyra said. “And you might not be able to use it for supplies.”

The shelter assembly voted to fund the medical shipment according to its approved manifest. The audit arranged a separate courier service, contingent on the document being ready. It was not the cheapest option on paper, but it prevented one institution from shifting its risk onto another.

Ema stayed behind after the meeting.

“You used to try to make both journeys yourself.”

Lyra felt the old impulse to prove that she still could.

“I used to think that knowing a road meant I was obliged to travel it. Not anymore.”

She remembered some crossings with painful clarity and others only through records kept by other people. No effort could restore her lost experiences intact.

She could, however, choose which promises she made now.

***

The reproduction arrived by a route Lyra had not traveled.

The outside depot had authorized a comparison of its index with the manual of abbreviations. The entry proved to mean “conditions for conservation of the supporting material.” It was a packaging instruction, not the hoped-for evidence that a technical warning had accompanied the transfer.

The audit coordinator swore when he heard the answer.

“Then we made the journey for nothing.”

“No one traveled for that piece of paper,” Lyra replied. “The custodian checked it in its own archive and kept us from pursuing a false interpretation.”

Even so, the examination had revealed something else. The depot's ledger recorded a second referral with the same date of provenance but a different recipient. Its description mentioned an “evaluation summary,” separate from the packaging material. The director certified only the existence and metadata of the referral. She had not located its contents and could not guarantee that it concerned the test they were studying.

A certified copy of those metadata could be sent by ordinary mail. Receipt would be handled by an audit custodian, not Lyra.

Ema appeared with a message from the valley. The water had passed an initial quality inspection, although not all service restrictions could be lifted yet. The parts delivered had enabled concrete progress.

Lyra smiled. It was not a complete solution. It was something people could actually use.

The technical coordinator called again.

“Can we organize an expedition to the second office?”

“First ask whether it still has the summary and who has the right to examine it. Then we'll see whether anyone needs to travel.”

When the call ended, Lyra looked at the manifest with its torn corner.

The absence had not been filled by a miraculous discovery.

They had established that the visible field described something else, and that a different document had followed a different route.

If they wanted to know who had known about the risk, they would have to follow that second referral.

And every step would require evidence that the path existed.

***End of Episode 25.***
