# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 13: The Measure of Two Shadows

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Sera Nhal, limited third person.

***

The instrument said the two marks were identical.

Sera Nhal knew that was impossible.

Two reference plates lay on the bench, made from the same alloy and engraved on different days. One had been heated and cooled under controlled conditions; the other had remained in a sealed box. The Resonance reader, designed to detect surface alterations, showed the same trace for both.

Nel Or pointed at the display.

“If it can't tell these standards apart, it won't distinguish an old correction from recent damage either.”

“We don't even know whether it can date anything,” Sera replied. “First we have to establish what difference it can detect.”

The audit authorized at the end of the previous block needed to compare physical records preserved by different archives. Some custody seals had been measured by instruments from different generations. Others survived only as impressions, photographs, or operators' descriptions. There was a proposal for comparison, not technology capable of revealing the past.

Sera had agreed to design a comparison procedure using new standards, without moving originals or touching restricted documents. Her experience with the isolated bench qualified her to discuss calibration, power, and errors. It did not make her a historian or give her authority over the archives.

“What's the consumption?” she asked.

Nel showed her the log. A workshop accumulator supplied the reader in short pulses; the equipment released some of the energy as heat and converted some into a useful signal. There was no connection to a strategic node and no possibility of activating Transit.

“It's within the authorized maximum.”

“That tells me how much it used. Not why it stopped distinguishing the plates.”

Sera disconnected the power and requested another reading after the equipment had cooled completely. No one would be allowed to adjust the signal while it was live.

On the second test, the standards began to show different readings.

The missing shadow was not in the plates.

It was in the heated instrument.

***

An independent laboratory sent its own standards and two calibration sheets.

The first was a modern plate measured against a mechanical reference. The second was an old reference support, containing no sensitive historical material, with a mark documented during conservation. It could not be used to infer a document's age, but it could reveal whether the reader responded differently to variations in material and surface roughness.

The external observer found a problem before anyone switched anything on.

“Your zero references aren't mounted on the same type of support as the other laboratory's.”

Nel checked the box. One standard was fixed to a metal base. The other rested on an insulating table. In the first arrangement, part of the return signal could couple to the base and change the reading.

“We can replace all the supports,” he suggested.

“Then we'd lose the comparison with earlier records,” Sera said. “First we'll document how they were set up.”

They photographed the components, measured temperature, checked the accumulator's discharge limit, and installed independent heat sensors. The procedure allowed one pulse per standard and required waiting for recovery. No one was to remain near the conductors during activation.

The result changed when they used a common base. Not enough to make the materials appear identical, but enough to account for part of the difference between the laboratories.

The expert drafted a provisional conclusion: the signal depended on the surface and the conditions of support. Calibration had to specify both. It did not allow just any old mark to be converted into an exact date.

Another consequence troubled Sera. The bench's budget had consumed staff hours needed for checking the municipal secondary pump. The technical office had authorized the audit work, but they still had to fulfill their maintenance shifts.

“Tomorrow we go back to the civilian station,” she told Nel. “History can't leave us without water this week.”

***

The serious problem emerged when they examined the records.

An archivist presented a certified printout of a seal held at another depot. Sera did not touch the original, which remained with its custodian. The printout included a curve recorded decades earlier by a device whose temperature reference had been replaced during a later reform.

The audit representative wanted to know whether they could overlay that curve with a current measurement.

“We can draw them on the same sheet,” Sera said. “That doesn't mean they're measuring the same thing.”

She requested the old device's calibration record. It was absent from the material supplied. There was a maintenance note, but no identified standard. A scale number might refer to intensity, a support correction, or merely a needle position.

The representative insisted.

“If the seal was altered, we ought to be able to see it.”

“We might detect certain alterations if we had a reference, comparable material, and authorized access. We don't have those three things together.”

Nel proposed building a mathematical adapter from the available curves. Sera asked him to write down his assumptions first: identical thermal response, identical geometry, and no previous repairs. All three were open to question.

The external observer suggested something less impressive. The two archives could run separate tests on common modern standards, each keeping its originals and transmitting only authorized readings and configuration records. That would help establish how far apart the instruments' readings were. It would not prove what had happened centuries earlier.

Sera agreed to design the protocol. It would have to be signed by the custodians and by those operating each device; no reading would leave without provenance and uncertainty recorded.

“So we won't have proof of forgery,” the representative said.

“We'll have a way of detecting whether we're manufacturing a false match.”

The archivist put the certified printout back in her folder.

For the first time, Sera saw relief instead of disappointment.

***

The day ended with three conclusions of different sizes.

Heating the reader reduced its ability to distinguish standards. The metal base altered the return signal. And the historical curve could not become a date without its missing reference.

Sera approved a calibration procedure at cold and operating temperatures using modern materials, with measured recovery intervals, a common support, and documented error. Another laboratory would have to reproduce it without copying their results. For comparisons between archives, each custodian would retain its objects and originals; comparisons would use authorized data, not documents transported for convenience.

The budget was enough to prepare standards and send them through ordinary post. It would not cover moving entire devices and did not require opening any multiversal connection.

When she returned to the station, Sera found a worker adjusting the secondary pump under supervision. The scheduling error had been corrected, but reserve capacity remained limited. She signed the maintenance sheet first and only then filed the laboratory report.

Nel caught up with her near the exit.

“If we manage to calibrate both archives, will we be able to say their seals are authentic?”

“We'll be able to say which differences the method detects and which it doesn't. Authenticity also requires a chain of custody.”

A messenger delivered an envelope from one of the depots. It contained provisional acceptance of the protocol and a warning: its reference standards had been replaced during an old flood. Documentation of that replacement was held by another office.

Nel sighed.

“Another missing piece.”

Sera looked at the pump report, where every repair had a signature, a date, and a record of the material used.

“It's not just a piece that's missing,” she replied. “We need to know which one was used when the reading was taken.”

The device worked.

The question it was supposed to answer had not yet been calibrated.

***End of Episode 13.***
