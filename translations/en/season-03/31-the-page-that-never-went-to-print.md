# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 31: The Page That Never Went to Print

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Inara Vel, limited third person.

***

Inara Vel found the folded printer's proofs beneath the door.

She had spent the night preparing a report on the historical hearing. At first glance, the front page was correct: it attributed no criminal guilt and did not declare the Network repaired. But on an inside page someone had added an illustration of the ancient test. The drawing showed two circuits and a set of marks taken from a restricted reproduction. One of those marks did not appear in the material cleared for the press.

“Who delivered this?” she asked Joren Mal.

Joren set a cup on the counter.

“It came with the hearing summaries. I thought it was a public annex.”

The presses were due to start before noon. The editor could not afford another wasted print run: paper prices had risen since the corridor closures, and vendors charged for every delay.

Inara set the sheet aside.

“We can publish an explanation of the mechanism without copying an operating configuration. This detail isn't in the material cleared for disclosure.”

“If we remove it, they'll say we're still protecting secrets,” Joren replied.

“If we leave it, we could be circulating instructions no one has authorized for release.”

The word *transparency* did not resolve that tension. Historical archives held information about systems whose present-day safety remained fragile. At the same time, those affected had the right to understand why a decision taken in the face of known risk had been established.

Inara called the custodian of the original material. She did not ask for the hidden parameters so she could check them herself. She requested confirmation of the publication limits and the image's provenance.

The answer would take a few hours.

The editor entered carrying the proof, already marked for printing.

“Are we running it or not?”

Inara placed a hand on the page.

“The verified text goes to print. This image doesn't. Not yet.”

***

The first obstacle came from readers, not Safety.

A delegation of relatives and workers waited at the newspaper's entrance. They carried a copy of the official statement and had underlined the sentence describing a historical decision taken with conscious acceptance of risk. They wanted to know why the decision-makers' names were absent from the newspaper.

Inara explained that the ruling had established an act by a competent body, while individual participation and responsibility remained subject to a separate procedure. The most visible name in the records belonged to a receiving officer, not someone who had cast a vote.

“Does that mean none of them will answer for it?” a worker asked.

“It means I won't attribute a vote to someone who signed a receipt.”

The woman beside him tapped a transport invoice.

“Then print this too. Every week that passes, we pay again.”

Inara recognized the problem. In her historical report, present-day losses occupied only a small box. It was a convenient way to discuss the past without listening to those still bearing its consequences.

She requested permission to use the association's aggregated figures, with an opportunity to correct them before publication. She asked for neither medical names nor photographs of families. The delegation agreed to supply an accounting summary and reserved the right to review how it was cited.

The editor watched from the corridor.

“We don't have room for two complete stories.”

“Then we shorten the explanation of the seals,” Inara answered. “Not the rights of the people paying the cost.”

The meeting ended without agreement about the names. But one representative returned to leave a warning.

“Don't make us wait for a conviction before we can ask for water.”

Inara did not promise a remedy her newspaper had no power to order.

She promised to distinguish clearly between responsibilities today and those still awaiting judgment.

***

Confirmation about the image arrived with an uncomfortable question.

The historical custodian stated that the drawing in the printer's proof came from a restricted review copy. The public extracts permitted an explanation of the general phenomenon of coupled loads; they did not authorize reproduction of that equipment's layout.

“I need to know how this reached our newsroom,” Inara told Joren.

He showed her a folder received from a printing collaborator. Apparently without noticing, the collaborator had mixed a sheet marked for internal review with the material approved for release. Inara recorded its receipt, restricted access to the working copies, and asked Safeguards for a formal return procedure. She did not destroy material herself when its chain of custody might require examination.

The archivist confirmed that the leak would have to be investigated without presuming bad faith. A distribution error did not make sensitive instructions safe to publish.

Inara prepared an alternative illustration: two reservoirs sharing a drain, whose total capacity did not increase just because each reservoir had its own valve. It was an educational analogy, with no proportions or operating details. Before approving it, she asked an expert authorized to communicate publicly to check whether it suggested an overly broad conclusion.

The response was cautious: the image could explain the limitations of models that considered each load separately, not reproduce the Fracture exactly or establish the conditions within an Anchor.

“We'll put that beneath it,” Inara decided.

Joren studied the new front page.

“It looks less spectacular.”

“It makes it easier to understand why we can't improvise another test.”

Outside, a cart unloaded reams of paper. The invoice demanded immediate payment. The editor held it between two fingers, well aware that the delay already had a cost.

Inara signed off on the replacement printing plate.

This time, she knew exactly what information would leave the presses.

***

The edition appeared at nightfall.

It contained neither names without verified attribution nor installation plans. It distinguished the established historical finding, the individual questions still open, and the technical restrictions still in force. It also published civilian expenses verified by the people entitled to report them, without turning present suffering into proof of historical guilt.

The first call came from Licensing. The spokesperson argued that the analogy might alarm the public.

“Then explain which safety limits you're monitoring today,” Inara replied. “Our article doesn't claim the Annex is failing in that way.”

The second came from Ada Venn at the dining hall. They had received the edition and wanted copies for an assembly outside the municipality.

“We can't afford the full newspapers,” Ada clarified.

Inara arranged to send a separate sheet containing the freely reproducible civilian information and the procedure for filing claims. The newsroom would pay for it from its own budget, not the dining hall's.

When she ended the call, Joren showed her an unsigned letter. Someone demanded the publication of the complete technical annexes or an admission that the investigation was a sham. The letter mentioned a specific page of the restricted material.

Inara kept it as a received communication and notified the authorized channel for documentary incidents. She neither published it nor turned it into an accusation against an official.

As she left, she saw a little girl reading aloud the simple diagram they had put on the front page.

The girl understood that two demands could exhaust the same support.

She did not need to know how to activate a dangerous machine to understand that someone had disregarded a warning.

Inara descended the stairs, her fingers stained with ink.

The truth had reached the streets.

The part that could cause harm remained under examination.

The cost of both decisions would appear in the next edition.

***End of Episode 31.***
