# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 35: Those Who Weren't in the Room

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Lyra Saren, limited third person.

***

The invitation to testify had arrived without money for the journey.

Lyra Saren found it beside the shelter's supply list. The commission reviewing accountability for the Fracture was summoning civilian representatives, but the session would take place in Vael-Tir and required in-person registration to speak. The valley communities, whose roads remained subject to restrictions, would have to hire transport, miss days of work, and pay for accommodation.

Ema laid the paper on the table.

“They invite us because we're affected. And to explain how we're affected, we have to spend money we don't have.”

Lyra examined the participation rules. They allowed remote appearances for justified reasons, although the public form did not mention them. There was also a support fund for affected people, subject to application to the competent authority. It would not cover travel for everyone.

The valley cooperative had completed part of the water system's repairs; quality tests allowed some service hours to be extended, not the declaration that full service had been restored. New travel expenses might delay maintenance supplies.

“I can take two people by road,” a driver offered.

“Who would we choose?” Ema asked. “The ones who can leave their jobs? The ones who speak better?”

Lyra did not want to make that choice simply because she knew the roads. She had learned that transporting someone did not confer the right to represent them.

She called for an assembly at the shelter and sent an inquiry to the commission: which forms of participation it recognized, which expenses it could cover, and whether appearing remotely would carry the same procedural weight.

The answer would not arrive before the community meeting.

The hearing clock was moving faster than the roads.

***

The assembly took place beneath a borrowed lamp, with the windows open.

Representatives of the cooperative, a caregiver, two drivers, and people living beyond the valley's administrative boundaries attended. They did not all share the same priority. Some wanted to discuss the corridor closures; others demanded compensation for earlier expenses. A delivery worker asked that her labor shifts not be mixed into the dining hall's accounts.

Lyra placed a road map on the table, then quickly moved it aside.

“Before we discuss transport, we need to know what each person wants to present,” she said.

Ada Venn joined over an authorized line. She explained that she could submit the dining hall's figures with the consent of its assembly, not speak for every community receiving food. The cooperative had data on water supplies; the neighborhood association kept its own receipts. None of them should borrow another's voice.

An older man asked Lyra to be their general spokesperson.

“You know the roads, and people listen to you.”

She felt the dangerous relief of a quick solution.

“I can describe transport costs I've verified,” she answered. “I can't represent people who haven't chosen me.”

The caregiver suggested a simple procedure: each group would decide whether to participate, what information to share, and who had limited authorization to present it. People without representatives could submit their own statements, with personal data protected.

Ema wrote down the agreement. They disagreed about priorities, and those disagreements stayed in the minutes. A genuine assembly did not have to produce a single voice to deserve to be heard.

As they left, Lyra received a message from the commission. It would consider a remote connection but required a location with stable communications and privacy safeguards.

The shelter had a line.

It did not always have enough power to sustain one throughout a hearing.

***

The connection test failed just as people began speaking.

A low-capacity accumulator powered the civilian repeater during interruptions to the conventional network. The equipment was not an Anchor and could not enable Transit; it depended on stored energy with limited output and duration. The signal dropped when another shelter installation began drawing power.

Ema consulted the load table.

“If we disconnect the auxiliary pump during the session, we can keep the line running.”

“We're not going to leave a water shift without supply so we can tell them we need water,” Lyra replied.

A municipal technician offered a connection from the valley library, which had conventional power and a separate backup system. The room was an hour away by road, and reaching it was difficult for some older residents.

Lyra organized local transport using an inspected vehicle. The driver agreed to a reduced fare paid from the assistance fund after the commission confirmed the authorized expenses in writing. No shelter money would be used without the assembly's approval.

The communications rehearsal worked, though one participant could barely hear because she was seated too far from the microphone. They moved the chairs rather than increasing the volume until it overwhelmed the line. They arranged for each statement to be read by its author or by a person expressly authorized to do so, without pretending someone unable to connect was speaking in person.

“This is more complicated than taking everyone there,” the driver said.

“And probably fairer than choosing two names,” Lyra replied.

The commission required documents before the administrative deadline. A certified copy of the transport expenses arrived with a duplicated figure: the same journey appeared both on the cooperative's receipt and on the dining hall's.

Lyra asked for the costs to be separated according to who had actually paid them. She did not want to inflate a legitimate claim with duplicated accounts.

The deadline was drawing close.

The assembly preferred submitting a smaller figure it could defend.

***

On the day of the hearing, the library smelled of dust and warm wood.

Lyra sat behind the front row. She was not the principal spokesperson. Her role was to accompany the participants, check that communications worked, and answer questions about routes and manifests within the limits of what she knew.

The chair of the session confirmed that remote speakers would receive a turn equivalent to those present in person. The secretary explained which materials could enter the public record and which would remain protected. People could withdraw unnecessary personal details without forfeiting their claims.

The first speaker discussed water service. She did not demand that a Threshold be opened or dispute the Annex's measurements. She demanded an assistance schedule and an authority accountable for supplies.

A driver described the bridge's weight restrictions and the working days lost to detours. Ada presented the dining hall's expenses without naming those receiving meals. Another resident questioned whether a single commission could hear consequences spanning several jurisdictions.

The chair agreed to receive the claims and refer them to the competent bodies, maintaining a public record of referrals and deadlines. She promised neither compensation nor reopened roads. She did, however, order responses to identify which authority had taken responsibility for each matter, so nobody would have to start again simply because they had been born on the other side of an administrative boundary.

When the session ended, the connection stayed open a few seconds longer.

The valley caregiver approached the microphone.

“We didn't come to ask for the past to become different,” she said. “We came to make our lives count when you decide what to do with what you know.”

Lyra felt the pressure of her own missing memories. Some promises she had made on other roads now existed only in accounts and records. That absence could not be repaired. But she could listen to a request and help sustain it in the present.

At the exit, Ema reviewed the list of responses promised by the commission.

They had obtained responsible recipients and deadlines.

They had not obtained permanent water service or an open route.

Lyra folded the map.

The next decision concerning the Threshold could not be made solely by those permitted to approach it.

And someone had just put it on record that everyone else was there, too.

***End of Episode 35.***
