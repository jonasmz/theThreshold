# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 22: The Test Before the Warning

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Kael Veyran, limited third person.

***

The old test had a date, but nobody knew what had been tested.

Kael Veyran studied the index of a workshop that no longer existed. The public catalog mentioned a contract to measure load recovery, an acceptance report, and an invoice paid in two installments. Everything had taken place before the Fracture and, according to the recently corrected chronology, before the certified circulation of the first warning.

Rena Tor slid a sheet across the table.

“If there was a test before the warning, perhaps they already knew about the danger.”

“First we need to establish whether the test involved the same system,” Kael said. “'Load recovery' could describe very different equipment.”

His voluntary work at the Archive allowed him to consult published indexes and commercial records. Tessa Ord had made it clear that no request from the audit automatically expanded his permissions. Kael's private file remained with Safeguards, far from that table.

The invoice listed a unit of measurement that had fallen out of use. Rena thought she recognized it from the documentation of Sera's modern test bench. Kael noted the similarity without treating the units as equivalent. Names for quantities survived reforms; their definitions did not always survive unchanged.

“Let's look for the contract specifications,” he suggested.

The old purchasing office retained a public procurement register. The specifications described a workshop bench test using components taken out of service, with a prohibition on connecting a load to an active node. That eliminated one spectacular possibility: there was no record of anyone opening a Threshold during that test.

But the specifications mentioned a calibration reference sent from another office.

It wasn't the result.

It was a way to date the instrument they had been talking about.

***

The second archive confirmed that it held a ledger of reference standards loaned out.

Its custodian permitted examination of a certified reproduction of the administrative fields. The standard had been sent to the workshop before the invoice date and returned with a notation concerning wear. Its number matched the contract specifications, although one digit in the original register appeared to have been corrected.

“Can we be sure it's the same one?” Rena asked.

“The custodian can certify what its ledger says. For the corrected number, it needs a physical examination we aren't authorized to conduct.”

Kael prepared two columns: evidence of the loan and possible identification of the standard. The first contained consistent dates. The second had to remain conditional.

A conservation specialist joined their afternoon call. She explained that the apparent correction had been made using ink employed to update catalog references after a reorganization. The original bore an equivalence mark connecting the two numbers; a later transcription had omitted the symbol.

It was not evidence of forgery. It was a descriptive defect.

The loan ledger did establish a time window: the workshop had access to an identified reference standard when the declared test was performed. Alongside the acceptance register, invoice, and return acknowledgment, the sequence made it plausible that the contracted work had been carried out before the disaster.

“Plausible isn't the same as proven,” Rena said.

Kael gave the faintest smile.

“That's why we need the acceptance record, not just the invoice.”

The purchasing office confirmed that it retained the cover and a sheet bearing acknowledgment signatures. The technical body of the report was subject to a safety review because it might contain parameters still applicable to vulnerable installations.

Tessa authorized Kael to continue working with public headings only.

The important piece remained behind a permission someone else had to grant.

***

At the documentary group's meeting, a councilor wanted to link the dates.

“Test, warning, Fracture. We already have a sequence.”

Kael pointed to the distance between each word.

“We have a contract and acknowledgment of completed work dated before a warning whose contents we still haven't examined. We haven't demonstrated that the test led to the warning.”

The purchasing custodian's representative supplied a new certification: the acceptance record stated that results had been received from a recovery test conducted on an isolated workshop assembly. She could not disclose adjustment values or the composition of the support, but she could confirm the general purpose and administrative date.

An independent expert had compared the procurement calendar with that of the office that lent the standard. Their conversions agreed within the ordinary uncertainty of the cycle closing dates.

That allowed them to date the test with greater confidence than a catalog legend could provide. It did not establish that the results were correct, that anyone read them, or that their conclusions justified the intervention preceding the Fracture.

The councilor persisted.

“If a safety warning appeared afterward, what other interpretation could there be?”

“They might concern the same problem, or different problems, or have been compared later. We need the documentary connection before we can choose.”

The argument consumed an hour of work. Kael knew that outside the room supplies were still scarce and rest shifts were still being missed. He also knew a premature accusation could close off access to custodians who were still cooperating.

The representative suggested a way forward: certify the date and nature of the test, then submit a request through the proper channel for a restricted examination of whether its report had been cited in any warning. Accredited specialists, not Kael, would conduct that examination.

Tessa supported the procedure.

“Your contribution ends with identifying the metadata,” she reminded him.

Kael accepted that without feeling excluded. He had learned that choosing the limits of his work was part of keeping it a choice of his own.

***

A week of correspondence was condensed into one small acknowledgment.

The purchasing office confirmed the date of the work and a later referral of its summary to the former technical advisory office. In the margin was an abbreviated reference resembling one used by the warning examined at the Archive. For now, the custodian refused to certify that the references were equivalent: indexes from different offices reused abbreviations.

Kael kept the references separate.

Rena had been waiting for a definitive match for days.

“Don't you get tired of every door having another ledger?” she asked.

“Yes. But if we open the wrong one, we may destroy the very evidence we're looking for.”

A woman entered the consultation room with a request to locate a family file. Tessa explained the procedure for personal access; she did not mix it with the audit's historical work. Kael saw again what was at stake in a library: the ability to preserve lives without taking possession of them.

At the end of his shift he wrote his note: evidence of an isolated technical test before the Fracture, dated through procurement, a reference standard, and administrative acceptance; technical results unknown to those present in that room; causal connection with the warning and subsequent decisions still pending.

Rena read it and pointed to the phrase “results unknown.”

“What if someone knows them somewhere else?”

“Then let whoever has the right to examine them establish that, and explain how.”

The preservation office sent one final message. It had identified a file recording the dispatch of the test summary. Its cover contained a field marked “recipient by function,” but the public transcription omitted the position because it was restricted.

A complete answer would require comparing that function with the legal powers of the time.

Kael closed the index.

They could now place a test before the warning.

They still could not say who had turned its results into an authorization.

***End of Episode 22.***
