# Echoes of the Threshold
## Season 3 · Episode 03: What Could Still Be Preserved

**Status:** Narrative draft 0.1. **Point of view:** Daren Holt, limited third person.

***

The petition arrived bearing the seal of a neighborhood association and an appendix that was much too thick.

Daren Holt received it because his office handled preservation matters connected to municipal investigations. Not because he could order every archive in Eryndor to keep everything.

The association's spokesperson had signed alongside twelve merchants and four workers. They requested a halt to the routine destruction of shift, transport, and correspondence ledgers relating to the recent closures. They also wanted historical records preserved—documents concerning decisions that had restricted corridors before any of them were born.

The second part of the request was much harder.

“What connection do those older records have to the harm you've described?” Daren asked.

The spokesperson, Lena Vard, opened a notebook.

“They tell us we have to endure the closure because something terrible happened once. We want to know whether they made the right decision then, and whether they're making the right decision now.”

Daren did not mistake that legitimate question for sufficient legal grounds. There was a concrete risk that municipal ledgers would be destroyed when their administrative retention periods expired. The historical documentation, however, belonged to different collections and authorities. Neither public anger nor a signature from his office could open them.

“I can receive your petition and establish which municipal records are at risk,” he said. “To seek measures concerning material held by other custodians, we need to identify the series, the reason it should be preserved, and who has the authority to decide.”

Lena pressed her lips together.

“How much time do we have?”

Daren checked the disposal notice.

“For one of the series, less than a week.”

That urgency, at least, could be verified.

***

The municipal depot manager was standing in front of two empty shelving units when Daren arrived.

The transfer of ledgers to a secondary archive had been delayed for lack of space. Some boxes were marked for preservation and others for disposal. The manager showed him the schedule approved months earlier, before the new petition.

“We aren't destroying evidence,” he said. “We're following the normal retention schedule.”

“I haven't said otherwise. I want to check the dates, the criteria, and whether a suspension order exists.”

The inventory described expired shift sheets and duplicate distribution receipts. Some originals were supposed to be preserved by another department; others were the only physical record of a particular operation. The labels did not clearly distinguish between the two.

Daren asked to photograph the condition and location of the boxes, without opening files containing personal information. The manager agreed to an inventoried inspection with an archive witness present.

On a table, they found two folders bearing the same series identifier. One said “reference copies” and the other “originals.” Inside the second were bound ledgers with numbered pages.

“Can these be separated today?” Daren asked.

“Yes, if you send me staff and somewhere to put them. The rooms are full.”

The problem was no longer an abstract debate. Suspending disposal required floor space, working hours, and security. If Daren wanted the measure to be proportionate, he had to specify what should be retained, for how long, and how it would be paid for.

He returned to his office with the inventory, not a box of documents. The originals remained with their custodian.

***

The urgent hearing took place before the competent municipal authority.

The association argued that the shift ledgers could help calculate interrupted services and compensation claims. The municipality agreed to preserve records that were unique, but objected to a blanket suspension covering all archives. A data-protection adviser warned that some records contained the names of workers and recipients of medical supplies.

Daren presented the risk of deletion and explained what he had been able to inspect.

“I propose temporarily preserving three identified series, under a sealed inventory with restricted access. I'm not asking for the association to receive the names, or for the police to examine unrelated historical correspondence.”

The municipality's counsel pointed out that some files might have duplicates in newer systems.

“Let that alternative be recorded,” Daren answered. “The duplicates must be verified before destruction, not used to justify it afterward.”

The authority ordered a halt to disposal of the specified series for a review period, with the originals kept at the depot and an index prepared identifying duplication and sensitive data. The municipality was to provide temporary storage space. The association could consult a redacted inventory and submit observations; it was not granted unrestricted access.

Lena asked for the measure to be extended to Council documents.

The authority explained that the ruling did not cover external collections or override security restrictions. A formal communication could, however, forward the request so that each custodian could consider under its own procedures whether identifiable series needed preservation.

Daren watched Lena's expression. She had hoped for a complete answer and received a partial one.

“Does that achieve anything?” she asked him as they left.

“It keeps three kinds of records from disappearing before they can be assessed. We'll have to request the rest on grounds appropriate to each authority.”

“It sounds small.”

“It's a measure that can actually be carried out.”

***

Late in the day, a notice arrived from the municipal custodian.

Staff had reviewed one of the boxes placed on hold and discovered that its index included not only current distributions but references to messenger ledgers from an old secretariat. The originals from that period weren't there. The catalog pointed to a historical archive under another authority.

Daren read the note beside the report on his still-open judicial case. The investigation into the execution and the clinical trials remained subject to its own safeguards. He could not use that case to seize a historical series simply because both raised questions for him.

The new reference came from an administrative inventory, not a secret statement or a forensic examination in a criminal case. Its value lay in identifying a possible custodian. Nothing more.

He asked his assistant to enter the communication into the neighborhood petition's file and notify the association of the finding's limited scope.

“Shall we send a demand to the historical archive?” she asked.

“A request to locate the records and preserve them preventively under its own rules. Not an order to surrender them.”

The assistant paused.

“What if they say the series is restricted?”

“Then they should identify the custodian and the review procedure. Restricting access to the contents shouldn't erase the obligation to preserve them.”

The message went out with two attachments: the municipal inventory and the limited preservation ruling. Daren included neither photographs of workers nor information from the clinical proceedings.

Lena returned before closing time. She carried a list of new signatures.

“People from other districts,” she said. “They want to join.”

“We don't need everyone to sign the same petition to recognize that they have interests of their own. We need to know which records and which losses they can document.”

She held the paper in silence, then put it away.

“I'll ask them.”

Daren thought the day was over when the depot's final reply arrived. The first box marked for disposal had been immobilized under inventory. The other two were still being checked.

At the foot of the message, the manager had added an observation: a historical transfer ledger was recorded as having been sent to a successor secretariat, but the receiving copy was not in his depot.

It was the sort of gap that might be explained by a legitimate transfer, a loss, or faulty classification.

Daren filed the note without choosing among them.

Someone would have to prove what had been transferred, where it had gone, and whose signature authorized it.

For now, at least, the records that could help answer those questions were still there.

***End of Episode 03.***
