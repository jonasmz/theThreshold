# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 035 · No House to Register

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Orin Farel. **Location:** Port Cendra and the marshes, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/035-sin-casa-que-registrar.md`.

***

The widow arrived at the bank with a door under her arm.

Orin Farel saw her lay the enormous board on the tiled floor. It had a rusted lock, a water mark across the wood at chest height, and a number painted in white.

“This was my home,” she said.

Elira Anet, manager of the Tides Bank, came out of her office. The woman's name was Maira Bel, and she'd applied for an emergency loan to rebuild a net-making workshop along the inland riverbank. Her request had been refused because the municipality couldn't confirm the address of the damaged property.

Maira wasn't disputing the amount. She was disputing the denial that a home she'd lived in for seventeen years had ever existed.

“My husband died four winters ago,” she explained. “Since then I've paid taxes on the workshop and a boat-crossing fee. But the land registry says it's just riverside land with no permanent occupation.”

Orin studied the door. The number belonged to an old system for identifying service huts, used before the canals were reorganized. The municipality had stopped updating it even though people continued to receive mail under those numbers.

“Where is the house?” he asked.

“By Willow Crossing, before the marshes. The water came in on the night of the flood and took my loom, the coal, and the nets belonging to three customers.”

Elira asked for the door to be moved against a wall so it wouldn't obstruct the entrance.

“Have you brought a repair estimate?”

Maira took out a notebook. She'd listed timber, tools, and lost working days. The prices were written with a precision Orin recognized from his cooperative's accounts.

“I don't need anyone to give me a new house,” she said. “I need to buy a table and rope so I can work again.”

Elira studied the figures.

“The bank can consider a trade loan if you can substantiate income and ability to repay. What we can't accept is security over a property whose ownership nobody recognizes.”

Maira pointed at the door.

“Then don't ask me for the house as security. Ask me for work. I have orders if I can get tools.”

Orin felt this wasn't merely a banking dispute. It was the same mistake that had left Liron without a wage receipt: confusing an administrative category with the reality of someone who worked.

***

He accompanied Maira to the municipal assistance office, where Fara Nel had arranged for a claims counter after the dock assembly. The clerk who received them was exhausted and had spent the entire morning writing numbers on forms with no room for explanations.

“Affected address?” he asked.

Maira gave him the door number and Willow Crossing.

The man consulted the register.

“That's listed as a service canal, not a residence.”

“What does it matter what you call it?” Maira asked. “I slept there.”

“It matters for the household repair allocation. We need evidence of occupancy and damage.”

Orin showed him the widow's account book, tax receipts, and two letters mailed to the old address. The clerk examined them.

“They're supporting records, but they aren't enough to amend the land registry.”

“We're not asking you to amend it here,” Orin said. “We want the loss recorded while the facts are checked.”

The man searched for another box.

“I could open a claim for damage to movable property. Tools and nets don't require proof of ownership of the land.”

Maira clenched her jaw.

“And the walls?”

“For the walls, we need a habitability inspection.”

“There are no walls left to inspect,” she replied.

The clerk put down his pen.

Orin realized the word “inspection” had become another locked door. Nobody could inspect the present condition of a house washed away. They could document remains, witnesses, earlier photographs, and mail delivered there, but the form assumed the building would still be standing.

“Let's request a site visit and add statements from neighbors,” he suggested.

“I can take those,” the clerk said. “But immediate aid would cover tools, if approved.”

Maira looked at him without gratitude.

“I didn't lose only tools.”

***

At the dock, Orin brought the claim to the cooperative. Mara Veis knew Willow Crossing because she'd towed timber there before the renovation.

“The house stood on piles,” she recalled. “And it had a platform for drying nets.”

“Could you testify to that?”

“I can say I saw it six months ago. Not that it was unchanged on the night of the accident.”

Maira nodded.

“That's enough. I don't want you making anything up for me.”

Solen searched the old transport receipts. They found a delivery of beams three years earlier, with the door number as its destination. Mir located Maira's signature on a net-repair job for the cooperative.

They were fragments of a commercial life the municipality had received, charged, and filed without acknowledging it as a residence.

Orin felt irritated. He'd been required to prove Good Shadow was still the same boat despite having almost every part replaced. Maira had to prove a house had once existed after losing most of its timber.

The widow returned to the bank with the folder in order.

Elira examined the evidence and called in the trade-loan officer.

“We can't invent a property title,” she said. “We can assess outstanding orders as income and consider limited community security if the cooperative agrees to back it without putting other people's deposits at risk.”

Orin thought of the extraordinary fund the bank had already used to support the tugboats.

“We could guarantee a specific work contract,” he proposed. “We'd have to discuss it with the members.”

Maira took out a net-repair order issued by two skippers. It had been outstanding since before the flood.

“I don't need anyone to pay my debt,” she said. “I need somebody to believe I can fulfill an order.”

Elira laid the order on the table.

“That we can examine.”

***

The site inspection took place two days later. Orin accompanied Maira as a cooperative witness. A municipal technician measured fastening marks on the piles and photographed scraps of timber caught among the roots. The door had been kept separately, its number capable of being checked against old receipts.

Mud covered the ground. No habitable structure remained. Maira found a loom weight and kept it as someone might pick up a stone from the place where a person was buried.

“My husband drove those piles,” she said. “He said the water would never reach this far.”

Orin didn't argue. He'd seen tides rise above levels everyone had regarded as safe. What mattered was establishing how high the flood had reached and why that ground was exposed to a canal officially declared closed.

The technician pointed to a sediment line.

“We can certify that water reached this height. Not when it happened, without other information.”

Maira looked toward the bridge.

“The neighbors saw it that night. Several helped me get my things out.”

The man requested their testimony and recorded the request. He made no promise that the municipality would compensate a property without title.

On their way back they found Talia, the shelter coordinator, beside the walkway. She'd received notice of a territorial inspection intended to update the riverbank occupancy register.

“They're going to count our houses as obstacles to a construction project,” she said. “Not as places where people live.”

Orin read the heading. The project proposed clearing a strip of land to restore the relief canal. It wasn't yet an eviction order, but the drawing classified platforms as “structures with no substantiated use.”

Maira recognized her old door number in a corner.

“That's what they did to me,” she said. “First they stop calling it a home. Then, when it's gone, they say there was nothing to repair.”

***

The public hearing was held in a room in the low-lying district. Orin attended on behalf of the cooperative, and Maira brought the door, although this time she leaned it out of the way.

Fara heard the claim and asked the technician to explain the limits of his report. He confirmed physical damage, the previous presence of piles, and the need for witness statements establishing the flood's date.

Maira spoke afterward.

“My husband died years ago. I carried on working and paying fees. I wasn't listed as the owner of a house, but the municipality could always find my address when it needed to collect money from me.”

Nobody answered.

“If you want to know who was harmed by the flood,” she continued, “ask what each person lost. Not only which box they belonged in before they lost it.”

Fara requested a provisional category for homes with substantiable occupancy but no cadastral registration, and asked that clearance works not proceed through occupied homes before a social and technical assessment. It was neither a declaration of ownership nor a guarantee of compensation. It would allow evidence to be heard before an irreversible decision.

The measure remained subject to administrative approval. Meanwhile, the assistance office authorized limited support for Maira's tools on the strength of verified orders and damage.

Orin walked out into the square with her.

“You haven't got your house back,” he said.

“No,” Maira replied. “But now they have to write down why they say I never had one.”

On the steps, a messenger was posting the new territorial assessment notice.

The plan still showed a canal, a bridge, and a blank strip of land.

Orin looked at the white space where Maira's home had stood.

There were still many people missing from that drawing.
