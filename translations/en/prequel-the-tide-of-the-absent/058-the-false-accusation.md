# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 058 · The False Accusation

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Vela Noren. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/058-la-denuncia-falsa.md`.

***

The headline had been pasted to the tower door before Vela Noren arrived for her shift.

“THE OPERATOR WHO CHANGED THE SIGNAL: WAS THE WARNING THAT COULD HAVE SAVED THE PORT CONCEALED?”

Her full name appeared beneath it, along with a photograph taken during the first inspection of the inner buoy. The article filled two pages of the Estuary Gazette. It claimed that a communications technician had altered the emergency record to make it seem that an order for a scheduled opening had been transmitted before the flood. It cited a reproduction presented in court and referred to a “confirmed receipt” that did not appear in the original.

Vela read the text standing up, without removing her gloves.

It was not true that she had altered the original. She had written an imprecise legend on a working sheet. That sheet had been reproduced outside the dual-control protocol, and the copy had been excluded as independent evidence. The article joined those facts together and turned them into an intention no one had proved.

Peran arrived with two more copies.

“People at the quay are saying you falsified the signal.”

“Who is saying that?”

“Everyone who read the headline.”

Vela folded the newspaper in half.

“Then today I won't be able to explain anything to everyone.”

***

The tower's acting director asked her to surrender her operating credential while the copying incident was reviewed. It was not a formal sanction. The administration wanted to prevent the signal operator from taking part in new readings that might enter the case file before the internal audit.

Vela agreed to hand over the credential and requested a written statement defining the scope of the measure. She did not want a precautionary suspension to become a declaration of guilt later.

“I can't assign you to the main console,” the director said. “You can help inventory the records and prepare the technical correction.”

“Can I review the originals under supervision?”

“When the court authorizes it.”

Vela handed over her credential. In the corridor, two operators stopped talking when she passed. One had worked with her for six years and did not greet her.

In Amiel's office, the newspaper was already open on the desk.

“I'm not going to ask them for an immediate correction,” he said. “First we have to separate what is false from what actually happened.”

Vela sat down.

“It is false that I changed the original.”

“We can support that with the chain of custody and the laboratory report. The disputed copy is a working sheet, not the original medium.”

“It is also false that the phrase proves the warning reached the external channel.”

“Yes. The technical report says the opposite: there is no verifiable final acknowledgment.”

“Then why don't we say so?”

Amiel pointed to the article's second paragraph.

“Because it also claims someone ordered the press to use that copy. We don't know who gave it to them, and we have no grounds to accuse the newspaper of inventing the document.”

Vela felt that caution was beginning to look like a closed door again.

“They can publish my name, and we can't answer.”

“We can answer with facts. We can't answer with a theory about who leaked them.”

***

The story spread through the market before noon. A group of relatives had brought the newspaper to the square. One of them asked Orin whether the cooperative still supported the signal operator.

“We support investigating the record,” the captain replied. “We have not claimed she falsified anything.”

“But she worked with you.”

“And she also testified about what she didn't know.”

The man showed him the headline.

“So this is a lie?”

Orin read the article. He recognized the photograph and the fragment of the working sheet he had seen during the hearing.

“I don't know who wrote the headline. I know the copy was challenged and the original remains in custody.”

“That doesn't answer the question.”

“It's what I can answer without making something up.”

The man walked away with the newspaper clenched in his hand. Orin stayed beside the fish stall, aware that every qualification could look like a retreat.

At the tower, Vela found an envelope with no return address. Inside was a copy of the disputed reproduction and a note: “If you want the truth to come out, say who told you to write it.”

There was no signature. Nor was there an explicit threat.

She handed it to Amiel, touching only the edges. The inspector ordered its receipt to be recorded but did not add it to the case evidence until its origin could be verified.

“Do you think someone from Mareval sent it?” Vela asked.

“I don't know.”

“An employee from the tower?”

“Neither do I.”

“Then what do we do?”

“We don't attribute it to anyone until we can.”

Vela looked at the envelope. The investigation seemed to have become a machine that accepted answers only after the damage had already been done.

***

That afternoon, the journalist who had signed the article arrived at the tower. Her name was Leda Var, and she carried a small notebook. She asked to speak with Vela and the acting director.

“Is it true you wrote ‘receipt confirmed by central’?” she asked.

“Yes, on a working sheet.”

“Why?”

“Because I confused an intermediate acknowledgment with final receipt. The phrase was wrong.”

“Isn't that altering a record?”

“No. The original was not modified. The working sheet was a derived analysis, and the copy was reproduced without the required controls.”

Leda took notes.

“Do you admit, then, that the evidence was manipulated?”

“I admit that the copy was defective and my annotation was imprecise. I can't claim that anyone deliberately tampered with it.”

“And who leaked it?”

“I don't know.”

The journalist put away her pencil.

“The article says you changed the signal to protect someone.”

“There is no evidence of that.”

“Can you prove you didn't?”

Vela felt the question was asking her to prove an absence.

“The original has its own chain of custody. The laboratory can compare the copy with the medium. That shows the original does not contain my legend. I can't prove what someone was thinking when they published the article.”

Leda watched her for a moment.

“Will you give me a written statement?”

“When it has been reviewed by the court and the technical authority.”

“Tomorrow's edition closes in two hours.”

“Then you'll have to publish what you can already verify.”

The journalist did not promise to do so.

***

That evening, Vela read the laboratory report in the archive room. The original medium retained its identification marks and showed no signs of added writing. The excluded reproduction had a cut-off edge and a typed legend that did not appear in the source. The report did not identify who had used the copier.

Amiel approached with the request to review the precautionary measure.

“Management wants to know whether we can certify that there was no intentional alteration.”

“We can't certify an intention.”

“That's what I said.”

“We can certify the condition of the original, the difference between the documents, and the error in my annotation.”

Amiel nodded.

“Tomorrow the magistrate will receive the report. If we submit it, the discussion about the copy may be reopened.”

“Let it be reopened.”

Vela looked at the credential she had left on the table. She did not know how long it would take to get it back or whether she would operate the main console again.

Before leaving, she passed the tower door. The headline was still pasted there. Someone had drawn a black line through her surname.

She was not bothered that they had crossed out her name. She was bothered that the line did not touch any of the false statements.

She pulled the newspaper from the frame, folded it, and took it to the archive as a record of publication. She did not want to destroy it. Nor did she want to leave it on the door so it could keep speaking in her place.

In the margin of her notebook, she wrote a sentence for the next day's statement: “The annotation was mine. The published interpretation is not supported by the original.”

The sentence did not absolve her.

It was the beginning of a correction she would have to make in public.
