# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 042 · The Man Who Never Left

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Amiel Rusk. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/042-quien-nunca-salio.md`.

***

Baret Luno's name appeared on the passenger list of a company that claimed to have transported him out of Port Cendra.

It also appeared in the municipal hospital's death register.

Amiel Rusk placed the copies side by side without allowing the match to settle the case on its own. He'd learned that two incompatible records could refer to different people, a miscopied date, or an employee who had filled out forms after the events.

“What do we know about the deceased?” he asked Daren.

“He was a grain loader. Thirty-nine years old. He was admitted with injuries during the emergency and died at dawn the following day.”

“Who confirmed his identity?”

“A brother and the clinical shift supervisor. We have references to the certificate, but not the full file.”

Amiel picked up the desk phone and requested a certified copy of the relevant civil and temporal details from the hospital. He didn't ask for diagnoses or medical histories unrelated to the investigation. Magistrate Yuna Tor had already authorized a limited comparison to determine whether the transport services that had been invoiced corresponded to real people.

On Estuary Cargo's form, Baret was listed as the beneficiary of transport, accommodation, and a meal ration. His scheduled departure was the morning after the flood, at six fifty. The train had been delayed, and the company was using a reservation receipt as proof that the service had been committed.

At the hospital, the register recorded his death at four eighteen that same morning.

If the times were correct and this was the same man, Baret couldn't have used the ticket after he died.

There was still a need to establish that the company had charged for the journey as though he'd actually traveled.

***

The deceased man's brother was named Taren Luno. He repaired windows for a living and arrived at the courthouse with his hat in his hands.

“I don't want to go over how he died again,” he said before sitting down.

Amiel left his folder closed.

“We don't need to ask you for clinical details. We want to confirm his identity and find out whether you know of anyone arranging a transfer in your brother's name.”

Taren nodded.

He explained that he'd gone to the hospital after hearing about the injury. He recognized Baret by an old scar on his eyebrow and a burn mark on his wrist. He'd accompanied the shift supervisor during the certification of death and signed as a family member.

“We buried him two days later,” he said. “The cemetery kept the burial receipt.”

“Were you ever asked to authorize a rail transfer?” Amiel asked.

“No. They offered us a death assistance form. We never talked about trains.”

Taren took out a crumpled paper. It was the municipal application for funeral assistance, with a case number different from the one used by Estuary Cargo.

“They told me they couldn't pay until they knew whether Baret was a registered worker,” he added. “But someone had already put his name on a passenger list.”

Amiel asked permission to copy the form. The paradox was cruel: one office was withholding assistance for a documented burial while another was preparing payments for an impossible journey.

“Could there be another Baret Luno?” he asked.

His brother looked at him without taking offense.

“Could be. Ours isn't a unique surname. But the crew number on that ticket belonged to my brother.”

Amiel compared the detail. The matching name and crew number narrowed the possibilities, though he still had to check the source of that number in the original register.

“We'll document it,” he replied.

Taren placed his hat on his lap.

“Document, too, that he had a name before he had a debt.”

***

At the hospital, the clinical supervisor, Sira Belven, had sent a registry colleague to deliver the authorized extract. The professional's name was Elva Ren, and she knew the procedure for identifying bodies when an emergency overwhelmed the shifts.

“The certificate was issued after two independent identifications,” she explained. “A family member and a nurse identified the patient. In addition, the admission wristband and clinical number match the intake register.”

Amiel checked the seals. The extract gave the time of death and the time of the later entry, which weren't identical. The death was recorded at four eighteen; the final entry was written an hour later.

“Had the hospital clock been calibrated?” he asked.

Elva showed him the calibration sheet. During the emergency it had run two minutes ahead of the municipal standard. The documented deviation wasn't enough to account for the difference between the time of death and the scheduled train departure.

“Have the original documents been preserved?” he asked.

“In the clinical and civil archives, under separate records. This extract doesn't authorize disclosure of the medical history.”

The inspector accepted that limitation. A strong identification didn't require exposing the deceased man's illness or injuries to the entire city.

He then visited the cemetery. The burial register listed Baret Luno, identified the responsible family member, and gave a date after the death. An employee showed him the original entry and the records for transferring the body from the hospital.

There was no need to exhume anyone to verify a documentary discrepancy already supported by multiple sources.

Amiel requested a certified copy and returned to the courthouse.

***

The cargo company responded to the request through Silven Rea. He admitted that it had issued the ticket in Baret Luno's name, but argued that it was a contingency reservation and shouldn't be interpreted as proof of boarding.

“Did you invoice for transport actually provided?” Amiel asked.

Silven opened a spreadsheet.

“We submitted a provisional settlement. The services actually provided were supposed to be reconciled afterward.”

“Where is the adjustment?”

The representative searched through his documents.

“The contracts office hasn't added it yet.”

“Did you receive an advance against that line item?”

Silven took a moment to answer.

“The municipality advanced funds for the operation as a whole.”

That wasn't yet an admission of improper billing. A company could receive advances and return the portion that went unused. The question was whether there was an auditable account and who had certified performance.

Amiel requested those documents without asking that the wages of unrelated workers be frozen.

Before leaving, Silven made an observation.

“Relief systems are prepared before anyone knows which patients will survive. Names inevitably appear for people who don't end up traveling.”

“Making a reservation is legitimate,” Amiel replied. “Claiming that it was carried out and demanding the corresponding payment requires different evidence.”

The man didn't argue.

***

Yuna Tor received the case file with three independent sources: hospital identification, a family statement, and the burial record. She added the railway document, the crew number, and the request for provisional settlement.

“We can conclude that the worker identified as Baret Luno died in Port Cendra before the scheduled departure,” she said. “We cannot conclude that the other ten people met the same fate.”

“Nor that every invoice line is fraudulent,” Amiel added.

The magistrate nodded.

She ordered the contracting office to review performance of Baret's service, preserve all versions of the spreadsheet, and establish who had approved the advance. The request didn't declare the company guilty or allow documents already subject to preservation to be silently corrected.

Amiel thought of Taren, who had requested funeral assistance and received nothing.

“Can we forward the certificate to the social assistance program?” he asked.

“With the family member's consent or under the service's lawful authority. Don't disclose more information than necessary.”

The inspector called Taren and explained that the court had corroborated the identity. He told him how to request a review of the assistance application without having to give another lengthy statement.

His brother fell silent.

“Will you find the others?” he finally asked.

“We're still looking. Baret's case lets us verify one entry, not resolve them all.”

Taren drew a deep breath.

“Then don't use him to say they all died.”

Amiel promised only what he could deliver: his report would keep each case separate.

That night, as he closed the file, he found a note from the transport office. Another identity was marked “service completed” without a receipt signature, but in that case there was no death certificate or record of arrival.

The investigation could prove that one man had never left the city.

The others were still people, not gaps to be filled with the same answer.
