# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 017 · The Accountant's House

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Breda Lin. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/017-la-casa-del-contador.md`.

***

Breda Lin found the accountant's address in a notebook she had promised to destroy at the end of her first year with the Mutual.

The notebook had green covers and a printed calendar that no longer corresponded to any office. In it she had written down suppliers' names, changes in rates, and observations that had no place in official reports. On the final page was the name Calvor Edren, former head of account reconciliation at Estuary Cargo.

Beside the name she'd written: “Do not accept summary statements. Request general ledger.”

She couldn't remember why she'd thought that warning necessary.

Nimo found her by the window with the notebook open.

“Another reply from the port registry has arrived,” he said. “One of the insured vessels changed owners after it had been declared scrapped.”

Breda checked the date and frowned.

“The transfer might have been regularized later. We need the registration file and the shipyard documents, not just the index.”

“The director asked us not to submit any more requests until his meeting is over.”

“What meeting?”

“With Mareval's broker.”

The Mutual's director had spent two days telling her not to disclose her suspicions about the policies. Now he intended to prevent her from gathering the documents that would confirm or disprove them.

Breda closed her notebook.

“I'm going to ask him in writing whether he's removing me from the audit.”

“And if he does?”

“Then someone will have to sign a record that he made that decision.”

Nimo looked as though he might add something, but returned to his desk. Breda drafted a brief note. She described the seven policies, the two questionable hull numbers, and the need to examine the broker's original ledgers. She did not use the word fraud. She requested access or formal suspension of the review.

The director replied before noon. He did not authorize further external inspections and ordered the case file kept in preliminary status.

Breda read the instruction twice. It didn't order her to destroy evidence, but it did prevent her from examining it while advance compensation payments were being discussed.

She could lodge an internal protest. She could go to the insurance supervisory authority and explain that the decision was impairing the Mutual's ability to assess claims. What she couldn't do was secretly take someone else's documents.

She thought of Calvor Edren.

Visiting a former accountant required no court order if the man agreed to talk. And he might be able to explain the meaning of the note she'd written years earlier.

***

Calvor's house stood beside a soap factory, on a street where the windows seemed to shut themselves against the smell. It had a blue door, a lemon tree in a narrow courtyard, and a bell that didn't work. Breda knocked with her knuckles.

A woman wearing a flour-stained apron opened the door.

“Mr. Edren doesn't work for any company anymore,” she said when Breda gave his name.

“I'm not here to hire him. I'd like to ask about some old accounting procedures.”

“That usually ends up being work too.”

Breda accepted that she might not be welcome. She left her card with an explanation of her visit and was about to go when a voice called from the courtyard.

Calvor Edren appeared leaning on a cane. He looked over sixty and wore his sleeves rolled up as though he'd been washing something.

“I remember you,” he said. “You were the one who kept asking where all those round sums came from.”

Breda smiled without amusement.

“I still ask.”

He invited her inside.

The house was full of household ledgers: coal expenses, flour purchases, little accounts with the neighbors. Everything was meticulously ordered, and Breda recognized the habit immediately. Calvor sat opposite her and placed his cane between his knees.

“You want to talk about Estuary Cargo?” he asked.

“I want to understand how you reconciled payments to temporary crews.”

“The company charged for services. Foremen distributed cash, deducted advances, and returned any remainder to the treasury.”

“And the general ledger?”

Calvor gave a dry laugh.

“Ah. That was the problem.”

He explained that the company kept two legitimate sets of accounts for different purposes. A commercial ledger grouped services by client. A distribution ledger recorded wage envelopes and the people authorized to collect them. Any differences had to be reconciled every week.

“If you look only at the commercial ledger, you'll see that the money went out,” he said. “You won't know whether it reached the worker.”

“Where did you keep the distribution ledger?”

“In the treasury archive. At least, that's where it was when I worked there.”

“Why did you leave?”

Calvor looked toward his wife, who had set two cups on the table and was walking away without joining the conversation.

“I refused to close a week with unsigned receipts. The manager accused me of delaying payments. Perhaps he was right about the delay. Not about the receipts.”

Breda recognized the sentence in her notebook: do not accept summary statements.

“Did you keep copies of those books?” she asked.

“No. The accounts of my house belonged to me. The company's did not.”

The answer both relieved and disappointed her. She wouldn't have to decide what to do with stolen documents, but she wouldn't leave with the proof she'd imagined either.

Instead, Calvor showed her a blank form he'd kept as a professional reference. It contained no client information. It laid out the sequence: paying depot, intermediary company, distribution cash desk, and recipient's signature. Each step had its own entry number.

“If there's a reserve called RC-7,” Breda said, “what should that mean?”

“There isn't a universal abbreviation. Request the chart of accounts for that year.”

“And if they refuse to give it to me?”

“Ask who has the authority to refuse, and insist they put it in writing.”

Breda had the uncomfortable impression that she was hearing her own words spoken by someone who had paid a price for following them before she had.

***

On her return, she found the director waiting in her office. Nimo had left every folder closed.

“I know you visited a former employee of a client,” the man said.

“I consulted him about general procedures. I received no confidential documents, and I disclosed no information about our policies.”

“You're exceeding your role.”

“I asked you in writing to tell me what my current role is. I haven't received a formal notice of removal.”

The director rested one hand on the filing cabinet.

“Mareval is threatening to withdraw its entire portfolio if we continue withholding payments. That would leave small businesses without coverage because they depend on our reinsurer.”

Breda hadn't expected that explanation. The risk was real. The Mutual relied on the income from large clients to insure vessels no individual company would take on.

“I don't want to destroy the Mutual,” she said. “I want to stop it paying for a boat whose existence we haven't verified.”

“And I don't want a client to sue us before your investigation is complete.”

“Then suspend those advances for a limited period and appoint another independent auditor. We can't resolve the tension by pretending the ledgers don't exist.”

The director fell silent. Then he opened his drawer and took out an internal instruction.

It temporarily removed her authority to request external documents. The review was transferred to a three-person committee appointed by him.

Breda read the document. The decision was signed and dated. At least the boundary was visible.

“I request a copy,” she said.

“You'll get one.”

“And I'll register a professional reservation concerning the outstanding policies.”

He didn't stop her.

That afternoon, Breda returned to her desk and prepared a technical statement for the audit archive. It set out what she'd observed, which alternative hypotheses remained open, and which documents she had been prevented from examining. She attached the register of her rejected requests.

When Nimo approached with the copies, he had news.

“The committee asked Estuary Cargo for the general ledger.”

Breda felt cautious relief.

“Did they hand it over?”

“They replied that the original isn't at the office. They say it was transferred to the accountant's house for an old review.”

Breda recalled Calvor's domestic shelves, full of expenses for flour and coal. He'd said he kept no company documents.

She had no reason to conclude he was lying. The term “accountant's house” might refer to an archive designation, another person, or an address long since abandoned.

She took the transfer form.

The destination was a property different from the home she'd just visited.

In the margin was a delivery stamp that remained valid.

Breda wrote down the address in her consultation register without removing the document from the office.

The house she needed to find wasn't Calvor's.
