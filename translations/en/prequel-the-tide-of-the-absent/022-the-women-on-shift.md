# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 022 · The Women on Shift

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Neria Calden. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/022-las-mujeres-del-turno.md`.

***

Irsa had never considered that a pencil mark could cost another woman her right to be paid.

Neria Calden explained it to her in the maintenance changing room while the women on the early shift gathered outside. They'd requested a meeting without subcontracting supervisors or guards. Some worked on floodgates; others tended boilers, warehouse shifts, and pumping stations.

“I'm not looking for the person who wrote fastest in the ledger,” Neria said. “I'm looking for whoever was actually beside each machine and can describe what she saw.”

Irsa opened a locker and took out a small notebook.

“Then you'll have to accept names that don't appear on the official reports.”

It wasn't an official register. It contained schedules, valve sketches, recipes, and marks that looked like household accounts. Someone had written “Shared Rounds” on the cover.

Neria recognized Irsa's handwriting. The woman habitually noted mechanical anomalies before reporting them to the station chief. For years they had relied on her notes, but she'd never been permitted to replace a signed report with them.

“What are these marks?” she asked.

“Swaps between us. When one woman couldn't make it, another covered part of her shift.”

“Did the supervisor authorize them?”

“Some. Others happened when there was no way to reach him.”

The door opened and Leda Sorn entered, a pump operator with a scar on her chin. Behind her came Fira Den, who cleaned storage tanks, and Maren Koss, a widow who worked as an auxiliary signaler. Sharing a surname with the communications director didn't mean Maren was related to him; she hurried to make that clear before anyone could ask.

Neria offered them water.

“I want to hear what happened, not impose an explanation. If you're worried about retaliation, we can keep the technical notes separate from the identifying statements until there's a protected process.”

Leda pointed to the official shift ledger.

“That says I operated the north pump all night during the flood.”

“Didn't you?”

“I was there until midnight. Fira came afterward because my daughter was sick.”

Fira raised a hand.

“I tended the pump for two hours. Then I was sent to the dike because the floodgate crew was short a worker.”

Neria glanced at Irsa. The operator didn't seem surprised.

“Who sent you?” she asked.

“A Mareval foreman. He said I had to cover a technical cleaning position.”

Fira knew how to clean filters and bleed pipes. She hadn't been fully trained to work on a retainer under pressure. If she'd been near a control during the emergency, her testimony might explain who saw the instruments at that moment.

“What did you do when you arrived?” Neria asked.

“I didn't go into the room. They sent me to the outside duct with a bucket and a wrench.”

Neria remembered the adjustment box accessible from the neighboring warehouse. She didn't want to turn a sentence into an indication of guilt.

“What exactly were you told to clean?”

Fira shrugged.

“An oil leak. Two men were adjusting something behind the wall. I didn't see their faces.”

***

The meeting moved into the courtyard so women on watch could come in by turns without leaving the installations unattended.

Maren explained the substitution system. Official positions belonged to people who'd passed examinations, paid their certification fees, and appeared in the municipal register. Auxiliaries could work under contract to a service company, but their names didn't always appear on the stations' operating sheets.

When a registered operator had to be absent, an auxiliary covered permitted tasks, and the regular operator made up the hours later. If the supervisor approved the exchange, it was recorded. Otherwise, the report continued to show the regular operator.

“We didn't do it to deceive the city,” Maren said. “We did it because the city demanded impossible shifts.”

Neria knew the problem. She'd spent months requesting more reserve personnel and had always received the same answer: insufficient budget.

“I can't say all those swaps were safe,” she replied. “A dangerous operation requires training and someone identified as responsible.”

“And who trains a woman they won't even allow into the register?” Fira asked.

There was no answer that wouldn't sound like an excuse.

Irsa placed an old shift sheet on the table. It bore one name in blue ink and another penciled in the margin. The second belonged to an operator who'd covered six hours without receiving the night-work supplement.

“The money was paid to the official name,” she explained. “We settled up among ourselves afterward.”

“Did they always hand over the difference?” Neria asked.

The silence said more than an accusation.

Leda spoke first.

“Some women shared everything. Others said the deductions were for insurance or the common fund.”

“Could you identify those payments?” Neria asked.

“From our notebooks, yes. Not from the company's receipts.”

Neria thought of Liron and his crew's filed-down token. Different systems could produce the same result: money acknowledged in an account but never delivered to the person who had worked.

She asked permission to photograph the technical notes. She didn't demand the entire notebooks; they contained family addresses, household expenses, and information irrelevant to the case. Each woman chose the pages she wished to share and signed a record of what she had provided.

***

The most important piece wasn't a name but a time.

Maren had covered signaling at the west dock on the day of the emergency. The official report listed another operator, now retired, whose certification had remained active during a transition month. Maren hadn't touched the central panel, but she'd received calls from crews working near the floodgate.

“At half past one, we were told to finish clearing the canal,” she recalled. “There was no mention of a storm.”

Neria leaned toward her.

“Who called?”

“An operator on the freight line. He didn't give his name.”

“Is it written down anywhere?”

Maren took out a notebook using the format of the old stations. She'd recorded a line number and an instruction: “Clear service route. Hold lighter until further notice.”

The words didn't match the evacuation order Neria had received. Nor did they prove someone had anticipated the opening that caused the flood.

“Why didn't you hand this over before?” Neria asked.

Maren ran a finger along the notebook's edge.

“Because my name wasn't on the shift. I thought if I testified, they'd penalize me and then say my testimony was worthless.”

Neria felt ashamed. She'd demanded accurate records since the first night without considering how many people made their living through records that erased them.

“You can testify to what you heard,” she said. “Your employment situation must be examined separately. I won't promise there will be no consequences, but I won't ask you to lie to keep your job.”

Fira looked toward the door.

“And if they fire us?”

“We can request preventive measures and employment advice. We need to document who decided each substitution and who benefited from it.”

Irsa closed her notebook.

“Then don't start with the operators' names. Start with the people who approved the reports knowing they didn't match the rounds.”

The words hung in the air.

***

That afternoon Neria went to the courthouse with certified copies of the pages the women had authorized her to share. Amiel received the sheets and asked her to distinguish direct observations from memories discussed during the meeting.

“I don't want it to look as though they all saw the same thing,” he said.

“They didn't. They were in different places, and not everything they remember agrees.”

“That doesn't invalidate their statements.”

Neria handed him Maren's notebook inside a protective sleeve, without taking the original away from its owner. The inspector recorded its provenance and promised to summon her with safeguards for her personal information.

Before leaving, Neria stopped at the personnel office. She submitted a request to review auxiliary certifications, establish recorded substitutions, and ensure that no worker without training was left responsible for a critical control.

The official said the proposal would cost money.

“So does a false shift report,” Neria replied. “That expense simply never appears in your budget.”

When she returned to the changing room, Irsa was waiting with her rounds notebook.

“There's a line I didn't show you,” she said.

It was an entry from the night of the accident. Fira had returned from the eastern station before the first siren and written a single word beside the time.

“Test.”

Neria remembered the request to test the repeater that Vela had found soaked in water. She didn't know whether the two referred to the same activity.

But two women, in different posts and without being properly listed on the shifts, had recorded an operation that nobody acknowledged in the official reports.
