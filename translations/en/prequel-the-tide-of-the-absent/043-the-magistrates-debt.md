# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 043 · The Magistrate's Debt

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Yuna Tor. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/043-la-deuda-de-la-magistrada.md`.

***

Yuna Tor recognized her signature on a document she hadn't opened in twelve years.

She'd signed it during an outbreak of port fever, when hospitals couldn't wait for every person to find a birth certificate before receiving food and treatment. At the time, she'd authorized relief offices to use provisional identities and certify services in groups, provided they later kept the individual receipts.

The measure had saved time. It had also created a phrase that now appeared in Port Cendra's disputed contracts: “Consolidated list of beneficiaries pending regularization.”

The president of the Civic Council had sent a four-page letter to her office. He argued that the magistrate herself had recognized, in the old precedent, that an emergency could justify payments without named receipts. Therefore, he requested that the preservation order on Estuary Cargo's records be lifted and all allocations released.

Yuna laid the letter on her desk.

She couldn't deny that the precedent bore her signature. But neither could she accept that it meant what the Council claimed.

Her earlier ruling had allowed people to be helped first and the records to be completed afterward. It hadn't authorized anyone to declare services performed when nobody knew whether they had taken place.

The courtroom clerk, Rovel, was waiting by the door.

“Should I prepare a response?” he asked.

“Prepare a hearing. I want the parties to read the entire precedent, not just the paragraph that suits them.”

“Wouldn't it be better for another magistrate to review it, since you issued the original ruling?”

Yuna considered the question. The law required recusal when there was a direct personal interest, an incompatible relationship, or prejudgment of the case. Having issued a public ruling applicable to later situations wasn't, by itself, automatic grounds for disqualification.

“I'll disclose my role in that precedent,” she said. “The parties can raise objections under the procedure. I'm not going to hide the history of the rule.”

Rovel noted the instruction.

***

Before the hearing, Yuna visited the judicial archive. She searched for the original epidemic case file and found the conditions she remembered, written on a page almost no one cited: provisional assistance had to be reconciled individually within thirty days, and discrepancies that couldn't be substantiated could not be converted into final payments.

The archive also held a closing report. Not every office had met the deadline. Some took months; others had to return advances. A community kitchen had submitted records of hundreds of meals served without a list of diners, but with purchase receipts, cooks' shift records, and distribution witnesses.

Yuna remembered the woman who ran that kitchen. She'd stormed into the court because officials demanded the names of orphaned children before acknowledging that the children had been fed.

The court's decision had accepted material proof of service without requiring a public register.

It was an imperfect solution, but not permission to invent recipients.

Yuna requested certified copies of the conditions and the closing report. She didn't want to rely on personal memories that time might have distorted.

When she returned to her office, she found an unsealed letter.

“You owe us the right to work as you let us work back then,” it said.

There was no signature. Someone had slipped it under the door. It might have been a desperate request, an attempt at pressure, or a provocation.

Yuna ordered it preserved as correspondence received, not as evidence of a criminal threat. The word *owe* concerned her more than the anonymity.

The city was turning an old responsibility into a debt it could collect.

***

The hearing brought together municipal representatives, Estuary Cargo's lawyers, family members, and a court-appointed expert. The room was too small, and Rovel had to open an adjoining room so people could follow the proceedings.

The company's lawyer spoke first.

“We request that the emergency standard established in the port fever case be applied. The services were provided under exceptional conditions.”

Yuna asked him to be specific.

“Which services, exactly, do you want deemed performed without further verification?”

“Transport, accommodation, and ration distribution.”

“Do you have vehicle departure logs, recipient identification, or alternative delivery receipts?”

The lawyer submitted a consolidated spreadsheet.

“This is the documentation generated by the contractor.”

“Then it documents that the contractor declared a service. It does not yet establish that each recipient received it.”

Fara Nel spoke on behalf of the Council. He explained that some expenses had to be paid to prevent interruptions to food supplies and repairs. Yuna didn't deny it. There were invoices for deliveries that had been confirmed and could continue to be settled.

“I haven't suspended all payments,” she clarified. “I've ordered that completed services, items awaiting documentation, and contradictory records be distinguished. An interim measure must not destroy the aid it is meant to protect.”

The families' advocate presented the case of Baret Luno. His death in Port Cendra before the scheduled departure had been corroborated by independent documents, even though he appeared in a transport plan.

“This shows the risk of using a reservation as proof of travel,” she said.

The company's lawyer argued that the spreadsheets could still be corrected.

“Of course,” Yuna replied. “Every correction must preserve its date, author, and relationship to the previous version. The court doesn't prohibit corrections. It prohibits silently replacing the evidence of what was declared.”

Nobody applauded. The magistrate was grateful for the silence.

***

During the recess, a senior councilor asked to speak with Yuna alone. He'd served on the health commission during the old epidemic and had known her for years.

“Your ruling back then was courageous,” he said. “Don't let them use it now to bring the city to a standstill.”

Yuna invited him to submit a formal statement if he had relevant information.

“There's no need to turn every conversation into a record,” he protested.

“When you're asking me to decide a matter involving public money in an open proceeding, there is.”

The man lowered his voice.

“There are families who depend on the consortium. If we strangle it, they'll lose their income.”

“That must be considered when designing proportionate measures. It doesn't change what evidence proves a payment.”

The councilor left, displeased.

Yuna stood by the window. She didn't feel immune to concern for those jobs. She'd seen courts issue orders so broad that they ended up punishing people unrelated to the dispute. Her duty wasn't to reject every risk; it was to justify why a measure was necessary and where its limits lay.

She returned to the hearing room without granting that conversation the privilege of having taken place outside the proceedings. She ordered a note of the visit and its general substance added to the record, so the parties could know about it.

***

When the hearing resumed, Yuna read out the conditions of her old ruling.

She explained that alternative evidence could be sufficient when a recipient couldn't be identified without exposing them improperly, provided there were quantities, dates, responsible parties, and independent checks. She also explained that using a provisional name didn't authorize anyone to forge a signature or certify journeys that hadn't taken place.

Rovel received instructions to prepare a decision divided by service.

Rations with verified delivery would continue to be paid. Outstanding discrepancies could be checked through ledgers, witnesses, and quantity controls, without publishing residents' identities. Alleged transfers of people would require proof of departure, receipt, or equivalent records. The original documents would remain preserved.

The magistrate also ordered an independent assistance office to process the immediate needs of families whose payments were under dispute, so evidentiary conflicts wouldn't suspend essential care.

The company's lawyer announced that he would appeal part of the decision. He had the right to do so.

“The appeal will be recorded,” Yuna replied. “In the meantime, the preservation obligations remain in force, unless a competent authority rules otherwise.”

As the hearing ended, Amiel approached with a request for access to the railway documentation. There were new discrepancies between reservations and boardings, and Vela's inspection might help clarify them.

Yuna read the request.

“Give me the independent sources supporting it,” she said. “I won't rely on the selector whose chain of custody was interrupted.”

Amiel showed her the certified waybills and station entries. She authorized a limited comparison.

***

At dusk, Yuna returned to the epidemic case file. On the last page was a note in her own hand, written twelve years earlier: “Urgency does not lessen the obligation to remember whom we are helping.”

She didn't remember writing it. For a long time, she'd believed her debt to that crisis was to defend the flexibility that had made it possible to save lives.

Now she understood the other half.

A rule created to keep vulnerable people from being left out could also become a way to erase the difference between a person and an accounting entry.

The magistrate closed the file.

She couldn't repair every decision made under her old standard. She could prevent her signature from being used as permission to verify nothing at all.
