# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 003 · The Men at the Dike

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Neria Calden, with a marked scene break. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/003-los-hombres-del-dique.md`.

***

The riverside shelter had two doors: one for people and one for goods.

Neria Calden stopped in front of the first. The letters painted over the arch still read MUNICIPAL RELIEF, although a board nailed beneath them announced that the building had been leased to a fish-curing company. A cat slept on the steps, indifferent to the mud left by the flood.

“Eleven people,” Amiel Rusk said.

Neria didn't know whether the inspector was speaking to her or to the officer accompanying them.

“Eleven names,” she corrected. “We haven't found the people.”

“That distinction is precisely why we're here.”

The officer knocked three times. No one answered.

It was almost eight. In the street, a vendor was arguing with a woman who wanted bread on credit. Above their voices came the noise of the sawmills, which hadn't stopped even during the alarm.

Amiel motioned for the officer to wait.

“We can't force open the door of a commercial property without a warrant,” he said. “We need to identify the tenant and request access.”

Neria pointed toward a side window. A curtain had been rolled up, revealing a row of benches behind it. Someone had removed the beds from the old shelter.

“What if there are people locked inside?”

Amiel studied the window.

“Do you see any signs of distress?”

“No.”

“Then don't turn a suspicion into an emergency just because it would be convenient to go in.”

The answer annoyed her because it was reasonable. Neria moved away toward the dock, where a small pontoon carried employees back and forth across the canal. The water had dropped almost to its usual level. Even so, a mud line remained as high as the shelter's door hinges. The flood had reached this street.

A boy was pulling a rope attached to a basket. He looked about seventeen and wore an oversized green jacket. When he saw Neria, he hid his right hand in his pocket.

“Do you work here?” she asked.

“Depends on what you want to buy.”

“I don't want to buy anything. There was an evacuation to this building last night.”

The boy looked at the sign.

“They haven't taken evacuees here in months.”

“Who was on duty?”

“I'm not allowed to talk about work.”

Neria felt a familiar irritation. The same answer, delivered in different uniforms, was beginning to surround her everywhere.

“What's your name?”

“Jaro.”

“Jaro what?”

“Jaro Velk. Are you from insurance?”

“I'm the maintenance supervisor at the eastern dike.”

The boy's expression changed.

“You opened the gate.”

“Yes.”

“My brother was on the lighter.”

Neria stopped looking at the shelter door.

“Is he all right?”

“They pulled him out of the water. He gashed his leg on a plank, and he's waiting for someone to sign him into the clinic. He's not on the shift roster.”

“Who hired him?”

“A cargo foreman, not maintenance.”

“Then why was he beside the gate?”

Jaro looked away. Neria realized he was trying to calculate how much he might lose by telling the truth.

“They pay us to clear sediment,” he said at last. “Before the tide turns. If too much mud collects at the entrance, boats collide.”

“That work requires authorization from my department.”

“They told us they already had it.”

Neria breathed slowly. Eight people worked under her authority. But the port employed many more people around its facilities: stevedores, boatmen, cleaners, workers from auxiliary companies. A man could be in danger inside her safety zone and never appear on her shift roster.

“How many of you were there last night?”

“Four on the lighter. Two managed to jump.”

“And the others?”

“My brother and the skipper. The skipper disappeared when the current came.”

For a moment, Neria felt the sounds of the street recede.

The municipal report listed two provisionally missing people. She had assumed the number referred to people whose names they didn't yet know. Now one of them had a relationship, a job, a vessel, and someone waiting for him to return.

“I need you to speak to the inspector,” she said.

Jaro shook his head.

“If I do, they won't pay me for the week.”

“If you don't, they can say your brother was never there.”

She recognized the cruelty of the words as soon as she spoke them. But Jaro had already heard. He turned toward the basket and began winding up the rope.

“Give me a minute,” he said.

***

The shelter's tenant arrived shortly before nine with a ring of keys and a perfectly practiced look of indignation.

His name was Heskan Dorr. He wore his hair combed back and the coat of a man who never had to carry sacks to earn his living from the salt trade.

“This property is under private contract,” he announced. “I won't tolerate having it turned into a police office.”

Amiel showed him the voluntary-access request he had prepared.

“We're verifying the whereabouts of eleven people entered in a municipal evacuation register.”

“They weren't sent here by me.”

“That's why we need to see inside.”

“What you need is to speak to the port administrator.”

“We'll do that as well.”

Heskan finally opened the goods entrance. The building smelled of wet timber and salt. The walls still bore the height marks of old stacked beds; now there were piles of empty crates, barrels, and a cart without wheels.

Neria looked at the floor. Fresh grooves crossed the layer of dust, dragged from the side entrance toward a door at the back.

“Did you move cargo during the night?” she asked.

“Of course. Trade doesn't stop because a street gets wet.”

“At what time?”

“I don't keep my employees' watches.”

Amiel requested the dispatch ledger. Heskan replied that it was in another office and that they would need a formal demand before he handed it over.

Neria walked to the rear door. It wasn't locked. It led to a small room that had once served as an infirmary. An iron sink and a board displaying hygiene rules remained. On the floor lay eleven folded blankets.

They weren't new blankets. They were damp and smelled of smoke.

“What is this?” she asked.

Heskan appeared behind her.

“Old supplies. We haven't cleared them out.”

Neria touched the first blanket. Sand was still stuck to the fabric.

“You've kept them like this since winter?”

“I don't know where that sand came from.”

Amiel came over and photographed the room before authorizing anyone to touch anything else. Neria withdrew her hands. She had made enough reckless decisions overnight to understand why they needed to preserve what they found.

“Can you identify anyone who used these blankets?” the inspector asked.

“No,” Heskan answered.

“Can you tell us who came in before dawn?”

“No.”

“Then I need the name of the person who had custody of the keys.”

The merchant looked at him with distaste.

“That I may be able to find out.”

Neria looked around the room again. Eleven blankets did not mean eleven evacuees. They might have been placed there to create the appearance of assistance that had never been provided. Or they might have sheltered people who left before daybreak.

The number by itself settled nothing.

On their way out, they found Jaro waiting beside the pontoon. He had brought a piece of paper marked by a grease stain.

“It's the lighter crew's pay sheet,” he said. “My brother kept it. I took it out of his pocket when they carried him to the clinic.”

Neria examined it. It listed four men and a night shift beside the eastern floodgate. The skipper's name was Erven Tal. One line specified an extra payment for working during the turn of the tide.

“Who signed the employment order?” Amiel asked.

Jaro pointed to the bottom margin.

“I can't read that handwriting.”

The signature belonged to a cargo foreman. Neria recognized the name, but not the signature.

Below it, in red ink, was a note that had nothing to do with calculating wages:

“Withdraw crew before opening.”

Neria looked up.

“Who gave you this sheet?”

“The foreman, last night. He said it would make sure there were no problems getting paid.”

“At what time?”

Jaro looked toward the building, then at the inspector.

“Before you opened the gate, Ms. Calden. Long before.”

Neria held the paper by its edges. Someone had anticipated a dangerous opening and had left a written warning. The skipper who was supposed to receive it was, for now, a missing man.
