# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 040 · Paper Against Hunger

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Talia. **Location:** Port Cendra marshes, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/040-papel-contra-hambre.md`.

***

Talia had counted thirty-two sacks of flour when the official asked her for the residents' register.

Not one was missing. The sacks were stacked beneath the roof of the ration shed, and each bore a weight mark, a delivery date, and a supplier. What was missing was a way to distribute them without turning the shelter's families into a commercial list.

The official's name was Denor Vask. He'd come from Port Cendra accompanied by a clerk from the assistance office. He carried no guards. He showed them a municipal order to verify the delivery of food funded through the extraordinary contingency contract.

“We need to identify the beneficiaries,” he said. “Without that register, we can't certify that the expenditure reached real people.”

Talia understood the need for accountability. She'd organized rations for months and kept records of sacks opened, meals prepared, and the number of people served. She wasn't willing to hand over the names of residents who'd fled abusive employers, unresolved claims, or officials who confused residence with authorization.

“We can certify quantities and deliveries,” she replied. “Not every person's whereabouts for a company's use.”

Denor pointed to the contractor's signature.

“Estuary Cargo administers the supply. If we can't document the beneficiaries, it won't renew the allocations.”

“Then explain who will receive our data and what they can do with it.”

The official consulted the form.

“The information will remain in the assistance file.”

“Will the company paying for the carts be allowed to see it?”

“I can't guarantee how the internal copies are handled.”

Talia closed her notebook.

“Then I can't give you the book.”

The clerk looked away. Denor was silent for a moment.

“Without sufficient verification, we'll have to suspend the next delivery.”

The threat was administrative, but its effect would be physical: in a few days, the children would be having hot water for breakfast.

***

The shelter assembly met before lunch. Talia placed the remaining sacks, the ration budget, and a copy of the order on a table. She read aloud the section requiring every beneficiary to be identified. She didn't ask anyone to decide blindly.

Maira Bel, who was helping repair the settlement's nets, raised her hand.

“If we hand over the names, perhaps they'll be able to prove we live here and are entitled to assistance.”

“They could also use them to say we're occupying the canal strip without authorization,” Valco replied.

A woman with two children said her husband needed to find work at the port, and appearing on a register of marsh residents might get him accused of abandoning his job. Another feared that a list could be used to take away a benefit she'd been granted in her home village.

Talia listened without correcting their fears. Some had documentary grounds; others were based on experiences that were difficult to verify. In either case, their effect on people's decisions was real.

“The food isn't in dispute because it doesn't exist,” she said. “It's here. The dispute is what we have to hand over to make sure the next delivery arrives.”

Alna, the teacher from the closed school, proposed using family codes and keeping the name key in community custody.

“We can prove how many rations we distributed without giving the contractor a public list.”

“Will the office accept that?” a man asked.

“We don't know,” Talia replied. “We'll have to put it in writing.”

Edran, the fisherman, recalled that some people arrived for one night and left before receiving any document. A rigid system could leave them without food.

Maira pointed to the columns in the ledger.

“Here it says what was delivered. If someone wants to check, they can come count the sacks and speak with anyone willing to testify. They don't need to copy every child's name.”

Talia felt relieved. The proposal didn't resolve the supplier's pressure, but it turned an absolute refusal into a concrete form of accountability.

***

The first vote was difficult.

Several neighbors wanted to hand over the complete list to secure the next rations. Others preferred to reject all municipal assistance and live on fishing and their gardens. Talia knew the limits of that idea: fishing varied with the tides, and the plots had been damaged by the canal.

“We can't replace thirty-two sacks with a promise of eels,” she said.

The majority accepted a middle course: keep the existing food, distribute it according to need, offer the municipality records of quantities, and allow an independent body to verify the deliveries. Personal names would be shared only with consent or a legitimate request, under safeguards governing purpose and access.

The decision didn't oblige anyone who hadn't taken part to reveal their identity.

When the meeting ended, Talia asked for volunteers to review the ration books. Alna and Maira agreed. A boy who carried coal offered to count the sacks. Edran would prepare a small boat to take the proposal to the municipal office.

Talia wrote the agreement slowly.

She didn't want the shelter to appear to be evading all responsibility. They'd received supplies and needed to be able to show what they'd done with them.

What they rejected was turning aid into a tool for locating and classifying people who hadn't chosen to expose themselves.

***

Denor returned that afternoon, before taking the cart back to the port.

Talia handed him a copy of the agreement. It included quantities, the distribution method, and a proposal for independent verification. The original book remained at the shelter.

The official read it beside the shed.

“I can't commit the supplier to a system it hasn't approved,” he said.

“You can forward it to whoever makes that decision. The municipality is still responsible for the program.”

“The company advanced the goods. It needs to be paid.”

“And we need to eat. Both things must be verified without turning every plate into a declaration about our civil status.”

Denor looked at the sacks.

“Do you know how much it will cost to send an outside auditor all the way here?”

“Less than repeating an investigation because nobody can trust their own forms.”

The clerk wrote down the response. This time Denor didn't ask for the book. He signed a receipt for the proposal but kept his warning that the next delivery depended on administrative approval.

Talia accepted that uncertainty. She didn't accept that the copy of the order should leave without an intake stamp.

When the officials departed, a group of children waited beside the kitchen. One of the mothers asked whether there would be bread the next day.

“Tomorrow, yes,” Talia replied.

She didn't promise the following month.

***

That night, while Maira reviewed the food accounts, she found a discrepancy Talia hadn't noticed. The supplier's ledger showed forty sacks delivered. The shelter had received thirty-two.

“Are eight missing?” Maira asked.

“Not necessarily,” Talia replied. “They might have gone to the dispensary or another shelter.”

The delivery guide listed only “marsh complex.” It didn't separate the destinations. On the reverse was a receiving signature none of them recognized.

Talia checked her own record. On the delivery date, thirty-two sacks had been unloaded, counted by three residents. There was a note about a second cart that hadn't stopped.

“Edran said it went toward the bridge,” she recalled.

Maira looked up.

“Then we need to find out where the other eight were unloaded.”

“Yes. Without deciding in advance that someone stole them.”

Alna arrived with the school's attendance notebook. She'd recorded that on the day of the delivery the pupils helped carry flour and that the kitchen prepared food for an approximate number of neighbors. Her notes didn't prove the entire distribution, but they corroborated some of the sacks the shelter had received.

For the first time, Talia felt that the community ledger could serve a purpose beyond making sure the rations didn't run out before Friday.

It could be checked against invoices.

***

At dawn, a messenger from the court arrived with a notice from Amiel Rusk. He didn't demand the names. He requested preservation of the distribution records because there was an investigation into benefits that had been invoiced but not substantiated.

The order distinguished the quantity ledger from any register of residents. It allowed certified copies of the relevant pages under supervision, with personal data unrelated to the investigation's purpose concealed.

Talia read the document twice. Magistrate Yuna Tor had signed the restriction.

“This is closer to what we asked for,” Alna said.

“It is. We'll have to see how they carry it out.”

Talia agreed to let a clerk copy the flour in-and-out records in the presence of two shelter representatives. She didn't hand over the complete book or the children's names.

Among the entries they found a column of old receipts for canal maintenance work. Some bore the signatures of people who later appeared as ration beneficiaries; others referred to deliveries made before the flood.

Maira pointed to a reference to the cargo company.

“These pages might explain who was paid to transport food and who distributed it.”

Talia looked at her.

“Then we'll have to follow the money without taking away the bread.”

The clerk stamped the copy.

At the entrance, a little girl waited with an empty plate. Talia walked her to the kitchen and served her the first ration of the morning.

The flour was accounted for.

The names, for now, still belonged to the people who carried them.
