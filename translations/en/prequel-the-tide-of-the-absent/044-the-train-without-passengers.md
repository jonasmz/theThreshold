# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 044 · The Train Without Passengers

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Vela Noren. **Location:** Port Cendra–Costa Interior railway line, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/044-el-tren-sin-pasajeros.md`.

***

The train had departed with seventy-eight travelers. None of them occupied the carriage reserved for the eleven evacuees.

Vela Noren read it in the railway consist register, beside a drawing showing how the coaches were arranged behind the locomotive. This wasn't an empty train or a physical disappearance. The company had prepared a special section, paid for by Estuary Cargo, that wasn't listed as occupied in the boarding check.

The distinction mattered.

The municipal contract referred to a transfer service for eleven people. The reservation proved that someone had requested capacity for them. What was missing was independent confirmation that they'd used that capacity.

Vela had arrived with an order for a comparison authorized by Yuna Tor. Accompanying her were a railway inspector, Ilven Sar, and the route's former conductor, Duro Venn. Neither was obliged to reach the same conclusion she did.

“The train left late because of bridge maintenance,” Ilven explained. “The special carriage remained attached for the entire journey.”

“Who verified that it was empty?” Vela asked.

Duro opened a guard's logbook.

“I walked through the corridor when we left Cendra. The seats were empty. But I didn't make a specific count: the section had been reserved for possible assistance transfers.”

“Did anyone board later?”

“Not that I remember. There were brief stops where ordinary passengers could get on.”

Vela noted the difference between observation and memory.

A statement about empty seats wasn't yet proof of the entire journey. There might have been later boardings, coach changes, or people who traveled outside the reserved section.

“Let's look at the station records,” she suggested.

***

At the first stop, the stationmaster had kept a notebook of coach arrivals and departures, separate from the ticket sales ledger. The special carriage appeared there under its serial number, but there was no passenger handover record.

“We never required a nominal list for every coach,” the stationmaster explained. “Only for assisted groups when the authorities required a formal handover.”

“Was one requested on this journey?” Ilven asked.

The man searched for a circular.

Estuary Cargo's order announced a transfer coordinated by the company, but said final identification would be completed at the destination. This was a procedure that could be used legitimately when a group's documents had been damaged.

At the destination, however, there was no recipient who had signed for the people, either.

Vela asked to consult the luggage ledger, because the case of Erven Tal had shown that a bag could be registered even when its supposed owner didn't travel. There were eleven baggage vouchers, but only three item numbers. That wasn't a contradiction in itself: a traveler could travel without luggage.

“Who delivered those three items?” she asked.

“A corporate courier,” the clerk replied. “The record has an account code.”

The same abbreviation appeared on the tickets.

Vela resisted the temptation to treat that link as proof of boarding. It was, rather, evidence that someone had organized paperwork and goods under the same contract.

***

At the Costa Interior terminal, they were met by an administrator named Irven Del. She had copies of the arrival reports and a notebook in which she recorded incidents involving assisted passengers.

“I remember the train,” she said. “We were told a group was coming, so I prepared water and blankets. Nobody showed up at the reception desk.”

“Did you receive anyone that day?” Vela asked.

“We received ordinary passengers. Not the group of eleven under that reference.”

The precision seemed valuable to her. The administrator wasn't claiming that none of the eleven had arrived by other means. She was saying that the expected group hadn't been received.

Ilven asked about the blankets.

“They were returned to the storeroom,” Irven replied. “We have the return entry.”

Vela examined the document. It was signed by two station workers and dated the same day. The number of packages matched those prepared for the group, though it couldn't by itself establish how many passengers had or hadn't been there.

“Why was a certificate of completed service issued?” she asked.

Irven frowned.

“I didn't issue it.”

Vela showed her a copy from the municipal file. It bore a generic railway stamp and a field for “receipt confirmed.” The signature was written in an abbreviated name that didn't match any employee on the terminal payroll.

“That needs to be checked,” Ilven said. “It could be a delegation, a stamp used at another station, or a later addition. I can't call it false from here.”

The administrator agreed to preserve the destination books and the stamp register. She didn't hand over originals without a formal record.

***

Conductor Duro wanted to revisit one detail.

“When we left the port, a man got into the special carriage and left some envelopes on the front seat,” he recalled. “I thought they were instructions for the staff.”

“Did you see him get in or get out?” Vela asked.

“Get in. When I came back, he was gone.”

“Was he wearing a railway uniform?”

“A work jacket. I don't remember an insignia.”

Vela didn't suggest any names. The account might refer to an authorized courier who left documents, or to someone who used the carriage for another purpose.

She asked the conductor to draw the seat's location and note whether he'd seen any objects afterward. Duro pointed to a bench beside the door and wrote that he hadn't touched the envelopes.

Ilven checked the carriage cleaning report. The first inspection at the terminal mentioned a package of forms that had been found and handed over to the administrative office.

“Was it kept?” Vela asked.

Irven searched a cabinet.

The package had been filed with unclaimed materials. They opened it in front of witnesses, after photographing the seal. Inside were incomplete reception forms, envelopes, and a list of eleven reference numbers, not names.

Vela felt her pulse quicken.

The papers proved that documents had been carried to register a group. They didn't prove that the people had arrived. In fact, the absence of reception signatures was consistent with what Irven had said.

But something else caught her attention.

The forms bore a printing date from before the flood and a handwritten correction added later. The numbering matched that of the concession annexes Amiel had preserved.

“Who could have made the correction?” Ilven asked.

“I don't know,” Vela replied. “We need to compare the ink, circulation, and authorization. I don't want another piece of evidence rendered unusable by taking it out of context.”

The package was sealed under a railway report, and the court received a copy of the discovery record.

***

On the way back to Port Cendra, Vela cross-checked the consist registers, the conductor's statements, and the destination record. She had three independent sources pointing in the same direction: a carriage had been reserved and moved for eleven people, but there was no documented receipt of that group, and a witness at the destination said they hadn't appeared.

The conclusion had to be carefully qualified.

She couldn't prove that none of those people had traveled in another carriage, by road, or by barge. She couldn't claim that all the tickets had been invented. She could say that the certificate of completed transfer lacked the railway verification it appeared to rely on.

Amina Dorel, the expert who had inspected the selector at the booth, received a copy of the table.

“Do you need the machine whose chain of custody was broken?” she asked.

“No. This comparison is based on railway documents and separately obtained testimony.”

Amina seemed relieved.

Vela remembered the inner buoy, whose pilot light was illuminated by impulses they were still trying to explain. The investigation didn't need all its signals to share a single perpetrator in order to move forward.

“What do we do with the list?” Peran asked.

“Give it to Amiel with the source of each entry identified. And don't publish it as though it were a list of the dead or the missing.”

At the courthouse, Amiel read the report in silence.

“The train existed,” he said at last.

“Yes.”

“The reservation existed.”

“Yes.”

“What we don't have is proof that those eleven people were transported.”

Vela nodded.

The inspector filed the table alongside the certificate for Baret Luno, the worker who'd died in the city before the scheduled departure.

One specific person already contradicted the overall account. The railway now revealed a second weakness, independent of that death.

Vela looked at the eleven reference numbers.

For the first time, the main question wasn't where a train had been lost.

It was who had needed it to appear to have carried people whom nobody had received.
