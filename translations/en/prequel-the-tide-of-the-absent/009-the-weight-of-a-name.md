# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 009 · The Weight of a Name

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Amiel Rusk. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/009-el-peso-de-un-nombre.md`.

***

The young man had a leg in a splint and a name nobody at the port seemed willing to acknowledge.

Amiel Rusk found him at the clinic, sitting beneath a window with a folded blanket over his knees. Jaro Velk was beside him. The younger brother held a bowl of broth that was cooling untouched.

“My name is Liron,” the injured man said before Amiel could ask his first question. “Liron Velk.”

The inspector introduced himself and explained why he needed Liron's statement: to establish who had been working beside the eastern floodgate, what warning the crew had received, and how one of their vessels had ended up smashed apart.

“I'm not here to tell you what you ought to remember,” he added.

Liron looked at his brother.

“He told me what happened afterward. I can tell you about before.”

Amiel set his folder on an empty chair. The nurse had allowed twenty minutes for the interview. The young man had a fracture without displacement and a long wound running from knee to ankle. He was in no condition to attend a hearing yet.

“Who hired you?” Amiel asked.

“A foreman at the depot. He paid me to dredge for four nights.”

“Who were you working with?”

“Erven Tal. And two other men.”

“Do you remember their names?”

Liron identified one by his first name and the other by the nickname the workers used. Amiel wrote them down without inventing surnames.

“Did you sign a contract?”

“They made us put a mark on a sheet.”

“Did they give you a copy?”

“No.”

Jaro took the pay sheet he'd shown Neria from his pocket.

“He had this on him.”

Amiel recognized it by the grease stain and the red-ink note. They had already photographed it and recorded it as a document handed over by a family member. They still needed to verify that it had actually come from the depot.

“I need to keep the original,” he said. “We'll give you a receipt.”

Jaro's fingers tensed.

“It's the only thing that proves he worked.”

“That's exactly why it needs to be protected.”

“The port official said it was no good because Liron isn't on the workers' register.”

Amiel rested his hands on his knees. He'd seen that confusion before. The port register certified authorizations and access to facilities; it did not grant people their existence. A witness could testify even if someone had failed to fulfill the obligation to register him. Proving an employment relationship for a wage claim or compensation was another matter.

“Your brother can tell us what he saw,” he said. “The register doesn't decide whether he has a voice. What we need to establish is who employed him and under what conditions.”

Liron seemed to be listening from very far away.

“They gave us tokens at the end of each shift,” he murmured. “We exchanged them for money on Fridays.”

“Do you still have one?”

The young man searched the seam of his jacket, which hung beside the bed. He took out a piece of brass the size of a coin, pierced through the center. Two letters and the number four were engraved on it.

Amiel had him place it on a clean piece of gauze, without touching it himself.

“When did they give you that?”

“The night before the accident. For the first shift.”

“Who gave it to you?”

“The cashier at the north depot.”

“Could you recognize him?”

“If I saw him, maybe.”

Amiel considered the answer useful precisely because it promised so little.

***

At the north depot, the cashier insisted that all work tokens belonged to registered employees. His name was Osen Briar, and he wore an immaculate linen shirt beneath a waistcoat that was too tight.

Amiel had authorization to examine that week's payroll ledger. The order did not cover private correspondence or other customers' records.

“Liron Velk isn't listed,” Osen said after turning three pages.

“Could you check the token's identification number as well?”

The man examined the number four.

“That doesn't identify a worker. It identifies a crew.”

“Then I need to know who belonged to crew four.”

Osen opened another ledger, considerably thinner than the first. Its pages contained handwritten names, start dates, and hours worked. The column for the night of the accident was blank.

Amiel compared the pages. The earlier entries were in blue ink. On the empty sheet were traces of writing that had been removed: small indentations visible in the light from the side.

“Do you use carbon-copy paper?” he asked.

“Sometimes.”

“Where do you keep the duplicate sheets?”

“In accounting.”

“I'll request that they're preserved.”

Osen shut the ledger.

“There's no need to turn an administrative mistake into a crime.”

“I haven't called anything a crime.”

The cashier opened a drawer and took out a folder of brass tokens identical to Liron's. Some were tied together in groups of five; others lay loose. The engraved letters matched an abbreviation used by the depot.

“We issue them according to tonnage,” he explained. “Anyone could have picked one up off the ground.”

“That's why I also need the redemption ledger.”

Osen pressed his lips together.

“Those are commercial records.”

“I'll request an extension of the order. In the meantime, record that I've asked for them to be preserved.”

Amiel couldn't prove that the cashier was concealing a worker. He could prove that the man refused to produce a particular document and that the document was relevant. It was a less satisfying step than a confession, and a far more durable one.

As he left, he found the clinic nurse waiting near the gate. The woman had introduced herself as Rema during the first interview. She carried a bag of used bandages and looked irritated at having to leave work to speak to him.

“Liron is afraid we'll charge him for his care,” she said.

“The city has an emergency fund.”

“Until someone decides he wasn't authorized to work where he was injured.”

Amiel understood the problem. The conflict between records did not merely affect a future trial. Right now, it determined who paid for a bed, a medicine, and a family's food.

“What do you need from me?” he asked.

“A certificate saying his injuries occurred during the flood, not a ruling on his contract.”

“That I can certify from the proceedings we already have.”

Rema nodded.

“Then write exactly that. You can argue about the rest with your lawyers.”

***

Amiel returned to the clinic at dusk to complete the statement.

Liron had slept for part of the afternoon. Jaro was no longer there; he'd gone to get coal for their home. The young man asked for water before answering the remaining questions.

“Did you receive a warning that the gate would be opened?” Amiel asked.

“The skipper had a paper. It said we had to leave the canal before a maneuver.”

“Did you see it?”

“He showed it to me because I asked whether we had to stop working.”

“At what time?”

Liron closed his eyes.

“I don't know. The sirens hadn't sounded yet.”

“Why did you stay?”

“The foreman said the warning was for another crew. Said we'd be paid double if we finished.”

Amiel didn't ask him to name the guilty. He asked what words had been used and who else might have heard them. Liron remembered another worker protesting and receiving the same answer. That man had jumped before the lighter struck the mooring pile.

“And Erven?” the inspector asked.

Liron looked away.

“He tried to untie the rope. The current was pulling us toward the wall. After that I didn't see him.”

The young man began to shake. Amiel waited until he caught his breath, without filling the silence with questions.

“I thought they'd warned us by mistake,” Liron whispered. “Now everyone says we were never there.”

Amiel closed his folder.

“Just because a list doesn't name you doesn't make what happened to you disappear.”

In the corridor, an officer handed him an envelope delivered from accounting. It contained a copy of the crew payroll, sent in response to his preservation request.

Crew four was listed in full. All four members had names, wages, and assigned hours.

Not one of them was called Liron Velk.

And beside the space for the recipient's signature was a mark that, at first glance, resembled the one the young man had described.

Amiel put the paper away again. The resemblance alone wasn't enough to conclude that somebody had signed on Liron's behalf. But now he knew what he had to ask the cashier the next day: who had collected the pay for that work, and why the young man lying in the bed couldn't.
