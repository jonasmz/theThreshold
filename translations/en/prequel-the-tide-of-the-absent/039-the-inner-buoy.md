# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 039 · The Inner Buoy

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Vela Noren. **Location:** Inner passage buoy and repeater, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/039-la-boya-interior.md`.

***

The inner buoy flashed while Vela Noren still had the lens cap on her camera.

She saw it through an opening in the boat, between two pillars of the border bridge. It was a pale light, followed by three pauses that seemed too regular to have come from the sun. The day was overcast, and a layer of foam covered the water.

“Did you see that?” she asked Peran.

“I saw something. I don't know whether it came from the buoy or a window.”

Vela appreciated the precision. They'd traveled there with authorization from the local river authority, accompanied by a navigation inspector. The buoy had been taken out of formal service twelve years earlier. Its presence in the channel wasn't a secret: it served as a physical reference for boatmen when the tide covered the stones.

The strange thing was that some skippers still attributed a signal to it.

The inspector, Lerna Vis, showed them the mooring plan.

“The original lamp ran on a storage mechanism,” she explained. “It was dismantled when we installed the new signals. Only the casing should remain.”

“Has anyone checked inside?” Vela asked.

“During the last inspection, four years ago. Not since the flood.”

Vela prepared a photographic log. She couldn't interpret the flash based on an impression. She needed to record the time, the observer's position, the state of the water, and any nearby light sources.

A second glimmer appeared beside the bridge.

This time Peran saw it clearly.

“Four flashes, if we count the first one,” he said.

“Two observations don't establish a code,” Vela replied. “Let's see if it happens again.”

They waited.

The buoy rocked three times without flashing.

***

The inspection began when the current had slowed enough to work safely. Lerna required life jackets, a mooring line, and a technician from her department. Nobody could open a floating structure without assessing its stability and the risk of electric shock.

The buoy was covered in salt and small shells. At its base was a riveted metal box connected to a cable that descended toward the bottom. The old plan showed that cable as part of a power line that had been withdrawn.

“Is it still connected to anything?” Vela asked.

Lerna consulted the guide.

“Not according to the inventory. But I can't be certain without taking measurements.”

The technician used an insulated probe to check for voltage. He found a weak, intermittent current, insufficient to power a complete beacon but potentially capable of operating a relay or a low-power lamp.

Vela felt the investigation taking a different shape. The signal hadn't come from nowhere. There was a conductor receiving power from a source that had yet to be identified.

“Let's not disconnect it yet,” she asked. “First, let's document its condition.”

Lerna authorized measurements without breaking the seals on the main box. The technician recorded the voltage intervals. They repeated with a certain regularity, but each series had a different pause.

“They could be fluctuations in the feeder,” Peran said.

“Or activations of a device that's still connected,” Vela added.

The pattern alone wasn't enough to choose between them.

The inspector ordered the installation preserved and requested an updated plan of the underwater cable from the utilities registry. While they waited, Vela compared recent photographs with an image taken by a convoy skipper weeks before the flood.

In the older photograph, the rivet on the side box had a rusted head. Now it was shiny.

Someone had worked on the buoy after the last photograph, although they couldn't know exactly when or why.

***

The registry's response arrived that afternoon.

The cable hadn't officially been disconnected. A partial modification had left the line connected to a shore repeater to keep two auxiliary signals operating during the renovation. The final closure order had never received confirmation of execution.

Vela studied the diagram. The repeater was in a storage wharf, not the main tower. Its power came from a shared accumulator that also sustained a channel-level gauge.

“That would explain the current,” Peran said.

“It would explain one possible source. We still need to check which devices are connected and who maintains the line.”

Lerna asked them to locate the repeater's keeper. He was a maintenance employee named Ruan, close to retirement, who'd known the system since before the renovation.

Ruan arrived with a toolbox and a file with worn covers.

“The buoy shouldn't be flashing,” he said. “We removed the beacon and left a test circuit to check continuity from shore.”

“How was it activated?” Vela asked.

“With short impulses. If the cable was intact, a pilot light inside the box responded. It was a local test, not a signal intended for boats.”

“Then why can it be seen from the channel?”

Ruan opened a drawing of the casing. There was a small inspection window facing the bridge. The pilot light, originally protected by a screen, could become visible if the cover shifted.

Lerna examined the outer box.

The screen was loose. One of its fasteners had been replaced with wire.

“That isn't in the maintenance order,” she said.

Ruan went still.

“Nobody was supposed to open that window. Not for years.”

Vela photographed the detail. An improvised repair might explain part of the phenomenon, but not why the circuit was receiving impulses at irregular intervals.

“Was the test scheduled?” she asked.

“No. The last authorized test is recorded in the log from six months ago.”

***

They returned to the shore station to examine the repeater's log. The room smelled of hot dust and had a bank of disconnected switches. The buoy circuit was still powered from a branch that wasn't properly labeled.

Ruan showed them the accumulator. Its charge had decreased over the previous weeks but remained within its reserve operating range. The energy consumed by the impulses was small; there was no way those flashes could operate a floodgate or raise the water in a reservoir.

Vela requested the usage records.

The system didn't preserve complete messages, only activation counts and approximate time marks. On certain days there were sequences of impulses coinciding with the passage times of commercial convoys.

“Could it be an automatic test?” Lerna asked.

Ruan searched for a configuration sheet.

“The automatic test should emit three identical impulses. Here there are groups of four, five, and six.”

“Then somebody changed the pattern,” Peran said.

Vela pointed to a handwritten note at the bottom of the log.

“Not necessarily the device. It could receive external sequences through the cable if the line was left connected to another system.”

The note said that during an earlier renovation, the repeater had been used to check continuity of the bridge's freight signals. In theory, a commercial transmission could light the buoy's pilot as a side effect.

Lerna asked that the hypothesis not be circulated yet. If navigation pilots began interpreting the flash as an instruction, the risk of accidents would increase.

It was a reasonable decision. Vela had seen enough to understand that an old signal could look intentional to people who didn't know how it worked.

Even so, the variations deserved comparison.

***

At dusk, Vela compared the activation log with a convoy list Orin had been allowed to consult by its owners. The data was incomplete; some voyages had no exact time, and others used clocks whose calibration was unknown.

She found a partial correlation.

On days when a convoy platform had changed its route, the buoy activations showed an additional pause. It didn't happen every time, and there were activations with no convoy recorded.

“A password?” Peran asked.

“An imperfect correlation,” Vela replied. “There may be several causes.”

The inspector authorized a controlled test for the following day, after notifying the skippers and ensuring that no official signal would be confused with it. The technician would send an identified impulse from the shore repeater, using limited power, while two observers measured the response at the buoy.

They wouldn't activate an unknown circuit while vessels were passing.

Before closing the log, Vela noticed an entry made on the night of the flood. The number of activations was higher than usual, but there was no mark indicating an authorized test. The sheet bore a code that also appeared in a maintenance report for the commercial branch.

That didn't prove there had been a clandestine message.

It did allow them to make a precise request: preserve the buoy's log, identify the end that had sent the impulses, and compare its times with the port's opening signal.

Vela sealed the notebook.

When she went outside, she saw one last glimmer reflected on the water. It might have come from the buoy or from a lamp on the bridge. This time she didn't try to count it.

She'd spent too long hearing the meaning she wanted to find.

Now she wanted to establish which cable had actually spoken.
