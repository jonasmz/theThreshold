# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 001 · The Buoy That Fell Silent

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Neria Calden. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/001-la-boya-que-callo.md`.

***

Buoy number four stopped ringing three minutes before the water began to rise.

Neria Calden knew something was wrong because the silence came at the wrong interval.

Every buoy in Port Cendra had a different voice. The first struck twice; the second, three times. The third carried a cracked bell. The fourth gave a single stroke every nine seconds, whenever the current moved toward the canal's outlet.

At 2:07 in the morning, that stroke disappeared.

Neria looked up from the regulator she was dismantling. The workshop smelled of fish oil, damp iron, and reheated coffee. She had spent the last hour trying to determine why the eastern floodgate was consuming more energy than it had the previous week. The gauge showed a small but steady loss. A loss like that wouldn't sink a city. It would ruin its engines by making them work a little harder every night.

“Toben,” she called. “Did you hear number four?”

A grunt came from the gear pit.

“I heard you stop hammering away with that wrench.”

“The buoy.”

Toben poked his head out. His eyelashes were white with lime dust, and he had a fresh cut across the bridge of his nose.

They listened together.

Nothing.

“Maybe it's jammed,” he said.

Neria knew every single thing that could jam in the port. That was why she disliked the word when it was offered as an explanation.

She shut the regulator, chalked the needle's position, and stepped onto the walkway. The night was clear. No clouds hung over the bay, and the cargo ships' lights seemed motionless. The tide had begun to ebb an hour earlier.

A deep thud sounded beneath her boots.

It hadn't come from the sea. It had come from inside.

The service canal, which should have been falling with the tide, had risen enough to cover the first inspection step.

“Toben. The measuring staff!”

The worker came out carrying a lamp. Neria knelt beside the numbered scale. The water was brushing the mark they had painted for a winter flood. It wasn't churning. It was rising evenly, as though someone were filling an enormous bathtub.

“It hasn't rained in four days,” Toben said.

“I know.”

“What if the tide turned?”

“It doesn't turn like this.”

Neria ran toward the distribution panel.

Three routes could bring water into the inner basin: the northern river, the pumping station, and the return spillway. The first was regulated upstream. The second was supposed to be running at half capacity. The third remained closed except during maintenance operations.

Three mechanical indicators showed exactly those conditions.

All three could be wrong.

Neria lifted the cover from the flow recorder. A strip of paper passed between two rollers, with needles linked to pressure lines tracing their readings across it. The trace for the return spillway was so high that it nearly ran off the edge.

“The return valve is open,” she said.

“The panel says closed.”

“The panel shows the control's position. Not the valve's.”

The first emergency siren sounded from the western station.

Then a second came from the lower docks.

Neria checked the clock. 2:12.

“Cut off the return supply. Use the mechanical valve, not the switch.”

“The access is behind the underpass.”

“Take Sivan with you. And a rope.”

Toben ran.

Neria picked up the wired telephone at the panel and asked for the signal room. The receiver crackled four times before anyone answered.

“Coastal Central. Vela Noren.”

“Calden speaking, eastern floodgate. I need you to warn the lower docks: the water is rising inside the basin, not from the sea. Suspend all crossings of the discharge canal.”

“I was told six minutes ago that the situation was under control.”

Neria tightened her grip on the receiver.

“Who told you?”

“I don't have a name on this line. The message came under maintenance identification.”

“I am maintenance.”

There was a short pause.

“Then who's out in the water?”

A crash interrupted the conversation.

Neria dropped the receiver and ran to the window. A coal barge had struck a mooring pile broadside. Across the water, lamps were coming on one by one in the houses of the low-lying district. The canal level kept rising.

At the far end of the basin, a child appeared on a balcony and called out to someone.

She returned to the panel.

The eastern floodgate could release water toward the sea using a Binding motor powered by the station's accumulator. The system was designed to operate with moderate differences in water level. The pressure differential now exceeded the recommended range, though it had not yet reached the lockout limit. If she waited, the mechanism might not be able to open at all.

If she opened it, the current would sweep away anything in the outer canal.

Vela was still calling her name from the fallen receiver.

Neria picked it up.

“Clear the discharge canal. You've got four minutes.”

“There's a tugboat coming in that way.”

“Then get it out.”

“Its onboard radio is switched off.”

“Use a signal lamp.”

“Buoy four isn't responding, and I can't see around the bend from here.”

Neria looked at the clock. She didn't have four minutes. Water was reaching the thresholds of the first houses.

“Warn the dike guards. I'm opening the gate.”

“With that difference in level?”

“With the one I've got.”

She hung up before Vela could give her a worse answer.

She unlocked the emergency control cover. To prevent an accidental opening, it required two movements: pulling out the iron safety catch and throwing a lever that transferred the load to the winch. A warning plate instructed operators to wait for the safety supervisor.

Neria was the safety supervisor on that shift.

She lowered the lever.

The accumulator responded with a hum that vibrated through her jaw. A needle fell rapidly. Part of the load was being lost as heat; smoke began to curl from a seam in the regulator casing. There was enough stored energy to move the gate, but it couldn't all be discharged at once without burning out the conductors.

Neria kept the power flow below the red mark. The operation became unbearably slow.

“Come on,” she whispered, unsure whether she was speaking to the metal or the water.

The first leaf of the gate moved barely a handspan.

A dark current poured through the gap.

Outside, a tugboat's whistle sounded.

The mechanism's second jolt shook the floor. Neria felt a different vibration, much too sharp. The left pinion had skipped a tooth.

If she pushed it further, she could lose the entire transmission.

She reduced the power. The gate stopped half-open, shuddering under the pressure.

Through the window she saw the tugboat rounding the bend. Its captain was trying to escape before the current caught its stern.

A smaller craft became trapped between the hull and the dock wall.

Neria grabbed the telephone.

“Vela, I need confirmation. Can the tug get through?”

“It's trying. There's a lighter alongside it.”

“If I open any further, I'll capsize it.”

“If you don't, the low-lying district will flood.”

Neria looked at the houses. The child on the balcony had disappeared, but two women were trying to shut a door with water already pouring beneath it.

There was no decision without possible victims. Only a difference between risks she could measure and others she could barely imagine.

“Tell them to hold on,” she ordered.

She released the brake a quarter turn.

The gate advanced with a groan. The accumulator lost charge, the conductors overheated, and one of the workshop lights burst. Neria kept turning until the gate leaf reached the minimum relief mark.

The water inside stopped rising.

It didn't fall immediately. The mass of water needed time to find its way out.

A crack of splintering wood came from the outer canal.

Neria stepped away from the controls as though they had burned her hand.

When Toben returned, he was soaked through and supporting Sivan by the shoulder. The young man was bleeding from his forehead. They had closed the return valve, but a component had come loose under the pressure.

“We closed it,” Toben said. “Someone had taken the stop apart.”

Neria said nothing.

On the other side of the glass, the tugboat had managed to straighten out. The smaller lighter lay against a mooring pile, splintered, but two figures were moving on its deck.

Sivan sat on the floor and rested his head against the wall.

“It wasn't a breakdown,” he whispered. “The valve had been prepared to stay open.”

Then the fourth buoy sounded again.

Once.

And again, nine seconds later.

Neria watched the recorder's needle as the water level finally dropped. She had saved the low-lying district from a flood that should never have happened. She still didn't know what it had cost.

She took the duty log, wrote down the time she had opened the gate, and signed beside Toben's name. Her hands were trembling badly enough to distort one of the letters.

From the doorway, a municipal messenger handed her a damp envelope.

“Evacuation order,” the woman said. “They want you to confirm it was carried out.”

Neria opened it. The paper bore the floodgate maintenance seal and an issue time: 1:58.

Fourteen minutes before the first siren had sounded.

“This is impossible,” she said.

The messenger looked too exhausted to argue.

“I don't know. They told me to ask you about the missing workers.”

“What workers?”

The woman consulted her sheet.

“The ones evacuated at 1:58. According to this document, you were there.”

Neria looked at the signature stamped at the bottom of the form.

It was hers.
