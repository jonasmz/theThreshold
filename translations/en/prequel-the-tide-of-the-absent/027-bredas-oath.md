# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 027 · Breda's Oath

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Breda Lin. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/027-el-juramento-de-breda.md`.

***

Breda Lin had signed incomplete reports before. This was the first time she'd been asked to swear they'd been complete.

The summons arrived at the Mutual in an envelope bearing the court's seal. Magistrate Yuna Tor requested her appearance as the auditor responsible for a collective vessel certification. She was not accused of a crime. She was to explain what she'd seen, which checks she'd delegated, and what the signature on seven disputed policies actually certified.

Breda read the document in front of Nimo.

“Will you take the reserve ledger?” he asked.

“I'll bring the copies I'm allowed to hand over and a list of originals held by the Mutual.”

“The director says the committee is still working.”

“That doesn't change what I signed.”

Nimo looked down. On his desk was a letter from the committee: two provisional payments had been released after an outside surveyor confirmed photographs of the hulls. The others remained under review.

Breda asked for the references of the reports, not their confidential contents. Her authority to request external documents was still suspended. She could consult her working notes, the materials she'd received during her audit, and the files whose preservation was registered in her name.

A statement made with borrowed authority was no better than one made blindly.

That evening, she read the collective certification from the previous autumn again. It declared that the insured units had been verified according to the current protocol and that the Mutual could accept them for expanded coverage.

In the folder she found the outside provider's guarantee, the sample photographs she'd seen, and a note she'd written proposing individual inspections.

Beneath it was the director's reply: “Proceed with collective certification. Exceptions will be reviewed afterward.”

Breda remembered thinking that was a necessary concession. The commercial season was beginning, the Mutual needed premiums, and small vessels depended on the overall agreement remaining in force.

Yet she'd signed a sentence that failed to distinguish the samples examined from the units that hadn't been inspected.

That distinction hadn't vanished just because she had a message explaining the decision.

***

Yuna Tor received her in a modest hearing room. There was a clerk, two Mutual representatives, a lawyer for the insured parties, and Amiel Rusk seated at the back. The doors remained open during the non-confidential portion of the session.

“Explain what your signature means,” the magistrate requested.

Breda held her copy of the report without looking toward her director.

“It certifies that my department reviewed the campaign's documentation and accepted the adequacy of the procedure. It doesn't mean I personally inspected every hull.”

“Does the text make that distinction clear?”

“Not clearly enough.”

The broker's lawyer leaned forward.

“Are you claiming those vessels didn't exist?”

“No. I'm saying that the documents I'd seen couldn't establish their individual existence on every declared date.”

“Then why did you sign?”

The question hung in the air.

Breda could point to the director. She could talk about urgency. She could explain that other insurers worked with similar case files. All of it was true, and none of it answered completely.

“Because I accepted that a sample and a contractual guarantee were sufficient to issue a general certification,” she said. “It was a mistaken professional decision. I later realized the report suggested we'd verified more than we actually had.”

The Mutual's lawyer intervened.

“The witness has not testified to intentional falsification.”

“I haven't,” Breda replied. “I'm describing what I did.”

Yuna made a note.

“What checks were mandatory at the time?”

Breda listed photographs, registration numbers, the surveyor's identity, hull numbers, and an exception review for vessels with irregular histories. Some checks were documented; others had been deferred.

The magistrate asked her to separate each omission in a table. Breda handed over a prepared sheet with three columns: requirement, available supporting material, and verification status.

She hadn't brought a moral judgment written in advance.

She'd brought limits that could be discussed.

***

During the recess, the Mutual's director found her in the corridor.

“You're turning an administrative error into a public confession,” he said.

Breda closed her folder.

“I'm correcting the scope of a document I signed.”

“If the Mutual collapses, hundreds of vessels will lose coverage.”

“I know. That's why I haven't said every policy is fraudulent.”

He moved a step closer.

“We could resolve the discrepancies with a supplementary report without exposing anyone.”

“We should have done that before paying advances. Now we have to explain what we knew and when.”

The director watched her in silence.

“The committee has found documents you never saw. Perhaps the cases will turn out to be valid.”

“I hope so. It wouldn't change what I'd verified when I signed.”

Breda thought of the accountant Calvor Edren, who'd preferred to lose his job rather than close accounts without receipts. She didn't intend to imitate him as a heroic gesture. She knew that a dramatic resignation would leave her work without follow-through and allow others to describe it as an outburst.

She returned to the hearing room.

Yuna asked why three vessels shared the same photographic background and why one hull number was associated with an earlier scrapping.

“The image doesn't allow every vessel to be identified with certainty,” Breda said. “The hull number might have been copied incorrectly, transferred with additional documentation, or used improperly. We need the original shipyard and registration records.”

“Why did you order an advance withheld?”

“Because the contradiction could be verified, and payment might be irreversible before the comparison was complete.”

The broker's lawyer asked whether she'd visited a former Estuary Cargo accountant on her own initiative.

“Yes. He explained general procedures. He gave me no company ledgers.”

“Did you use that conversation to investigate outside your authority?”

“I requested no confidential information. I recorded the meeting and the professional questions. My external auditing powers were suspended afterward.”

Yuna considered the question answered. Testimony continued until dark.

***

When it was over, Amiel waited for her beside the courthouse door.

“There's a connection between the company collecting crew wages and the broker on certain policies,” he said. “I need to know whether your files recorded shared services.”

Breda listened cautiously.

“I can't hand you private documentation merely because you ask for it in a corridor.”

“I'm not asking you to. I want to draft a request that doesn't sweep in accounts unrelated to the case.”

She thought of the seven files and the commercial account appearing on their payment records.

“Request representation agreements, dates of ownership changes, and inspection invoices for those units,” she replied. “The policies alone don't identify the people who carried out the physical work.”

Amiel wrote down the categories without mentioning numbers he wasn't yet authorized to know.

“Thank you.”

Breda was about to leave when a clerk called her back. Yuna wanted to give her a copy of her testimony and ask her to confirm a correction of form.

Reviewing the text, Breda found a sentence that read: “The auditor verified seven vessels.” She'd explained that she'd checked only a sample of the documentation.

“That isn't what I said,” she pointed out.

The clerk checked his notes.

“It's a summary.”

“That summary removes the very distinction I came here to testify about.”

She requested that her exact answer be transcribed and waited until the magistrate authorized the correction. She signed each page after reading it.

Her hand didn't shake.

In the courtyard, Nimo was waiting with a dry coat. He had news from the Mutual: the director had sent the court a supplementary certification issued that same afternoon.

Breda opened the copy. The new text acknowledged that some individual verifications remained outstanding, but maintained that all previous reports retained full validity.

It wasn't a proven falsehood. It was a conclusion that couldn't be supported without additional evidence.

Three signatures appeared at the bottom.

One belonged to the director. Another to the new auditor. The third was a space intended for the person responsible for the original certification.

Her name was printed underneath.

Breda folded the document.

“I won't sign it,” she said.

Nimo looked at the empty box.

“What if they fill it in without you?”

“Then there will be another signature whose provenance they'll have to explain.”

Breda put the copy beside her corrected testimony.

She'd learned that telling the truth didn't erase the earlier mistake. It merely prevented her silence from making it worse from that moment on.
