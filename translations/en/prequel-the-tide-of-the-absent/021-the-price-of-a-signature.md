# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 021 · The Price of a Signature

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Amiel Rusk. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/021-el-precio-de-la-firma.md`.

***

The woman who appeared in Amiel Rusk's office refused to sit down.

She carried an envelope inside her coat and kept looking toward the cabinet where the case files were stored. She was the same Estuary Cargo employee who had mentioned the messenger log, although Amiel hadn't written down her full name in front of Silven Rea at the time.

“My name is Oda Ferren,” she said. “I need you to see something before it disappears.”

Amiel closed the door but left the interior window open. Unscheduled visitors had to be recorded; sensitive statements had to be protected from prying eyes. Neither obligation entitled him to promise immunity.

Oda took out three sheets of carbon-copy paper. Their upper edges were torn, and each page retained a general-ledger number. There were dates when wage envelopes had been collected, crew identification numbers, and receipt signatures. The recipient column contained names Amiel knew from the dike investigation.

“Where did you get these?” he asked.

“From Silven's desk.”

“Did he give them to you?”

“No.”

“Did you copy them with his permission?”

Oda lowered her head.

“I went in after closing and took them from the filing cabinet. Nobody noticed.”

Amiel kept his hands away from the envelope. Authentic documents could be obtained in ways that violated privacy or the limits of an investigation. Urgency did not, by itself, make the means of access lawful.

“Why bring them to me?” he asked.

“Because they're moving the originals tomorrow. They said the distribution office would no longer operate at the port.”

“Who said that?”

“The head cashier. I can give you his name.”

Amiel asked her to describe her own actions first, without reading the sheets or repeating rumors about their contents. Oda explained that she worked on settlements for small cargoes. Her authorization covered entering receipts, not opening management's archives. After seeing a transfer notice, she'd used a key that wasn't hers.

“Do you understand that entry could be reported?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“I can't guarantee that your statement or these pages will be excluded from that examination.”

Oda tightened her grip on the envelope.

“If you won't take them, what happens to Liron and the others?”

Amiel thought of the young man at the clinic. There was a cruel distance between doing the right thing and arriving in time. He didn't want to pretend the law always closed it.

“I can take your statement about what you lawfully saw in the course of your work,” he said. “And I can seek a preservation order for the original archive. As for these sheets, I need to record their provenance and consult the court before deciding whether they can be admitted as evidence.”

“Then the company's lawyers will see them.”

“Possibly. I'm not going to deceive you.”

Oda stared at the envelope as though she'd just discovered how much it weighed.

***

Magistrate Yuna Tor heard the account that same afternoon. She wouldn't allow Amiel to read the contents of the sheets to her before deciding how they should be documented.

“Is there an independent source for the possible disappearance of the archive?” she asked.

“A transfer notice can be checked against the service register. There's also an earlier limited production order covering the messenger log.”

“And what did you know before the woman came in?”

Amiel listed the brass tokens, the crew names, and the wage settlements through Estuary Cargo. Every item was recorded in statements or documents obtained by regular requests.

Yuna made a note.

“You cannot make the contents of stolen sheets the sole foundation of an order. But we won't pretend the witness doesn't exist either. Record that she offered them, without examining them, and preserve the envelope separately. Authenticity and admissibility will be decided at a hearing.”

“Can I request preservation of the ledgers?”

“On the basis of the facts you already had, and the report of the transfer, properly identified. Not figures or names extracted from the disputed material.”

Amiel felt relief and frustration. The proper course required him, for now, to surrender an advantage that might have helped locate someone.

“What happens to Oda?” he asked.

“She isn't your instrument. Inform her of the risks and allow her to obtain advice. What she knows as an employee doesn't disappear because she committed an infringement. The value of each part must be distinguished.”

The magistrate signed an urgent order preserving the messenger log and crew four's payment entries. They could not be destroyed or moved without inventory and authorization, but the order did not freeze all commercial operations.

Amiel took it to the office with a clerk and two officers. Oda had agreed to remain at the courthouse to give a statement about facts she knew without unauthorized access.

Silven Rea received them in front of several workers waiting for their wages.

“Again?” he asked.

“I have an order to preserve specific documents.”

“The company needs to move its archives for reorganization.”

“You may apply for authorization to do that. The order requires an inventory first.”

Silven read every line, then pointed to the names Amiel had listed.

“Who reported them?”

“The request identifies facts under investigation, not sources I can disclose without a judicial decision.”

The man summoned his records manager.

***

The messenger log was in a wooden box with six clasps. Amiel made no attempt to open other cabinets. The clerk checked numbers, pages, and seals while a company representative witnessed the inventory.

Two pages were missing between Wednesday and Friday.

“Is that normal?” Amiel asked.

“Canceled pages are removed,” the manager replied.

“Where is that recorded?”

The man searched an index and found two references to replaced forms. One had a file number; the other, an incomplete annotation.

“It could be a binding error,” Silven said.

“It could,” Amiel replied. “We'll describe it as an absence requiring comparison.”

He hadn't needed Oda's sheets to notice the discrepancy. The ledger lay before four witnesses under a valid court order.

They also found a request in the outgoing-crate register to transfer files the following week. The company had applied for a wagon to a depot at Costa Interior. The destination matched that of tickets issued in workers' names, but the coincidence didn't prove the same people were traveling.

Amiel requested preservation of the document and its consignment number.

A box of wages was not a box of human beings.

That distinction had to survive even as the investigation began to build bridges between the records.

As they left, Silven accompanied him to the door.

“You're making it difficult for us to pay honest people,” he said.

“Which is why the order doesn't prevent you from paying wages. It prevents the loss of documents showing who was paid.”

“And who's paying for all this caution?”

Amiel thought of Liron, Oda, and the waiting families.

“That question should be recorded too.”

***

That evening, Oda asked to speak to him again. A public defender had visited her. She didn't want to withdraw her statement, but feared the company would discover she'd opened the cabinet.

Amiel explained the available safeguards: an identified statement kept under custody, limits on copying, and a request for protection if a concrete threat arose. He couldn't erase her participation from the case or make her an anonymous witness without grounds.

“And the sheets?” she asked.

“They're sealed. I haven't read them. The court will decide what to do with them.”

Oda took a deep breath.

“Maybe I shouldn't have taken them.”

“Maybe not. That doesn't make your firsthand account useless.”

She stared at the floor tiles.

“I delivered envelopes to men whose names I didn't know. I thought it was normal for a foreman to sign for everyone.”

“Did you keep your own shift notes?”

“Yes. Notes I made while I was working, not copies from a private archive.”

“Describe them to your defender. She can assess how to present them without putting you at further risk.”

Oda nodded and left in the same coat, but without the envelope.

Amiel opened his notebook and wrote down two separate questions. The first was whether the stolen sheets could be admitted. The second was why someone had wanted them removed before the investigators found the original ledger.

The first depended on the court.

The second could still be investigated using properly obtained evidence.

As he extinguished the lamp, a note arrived from the railway archive. The wagon reserved to move the ledgers would not wait until the following week. Its booking had been brought forward to the next dawn.

Amiel looked at the time.

The preservation order would be in time only if the seals were still where they'd left them.
