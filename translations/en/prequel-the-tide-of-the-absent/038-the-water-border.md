# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 038 · The Water Border

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Amiel Rusk. **Location:** Costa Interior border crossing, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/038-la-frontera-de-agua.md`.

***

The water border consisted of a bridge, two booths, and a line nobody had painted across the canal.

Amiel Rusk arrived at dawn with authorization to interview witnesses to the flood. Magistrate Yuna Tor had requested cooperation from the neighboring republic under the judicial assistance agreement between trading cities. The document allowed him to request voluntary statements; it didn't authorize him to take people away or seize documents on the other side.

At the landing, Orin Farel was waiting beside a moored barge.

“The patrol stopped four travelers yesterday,” he said. “Two of them might have seen the carts after the accident.”

“Who says so?”

“A woman from the route. She said she shared a shelter with them. I don't know whether they'll testify.”

Amiel adjusted his coat.

“Then first we'll find out what they want to do.”

Captain Rendal received them in the stone booth. On her desk were crossing records, a hydrographic map, and a jug of water nobody had touched.

“Your request arrived,” she said. “I can facilitate interviews on our territory with consent. I won't authorize transfers outside our jurisdiction until the documentation and the protection of minors have been resolved.”

“I haven't requested forced transfers,” Amiel replied.

“I know. But some officials from Cendra use the word ‘witness’ as though it gave them permission to move anyone.”

Amiel didn't argue with the warning. It was exactly the sort of confusion that could turn a legitimate investigation into another form of administrative disappearance.

Rendal showed him an incident list, not the full confidential details. Two adults had presented passes issued by Estuary Cargo; one contained a date incompatible with the departure record. Another was signed by an authorized representative with no documented power to approve the movement of people.

“Does that make them suspects?” Orin asked.

“No. It makes the passes questionable. I need to establish where they came from before validating the crossing.”

“And in the meantime?” Amiel asked.

“They can remain here, receive care, and request representation. They can't continue toward the Cendra post under those documents.”

The solution was inadequate for anyone who needed to return home, but it wasn't the same as criminal detention. Amiel asked that each person's status be accurately recorded.

***

The first witness agreed to speak in a room at the border post. He introduced himself as Nel Caris, a boat carpenter, and showed them a trade credential with a water-damaged corner.

“I was repairing wagons at the Costa Interior depot,” he said. “The morning after the flood, before dawn, I saw two covered carts arrive.”

Amiel let him describe the scene without interrupting.

Nel remembered that one cart had stopped beside a locked warehouse and an employee had unloaded three crates. He'd heard people speaking from the second cart, but didn't know how many there were or who they were.

“Did you recognize anyone?” Amiel asked.

“I didn't see any faces.”

“What time was it?”

“It was still dark. The first-shift bell rang afterward.”

Amiel recorded the time reference, not an exact hour. The bell could be compared with the factory log and that day's sunrise.

“Why do you want to cross to Cendra?” he asked.

“My wife is there. She took our papers when the flood began. I want to join her and give a statement about the crates.”

“We can take your statement here. Returning requires another procedure.”

Nel rubbed his face with both hands.

“For three days I've been hearing that everything requires another procedure.”

Amiel felt the helplessness of someone giving correct explanations but unable to shorten the distance between two people.

“I can request an urgent verification of your family relationship,” he replied. “I won't promise you'll cross today.”

The carpenter agreed to have his testimony recorded and to keep a copy.

***

The second person was a woman who'd worked as a cook for temporary crews. Her name was Marael Din, and she'd arrived at the border with a twelve-year-old boy who wasn't her son. The boy's mother was still at a dispensary, she explained.

Rendal had denied the crossing because no valid authorization established that Marael could travel with him.

“He asked me to take him,” the woman said. “He was conscious when he said it.”

“Is there anyone who witnessed that request?” Amiel asked.

“The nurse who treated her.”

The captain intervened.

“We're checking with the dispensary. Until then, I won't separate the minor from his companion or let them cross without confirmation.”

Amiel asked to speak first with the boy and his appointed advocate, not to question him about the accident, but to explain what he proposed to do. The boy looked exhausted. He said he wanted to go home and didn't want to talk about the carts.

“Then we won't talk about the carts,” Amiel replied.

Marael looked at him in surprise.

“And your investigation?”

“It doesn't depend on forcing him to remember something he doesn't want to discuss.”

The woman agreed to give her own statement. She'd served food to a crew on the afternoon before the flood. She'd received an order to prepare eleven ration parcels, which were collected in a cart without the recipients' names.

“Who signed for them?” Amiel asked.

“The kitchen foreman. I only handed over the parcels.”

“Do you have a note of the quantities?”

“I wrote it in the pantry book. If it hasn't been destroyed, it'll be there.”

Amiel noted the location and asked Rendal to route a preservation request. He couldn't barge into a warehouse in another republic on the strength of a preliminary statement.

The number eleven matched the tickets, accommodation forms, and evacuation list. Amiel had spent weeks learning that a match didn't mean identity. Eleven rations could feed eleven people, fewer or more, depending on how they were distributed.

He wouldn't turn a food tally into the fate of eleven workers.

***

At noon, a commercial representative arrived with a power of attorney from Estuary Cargo. He asked to collect two trunks held by the crossing inspection and claimed they contained work uniforms.

Rendal checked the shipping guide and the representative's identity. The documents allowed him to claim commercial goods, but the contents had been described in two different ways in the records of origin and destination.

“We'll open an inspection procedure under this post's regulations,” she said.

“They're goods belonging to a Cendra company,” the representative protested.

“They're in our jurisdiction and moving through a regulated crossing,” the captain replied. “Ownership doesn't exempt them from declaring their contents.”

Amiel watched the exchange without intervening. The port investigation didn't give him the right to take over a border inspection. He could request the preservation of relevant information once it had been lawfully obtained.

The representative asked for a copy of the report. Rendal granted it.

Then she had the Cendra officials leave during the inspection to avoid confusion about which authority was responsible. Amiel agreed to wait in the corridor with Orin.

“They don't seem to want to help us,” the skipper muttered.

“They help us by doing their job and keeping a record,” Amiel replied. “Not by lending us powers we don't have.”

When they finished, the captain reported that one trunk contained clean work clothes and the other ordinary tools. No documents or hidden people were found. The difference in the descriptions would be resolved through a commercial correction, not an invented accusation.

Orin looked at the water.

“Another lead that goes nowhere.”

“Another check that keeps us from chasing a false explanation.”

***

In the afternoon, Rendal authorized Ada Teren and her daughter to continue their journey, after completing the health and family verification begun at the dispensary. The authorization didn't change the status of the other cases.

Nel Caris was still waiting for his wife's address to be confirmed. Marael and the boy would remain under provisional protection until the mother's authorization was obtained.

Amiel recorded those three situations as separate decisions. There was no single group called “witnesses held at the border” that could be described in one sentence.

Before he left, the captain gave him a record concerning an old river route. The current bridge had been built over a crossing where a signal buoy for grain convoys had once operated.

“Your specialist asked about old equipment,” she said. “Here's the public maintenance archive. It doesn't include private commercial routes.”

Amiel thought of Vela.

“Is the buoy still operational?”

“On paper, no. It was taken out of service twelve years ago.”

“And physically?”

Rendal looked toward the canal.

“It's still anchored beside the inner channel. Skippers sometimes say it flashes. The technicians insist it's just reflections.”

Amiel put the record away.

At the landing, Orin returned to Barge Seven, his wages reduced by the correction to the cargo description. Amiel went back to Cendra without having transferred a single witness.

He hadn't failed to take statements. He'd found a border he couldn't cross simply because he wanted to solve a case.

The water passed beneath the bridge without stopping.

People needed rights, documents, and consent to do the same.
