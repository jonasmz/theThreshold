# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 041 · The True List

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Breda Lin. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/041-la-lista-verdadera.md`.

***

Breda Lin counted twenty-six receipts before she dared to write a conclusion.

They had been spread across the table in an auxiliary courtroom, arranged in the order in which they had been received. There were wage vouchers, flour receipts, and small oil-stained slips that the workers had kept because nobody had taught them to trust an office summary. None of them looked like decisive evidence.

Breda couldn't examine them as though she were still in charge of the Mutual's audit, either. Her external authority remained suspended, and her signature appeared on a certification whose scope she had just challenged before the magistrate. She had requested permission to appear as a technical collaborator, disclosing that conflict on the first page.

Yuna Tor authorized a limited reconciliation, supervised by an independent accounting expert named Sema Ordel. The originals would remain in the custody of their owners or the court. Breda would prepare hypotheses, not final opinions about her own work.

“Where do we start?” Sema asked.

“With the people who actually received something,” Breda replied. “Not with the amounts someone claims to have handed them.”

On the table lay a copy of the thirty-two sacks recorded by the marsh shelter and another from the supplier who had invoiced forty. There was also a payroll ledger for work crews at the northern depot, whose distribution account had passed through Estuary Cargo.

Seeing them together, Breda understood why it was so easy to confuse them.

All three sets used similar reference numbers, but they described different acts: purchasing goods, transporting them, and delivering them. A transport signature could prove that a load had been moved without proving that the person named on the invoice had received it.

“Let's build one column for each act,” she suggested.

Sema drew five headings: origin, means, recipient, supporting record, and outstanding discrepancy. Breda added another.

“Verification date. A sheet may have been completed later without making the original event false.”

***

The first worker to appear voluntarily was Solen, a sailor with Orin's cooperative. He carried two partial wage receipts and a note of the fuel he'd bought for Mara's tugboat in a cloth bag.

“I don't understand why you want to compare this with flour,” he said.

“Because a commercial account can group services that reach different people,” Breda explained. “We want to avoid attributing a payment to you that belonged to someone else.”

Solen showed them his receipt. The cooperative had paid part of his wages and acknowledged the rest as a debt. The document bore two signatures, a date, and an amount that matched the ledger Orin had submitted.

“Did you receive this sum?” Sema asked.

“Yes.”

“And the rest?”

“Not yet.”

Breda recorded an outstanding obligation, not an unexplained loss or a payment that had been made. They then reviewed the fuel receipt. The figure was supported by a supplier and a specific delivery. There was no reason to link it to the eleven transfers, even though it fell within the same accounting period.

Solen left, relieved that he wouldn't have to remember more than he knew.

The next witness was Oda Ferren, accompanied by her advocate. She had worked distributing Estuary Cargo's payment envelopes. She didn't submit the sheets she'd removed without authorization months earlier. She brought her ordinary shift notes and was willing to explain how the messaging codes were assigned.

“The envelope left the treasury with a number,” she said. “The foreman signed for it. Then he was supposed to distribute it to each worker.”

“Was an individual receipt returned?” Sema asked.

“In theory. On some shifts, they filed only the foreman's signature.”

Breda drew a line between two columns.

“Then what we have here is delivery to an intermediary, not proof of payment to the worker.”

Oda nodded.

“That's what I tried to say the first time.”

Breda felt a pang of shame as she remembered her own summary certifications. She, too, had turned a partial check into a statement that seemed to cover everything.

***

The third box contained ration receipts voluntarily provided by the shelter assembly. Talia had authorized copies of quantities and dates, with residents' names concealed when they weren't necessary for the reconciliation. Two witnesses had certified the count of thirty-two sacks.

Sema examined the invoice for forty.

“Where are the other eight?” she asked.

Breda searched for the second cart's waybill, which Edran had seen passing toward the bridge. She found a transfer entry to the Costa Interior dispensary. The receipt listed eight sacks, but acceptance had been signed for only by a transit agent.

“We can't attribute theft,” Breda said. “But we can't declare them delivered to the dispensary without its receipt, either.”

They requested confirmation through the administrative cooperation channel. Until it arrived, the discrepancy remained marked as outstanding.

Three women from the pumping stations then arrived. They had agreed to share specific pages from their shift notebooks. One showed that Fira had covered part of the shift attributed to Leda; another recorded a cash payment divided between two colleagues.

“Why weren't separate receipts issued?” Sema asked.

“Because the employer settled a single position,” Fira replied. “We divided what came in.”

Breda distinguished a payment actually received from an omission in the identity records. The system could be unfair without every participant having tried to obtain money improperly.

By midafternoon, Sema had calculated the totals.

The amounts confirmed by recipients were lower than the amounts recorded as settled, but not by the same proportion for every service. Some of the difference was explained by intermediaries, some by deferred payments, and some by documents that hadn't yet arrived.

“We can publish a table of statuses, not one single figure for diversion,” she concluded.

Breda agreed. A sensational number would have been easier to publicize. It would also have been less true.

***

The Mutual's director requested permission to attend the close of the reconciliation. Yuna allowed him to submit written observations, without giving him access to the protected identities of the witnesses.

The man argued that the reconstruction couldn't alter policies that had already been contracted.

“We're not deciding coverage for each vessel,” Sema replied. “We're only checking whether some service payments appear twice or lack individual proof of receipt.”

“A company may use legitimate intermediaries.”

“Of course,” Breda said. “Then there will be contracts, orders, and receipts that let us follow each step.”

The director turned toward her.

“You also approved summary certifications.”

“I disclosed that to the court. That fact doesn't make the missing checks complete.”

Yuna closed the discussion. The Mutual would have an opportunity to submit relevant originals, but it couldn't impose an accounting conclusion on the court.

Back at the table, Sema discovered a pattern Breda hadn't noticed: four wage claims from different crews referred to the same transport order number. The beneficiaries and amounts didn't match; the document used to justify the service did.

“Reference error?” Breda asked.

“Or a framework contract grouping several jobs. We need the annex and the receipts of acceptance.”

Breda recorded it as a new question.

The reconstruction was producing fewer certainties than she'd expected, but every uncertainty now pointed to a specific document that could help resolve it.

***

When the witnesses had left, Sema and Breda reviewed the table one last time. They had confirmed partial wages, rations actually received, and services that still needed to be checked. They hadn't located every holder of the eleven tickets or established what had happened to Erven Tal.

“It's not a true list,” Breda murmured.

Sema put down her pen.

“It's a list that says what we know and what we don't. That may be the truest thing we have.”

Breda looked at the column of protected names. The people who had handed over their receipts hadn't thereby given up their right to live in peace or keep their addresses private.

She asked that the public copy contain categories and amounts, not a list of shelter residents. The magistrate would keep a complete version under restriction for the necessary checks.

Sema agreed, recording the procedure.

Before they closed the file, a reply arrived from the dispensary: of the eight sacks recorded in transit, six had been received there. The other two appeared in a later shipment to a community kitchen, whose receipt they still hadn't located.

Breda reduced the outstanding discrepancy from eight sacks to two, without erasing the earlier calculation.

That correction seemed more important to her than any grand accusation.

The truth wasn't waiting, hidden in a perfect ledger. It had to be reconstructed without taking away anyone's wages, food, or right to be known by their own name.
