# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 004 · The City Without a Storm

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Vela Noren, with a marked scene break. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/004-la-ciudad-sin-temporal.md`.

***

The city awoke to a storm nobody remembered seeing.

Vela Noren read that sentence in the special edition of the port newspaper. The ink was still damp. On the front page, beneath an illustration of enormous waves, a statement from the administrator explained that an unexpected surge had exceeded technical forecasts and forced the staff to act quickly.

Vela folded the paper on the signal desk.

There hadn't been enough wind overnight to move the flag on the building.

That difference didn't make the statement a lie. Water could rise without a local storm if conditions changed upstream. But the chosen words were dangerous: they made everything sound inevitable before anyone had finished investigating.

“Have you seen the paper?” asked Peran, the incoming shift operator.

“I'm using it to check whether I still know how to read.”

Peran put a bag of bread on the counter.

“I brought two. I assumed you hadn't had breakfast.”

The central signal room occupied the upper floor of a brick tower. From there, operators received calls over wired telephones, dispatched lamp signals to the docks, and maintained a network of limited-range resonant transmitters. The Pulse in the small accumulators was enough to sustain short connections. The link to the outer lighthouse, by contrast, depended on a conventional cable running across the breakwater.

The night had left six incident sheets on the table.

Vela had signed five.

The sixth carried the number of a station that wasn't listed in the service directory.

“I want to review the wind records,” she said.

Peran pointed to the window.

“It's sunny.”

“Last night's records.”

“They're in the meteorology drawer.”

“The lighthouse's records too.”

Peran stopped opening the bread.

“What are you looking for?”

“An explanation for a storm without wind.”

The operator glanced at the paper and lowered his voice.

“The director asked us not to contradict the statement until we have a joint report.”

“I don't intend to contradict it. I intend to check it.”

The lighthouse records arrived twenty minutes later over the cable. The lighthouse keeper recorded speed, direction, and pressure every half hour because vessels entering from the gulf depended on that information. Between one and three in the morning, he had observed no dangerous variation.

Vela spread the sheet beside the tower's barometer.

They agreed.

So did the notebook of a harbor pilot who had passed through the entrance shortly after midnight. His report described calm water and excellent visibility.

Three independent observations. None, on its own, proved what had happened inside the basin. Together, they made it unlikely that a sea storm had been the immediate cause.

“Maybe the statement uses ‘storm’ figuratively,” Peran suggested.

“Then we're inventing a new profession for meteorologists.”

He laughed briefly. Then, without being asked, he took out the call log.

“What do you want to do with this?”

“Make a copy for the municipal investigation.”

“The central office might be annoyed.”

“They'll be more annoyed if people start building walls against a wind that never blew.”

Vela sat down at the copier. The machine pressed an inked strip against the service sheets. It was slow, awkward, and utterly incapable of correcting a sentence of its own accord. If it copied an error, it produced an identical error. That was one of the few virtues Vela allowed it.

As she worked, she remembered Neria's voice during the emergency.

“Water rising inside, not from the sea.”

The engineer had been unequivocal. Vela didn't know the actual condition of the gates, but she did know the condition of the signals that had arrived at her desk. At 2:06, six minutes before the first siren, someone had transmitted an all-clear message using the maintenance identifier.

The message was written on the receiving sheet. Peran had seen it and merely raised his eyebrows.

“Are you sure it was at 2:06?” he asked now.

“That's what the line clock shows.”

“There was a drop in power last night.”

“After 2:12.”

“Could it have shifted the clock?”

Vela went still.

She hadn't checked.

She stood, opened the timing mechanism's compartment, and pulled out the synchronization strip. Every quarter hour, a pulse sent from the central station marked an ink dot on the strip. The line clock was synchronized at 1:45. At two it showed a slightly displaced mark, but the difference was still within tolerance. The first missing mark appeared after 2:15.

The all-clear message hadn't been produced by a later power failure.

Peran studied the strip.

“Whoever spoke knew which identifier to use.”

“Or heard it from someone else,” Vela said. “Let's not attribute extraordinary intelligence to someone who may simply have had a telephone directory.”

She preserved the timing strip as well.

***

At noon, the director of communications summoned her to his office.

Arven Koss's office was full of photographs of boats. In one, a boy waved from the rail of a steamship; the frame was so polished that it looked newly installed. Vela had never asked whether the boy was his son.

“They've requested all of last night's signals,” Koss said.

“Who has?”

“The port administration and a municipal inspector. Through separate channels.”

“They should both receive them.”

“That is precisely the difficulty. If we give them copies they interpret in opposite ways, we'll end up accused of failing to cooperate.”

“We can't control what other offices do with the records.”

Koss folded his hands on the desk.

“We can make sure no preliminary information leaves here without context.”

“What context is missing?”

“The joint technical report.”

Vela understood that the discussion wouldn't be about whether the readings were true. It would be about when other people could be allowed to see them.

“The report can add context. It can't change what reached my console at 2:06.”

“No one is asking you to change anything.”

“Then preserving a copy shouldn't matter.”

The director looked at her wearily.

“Preserve what you're authorized to preserve. Synchronization tapes are not ordinary documents. They're recycled every forty-eight hours.”

“Last night's is relevant to an investigation into injuries.”

“In that case, request a preservation order.”

“Before it's recycled?”

“That's the procedure.”

Vela left with the feeling that she'd received a correct answer designed to produce the wrong result.

Down in the lobby, she found two women waiting beside the notice window. One carried a basket of dry clothes. The other held a photograph of a young man standing in front of a lighter.

“Do you work with the signals?” the older woman asked.

“Yes.”

“My nephew went out to the dike last night. They tell us every vessel was warned before the gate was opened.”

Vela thought of the 1:58 transmission listed in the evacuation register. She thought of the 2:06 all-clear and the lamp signals sent too late.

“There were warnings,” she answered. “We're still checking what each one said and who could have received it.”

The woman gripped the photograph.

“He didn't have a radio.”

“Then I need to know where he departed from and which signals he could see.”

The woman pointed to a corner of the picture.

“From Erven Tal's lighter. The skipper. Nobody will tell us where he is.”

Vela recognized the name. She had first heard it that morning while routing a call from the clinic to municipal investigations.

She could simply refer the women to the inspector. Or she could help them formulate a question that someone would be obliged to answer.

She took a clean sheet.

“Tell me the departure time you remember. And don't give me the one printed in the newspaper. Give me the time you remember.”

When the women left, she brought her notes back to the central room. Peran was putting equipment away in a cupboard.

“Did they give you permission to keep the tapes?” he asked.

“No.”

“So what are you going to do?”

“Request it in writing. With a stamped acknowledgment.”

Peran handed her the service sheet she had left on the copier.

“I checked the empty box you pointed out. It wasn't a misprinted number.”

“What was it?”

“A decommissioned station.”

Vela followed him to the old directory. Among yellowed pages, they found an identifier crossed out in black ink. It belonged to a siren on the storage canal, disconnected after renovations.

Vela remembered the sounds of the early morning. The first alarm had come from the west; the second, from the lower docks.

And before either of them, for a moment she'd attributed to distant machinery, she'd heard a third.

The retired frequency was written on her sheet.

“That can't be operating,” Peran said.

Vela read the identifier again.

“Someone ought to explain that to the siren.”
