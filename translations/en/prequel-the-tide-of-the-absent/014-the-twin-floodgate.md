# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 014 · The Twin Floodgate

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Neria Calden. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/014-la-compuerta-gemela.md`.

***

The second floodgate had the same defect as the first, but it hadn't caused an accident yet.

It took Neria Calden nearly an hour to admit it.

The North Brine station had been built over a narrow canal between fish warehouses and a pottery workshop. Unlike the eastern dike, its gate leaves weren't exposed to the full pressure of the basin. They regulated the exchange with an elevated reservoir that supplied industrial water to the factories. Under normal conditions, maneuvers were slow and could be scheduled in advance.

She had gone there to examine a calibration request listed among the Mareval Consortium's maintenance orders. Its date matched the repair carried out months earlier on the gauge at the eastern dike.

Irsa accompanied her. She carried a tool chest, a flow log, and a bag of bread she'd insisted on buying before they left.

“If another needle has been calibrated incorrectly, I want to find out why before someone decides to open something,” Neria said.

“And if they're all fine?”

“Then we'll have breakfast without writing a twelve-page report.”

The control-room door stood open. A worker named Beren was examining the casing of a conventional pump and seemed more concerned about an oil leak than about the arrival of two inspectors.

“The readings are within tolerance,” he reported.

Neria asked for the logs from the past three weeks. The pressure had fluctuated with the factories' shifts, but none of the increases resembled the surge at the eastern dike. The reservoir had auxiliary manual gates, and the water level could be seen from an external walkway.

It wasn't an exact replica of the system involved in the accident.

What was identical was the gauge housing.

On the side wall was an adjustment box fitted with square-headed screws, connected to the main dial by a short shaft. Irsa studied the tool marks.

“This one has been opened too.”

“The last calibration was two months ago,” Beren replied. “They had to open it.”

Neria didn't want to turn every wrench mark into a threat. She checked the housing's serial number and compared it with the purchase order. They matched. The installation used parts from the same batch as the eastern dike; the manufacturer had supplied twenty regulator assemblies during the modernization.

“I want to see the spillway retaining mechanism,” she said.

Beren put away his wrench.

“That requires shutting down the supply pump.”

“We're not dismantling it under pressure.”

“The pottery factory needs water until four.”

Neria checked the schedules. It was midmorning. They could reduce the flow for ten minutes if the plant had its own reserves; to do so legally, they needed to notify the factory's operator and record the maneuver.

There was no reason to create a new risk to save themselves a formality.

***

The pottery supervisor's name was Mave. Her hands were cracked, and she spoke in a way that forced people to listen until she finished.

“You can cut the supply for ten minutes,” she said. “But the kilns are cooling. If the water doesn't come back on time, we'll lose a day's work.”

Neria signed an authorization stating the latest permissible reconnection time and left a copy at the factory.

“If we don't finish, we'll stop the inspection. We won't extend the shutdown without consulting you.”

Mave gave her a distrustful look.

“That usually means someone will tell me afterward that there was no other choice.”

“Which is why I'm putting it in writing.”

They returned to the station and slowly reduced the flow using the auxiliary manual valve. An independent pressure gauge confirmed that pressure had fallen into the safe range before anyone intervened. Irsa fitted a mechanical lockout to the pump control, and Beren recorded the regulation accumulator's consumption. The reserve was sufficient to maintain the instruments and lighting, but not to force the entire floodgate open.

The retainer cover came off with difficulty.

Beneath it lay the stop on the return shaft. It was an iron component with a slot designed to hold a safety pin. Neria touched the surface with the tip of an inspection rod.

The pin was there.

But the edge of the slot had been filed down.

“Is it wear?” Beren asked.

Neria studied the direction of the striations. They were parallel, fine, and almost the same length. Wear caused by repeated pressure would have left irregular deformations in the contact areas.

“I don't know yet,” she answered. “It looks like tool work. We need to compare it with a reference part.”

Irsa took out the factory drawing.

According to the design, the slot had an almost square profile that prevented the pin from turning under pressure. On the installed component, one corner had been smoothed into a ramp. If the flow pushed against the retainer hard enough, the pin could slip out of its seat without breaking.

It was the same kind of vulnerability Toben had described after the emergency.

Neria felt a chill that had nothing to do with the wind.

“Who authorized this modification?” she asked.

Beren searched through the repair orders.

“There's a note about adjusting tolerances. It says the stop was rubbing after the shaft was replaced.”

“Who signed it?”

“External service. Mareval.”

Irsa held the component steady without dismantling it.

“If they altered it to stop it rubbing, maybe they didn't understand what they were sacrificing.”

Neria nodded. It could have been a poor repair rather than a conspiracy. The danger was real either way.

They photographed the profile from three positions. Beren fetched a new part from storage to compare the geometry. The difference was visible even without special instruments: one still had the corner; material had been removed from the other.

“We can't leave this component in service,” Neria said.

“The station can't stay shut for an entire week,” Beren replied.

“It won't have to. We can hold the position with an approved temporary lockout, if the municipality authorizes it and the load stays within limits. After that, we'll need to replace the retainer and test it without load.”

The technician seemed relieved to hear a practical proposal.

They slowly restored the flow before the factory's permit expired. The main gauge needle responded smoothly. The external scale confirmed that water was again reaching the reservoir.

Neria gave Mave a certificate of reconnection. They had used eight of the ten authorized minutes.

“If it's working properly, why are you so worried?” the potter asked.

“Because a part can work today and fail tomorrow when the pressure is different.”

Mave looked toward the factory.

“Then make sure someone changes it before tomorrow.”

***

At dusk, Neria brought the photographs and repair orders to the municipal inspections office. She couldn't remove the pin without compromising the station, so the original remained installed, immobilized by the temporary lockout. She had recorded who could access it and which checks would have to be carried out over the following shifts.

Amiel listened to her report while drinking coffee that had been cold for far too long.

“Can you state that someone prepared both floodgates to fail?” he asked.

“I can state that both had vulnerable retaining mechanisms. One is missing its pin. In the other, the pin's housing has been filed down. I don't know whether they have the same cause.”

“And the work orders?”

“Both records mention an adjustment of tolerances. I need the workers' names and the original orders.”

Amiel wrote down the request.

“I'll ask for the records to be preserved. Physical intervention will depend on industrial safety requirements and whoever has jurisdiction over the station.”

Neria pointed to a third line in the inventory.

“There's another floodgate supplied from the same batch of spare parts.”

“Where?”

“On the canal leading to the riverside reservoirs.”

Amiel lifted his head.

“The one beside the old shelter?”

“That one.”

Neria unfolded a maintenance map. In the margin was a repair scheduled for the following day. The contractor's signature was illegible, but the work code matched the one used at the other two stations.

She couldn't be certain they'd find a third defect. Nor could she risk discovering it during the next flood.

“We need to inspect it before we authorize any more maneuvers,” she said.

Amiel looked at the clock.

“The Council debates the concession tomorrow.”

Neria studied the map.

“Then we'll have to explain that they aren't just buying a contract. They're deciding who inspects the parts before someone trusts them with their life again.”
