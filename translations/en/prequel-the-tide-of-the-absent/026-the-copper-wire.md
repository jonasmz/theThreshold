# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 026 · The Copper Wire

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Vela Noren. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/026-el-hilo-de-cobre.md`.

***

It took Vela Noren three days to get two clocks to argue in front of a witness.

The first belonged to the Port Cendra signal tower. Its pendulum gained a few seconds each week, and the staff corrected the drift every morning. The second was at Kelor Dam, where an operator had recorded readings from a reservoir supposedly out of service. They couldn't place the clocks side by side: several hours' travel, three repeaters, and a succession of ledgers that didn't always use the same time lay between them.

Vela brought the synchronization certificates to the municipal laboratory. Peran was waiting at the table with an independent expert authorized by the court. Her name was Amina Dorel, and she didn't appear impressed by the sealed boxes.

“Before we listen to anything, I want to know which device produced each line,” she said.

Vela pointed to the medium they'd rescued from the rain. The spool from the dike repeater had been dried in a ventilated chamber without direct heat. Its edges remained distorted. The laboratory had preserved photographs of its initial condition and a humidity log.

“It's incomplete,” she warned. “If we try to read the middle section, we might lose information.”

“Then we won't start there.”

They connected the reading head to a calibrated accumulator with limited capacity. The machine didn't interpret thoughts or bring voices from unknown places. It recorded variations in impulses that operators had sent along a physical wire, preserved as traces on a medium. The technician measured the power required and noted that they would not exceed the spool's threshold.

The first section yielded ordinary impulses: dock confirmations, cargo notices, and a scheduled test. Vela recognized the cadence of the decommissioned signal she'd detected on the night of the flood.

Peran leaned closer.

“That could be the test announced the day before.”

“Could be,” Vela replied. “We have to distinguish the test from the actual transmission.”

Amina asked them to separate the records from each source. A matching code wasn't enough to establish that a cable had been used for an unlawful purpose. They needed to check which station transmitted, which repeated the signal, and which device received it.

Vela spread the old directory on the table. The channel assigned to the riverside reservoir was listed as canceled. Yet a commercial account had continued to pay for its maintenance, and now there were readings from a supposedly inactive station.

The expert drew three columns on a sheet.

“Origin time, repetition time, and identified destination. If any is missing, we won't invent a route.”

***

The first comparison was disappointing.

The Kelor records showed that a sensor had transmitted a reading during the night. The Port Cendra tower had no acknowledgment from that identifier, although it did have an entry with a partially matching number. The water-damaged spool provided only a fragment.

“It might be a transcription error,” Peran said.

“Or a reused address,” Vela added.

Amina asked them to locate the wiring diagram produced after the reservoir canal was renovated. It was filed with the municipality, not the tower. Vela had requested a copy two days earlier and was still awaiting an answer.

A messenger arrived around noon with a certified envelope.

On the drawing, the sensor line didn't terminate at the main tower. It passed first through an auxiliary repeater installed in a hut beside the former shelter, then divided toward the console and a commercial branch line.

The hut was listed as disconnected in the public inventory.

Vela compared the terminal numbers with the Mareval maintenance invoice found in the archive. The length of cable matched, although the identity of the final user remained unknown.

“We need the auxiliary repeater's log,” she said.

“Perhaps there isn't one,” Peran replied.

“If the equipment was operating, it had to consume energy and leave some measurable effect. Not necessarily a spool, but at least a charge or a relay position.”

Amina closed her notebook.

“And we need authorization to examine it. A drawing doesn't give us permission to enter someone else's property.”

The court extended the inspection to the identified hut, with municipal technicians and the tenant present. The examination took place that afternoon. Vela traveled with Peran and Amina, accompanied by a clerk.

The shelter was still closed. Its windows were boarded up and its steps covered with dust. Around the hut's lock, however, there was a clean mark. A representative of the managing company opened the door with his key and placed on record that he disputed the need for the inspection.

Inside there was no mysterious transmitter. There was a bank of relays, a small accumulator, a recently replaced fuse, and a three-position route selector.

One was marked “TOWER.” Another, “DOCK.” The third bore a commercial number.

Vela photographed the position in which they found it before anyone touched anything.

***

The relay's operations could be counted without switching it on. Every changeover left a mechanical mark on a numbered wheel. The system was designed to retain its most recent operations, not the full contents of every signal.

The wheel showed two groups of notches separated by a long interval. Amina compared the sequence with the declared maintenance and the time of the last fuse replacement.

“We can't know who moved the selector,” she said. “But we can determine whether the route changed.”

Vela examined the marks through a magnifying glass.

The rest position indicated in the guide was “TOWER.” During an ordinary test, a spring returned the selector to that position. In the device they found, the spring had been immobilized with a clamp, leaving the contact directed toward the commercial number.

“Could it have failed on its own?” the clerk asked.

Vela pointed to the clamp's screws.

“The part was fitted to prevent automatic return. Why it was fitted, and who fitted it, are separate questions.”

Amina measured the contacts without powering the circuit. One connected the Kelor sensor to the tower; another took the same impulse to the commercial branch. The third allowed a local acknowledgment to be sent even if the message never reached the console.

It explained a possibility they hadn't been able to state precisely before: a sensor could transmit a genuine reading, and the device could deliver it to another recipient while returning an acknowledgment that appeared normal.

The test on the previous day appeared in the test marks. After it, a manual intervention was recorded before the flood. The position in which they found the selector didn't match the route authorized by the service drawing.

Amina wrote carefully: “Deliberate switching of the circuit by physically immobilizing the selector.” She did not write “sabotage of the floodgate.”

Vela felt part of the noise of the past few days begin to fall into order.

“So the reading from the closed station could have reached the commercial branch instead of the municipal one,” she said.

“Could have,” Amina replied. “We'll check the receiving end. The alteration of the route is documented. Where each impulse actually went requires further evidence.”

The hut was sealed, and its power supply isolated after they confirmed no safety equipment depended on it. The accumulator retained a small charge, enough to power relays, not to operate a floodgate.

***

Back at the tower, Vela found Koss waiting in his office.

“The court wants an explanation about the signals on the day of the accident,” the director said. “It says we had a decommissioned circuit that was still operating.”

“We have a line being administered outside ordinary reports,” Vela replied. “The report will distinguish measurements from assumptions.”

Koss rubbed his eyes.

“If this becomes public, they'll say our tower allowed the flood.”

“Allowing someone to divert a warning isn't the same as opening a valve. But if we conceal the inspection, we won't be able to correct anything either.”

The director didn't argue. He handed her a folder listing the people who had signed maintenance records for the auxiliary repeater.

They included a tower technician, a company technician, and a municipal supervisor. They held three different responsibilities, spread over months. Vela didn't recognize every signature.

On the last page was a service order: restore the selector to its normal route at the end of a test.

There was no record that it had been carried out.

Vela took a sealed copy for Amina.

The first solid evidence wasn't that somebody had predicted the future. It was smaller and more serious: a component had been deliberately fixed in place to send information along a route other than the one authorized.

And in an emergency, the route a warning took could matter as much as the warning itself.
