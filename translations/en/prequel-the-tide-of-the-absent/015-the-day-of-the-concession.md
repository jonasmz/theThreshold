# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 015 · The Day of the Concession

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Neria Calden. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/015-el-dia-de-la-concesion.md`.

***

The Civic Council of Port Cendra had placed twelve water pitchers on the debate table.

Neria Calden counted eleven representatives present and one empty seat. She had slept little for three nights and needed to save her attention for what she could prove.

The chamber smelled of varnish and wet wool. Residents arrived in coats soaked by the wind off the docks. The upper gallery was crowded with floodgate workers, families from the low-lying district, and members of the tugboat cooperative. Orin Farel stood beside Mara Veis. Dalia held a folder and did not look inclined to leave before someone answered her questions.

The chair announced the day's business: the extraordinary concession for the repair and maintenance of port facilities.

The proposal would give the Mareval Consortium coordination of urgent works for six months, with additional charges for backup equipment and a shortened procedure for contracting replacement parts. The municipality would retain the power to inspect, but technical reports would initially be routed through the contractor.

Neria had underlined that sentence at breakfast.

If the contractor received reports on its own interventions first, a failure might not reach the authorities until there were no parts left to examine.

The port administrator presented the justification. He read figures for commercial losses and spoke of restoring suppliers' confidence. Then he described the flood as “an exceptional natural occurrence.”

A murmur ran through the gallery.

Neria leaned toward Amiel Rusk, seated beside her as an observer for the municipal investigation.

“That conclusion hasn't been established.”

“I know,” he replied. “If they give you the floor, describe the measurements. Don't argue about his intentions.”

The inspector carried a blue folder. He couldn't tell the council members how to vote. He could submit a judicial request to preserve equipment if anyone attempted to repair it before expert examination.

Neria hadn't come to convince the chamber that everyone was guilty. She had come to stop a political decision from making the modifications at three stations irreversible.

***

Loden Vair, Mareval's representative, was the first to address the Council.

He carried a list of available workers and a repair schedule. The proposal was convincing in its details. There were crews, locomotives to transport iron, conventional generators, and backup accumulators. None of those resources was imaginary. The consortium had equipment the municipality would need even if it changed contractors.

“We are not debating whether irregularities occurred,” Loden said. “The competent authorities will investigate them. We are debating whether a city can afford to suspend trade until they finish.”

Neria recognized the question she'd avoided answering for days. Keeping dangerous equipment in service could injure workers; shutting it down without alternatives could deprive those same workers of their income and leave factories without industrial water.

A councilwoman asked how long an independent inspection would take.

Loden answered: several weeks.

Neria raised her hand. After consulting the rules, the chair granted her five minutes to speak as the municipal technical supervisor.

“The initial inspection of the three retaining mechanisms can be done sooner,” she said. “I'm not proposing that we dismantle all the infrastructure. I'm proposing that we identify critical components, prohibit risky maneuvers, and preserve the originals for expert examination.”

“Can you guarantee there won't be interruptions?” the councilwoman asked.

“No. I can describe which interruptions would be necessary and which reserves we have. Guaranteeing uninterrupted service without examining the parts would mean pretending to know something I don't.”

Neria explained the difference between an altered indicator and genuinely elevated pressure. She showed photographs taken in the presence of witnesses, the preservation order for the eastern dike, and the filed-down profile of the retainer at Salmuera Norte. She did not claim that the same person had tampered with both stations.

“In the first, the pin was missing,” she concluded. “In the second, it was present, but its housing had been modified. These are different defects that can create a similar vulnerability. We don't yet know whether they share a cause.”

A representative asked whether she wanted to take all of Mareval's contracts away.

“I want no one to certify their own repair when that repair is under investigation,” she answered.

The remark drew applause. The chair called for silence.

Orin raised the petition signed at the dock assembly. A clerk confirmed it had been submitted within the deadline. Orin was allowed to read the requests: assistance for the injured, an inventory of those affected, an independent assessment, and public access to the terms of the concession.

The chair replied that the first three matters could be handled through existing channels.

“Then record who will be responsible for answering them,” Orin requested. “A channel with no name is a corridor that ends at another service window.”

Neria saw several council members exchange looks. The intervention proved nothing about the cause of the accident, but it prevented them from treating its consequences as a minor appendix.

***

During the recess, Amiel received a note from the court. He showed it to Neria without handing over the original.

Magistrate Yuna Tor had ordered the preservation of the paper-withdrawal records and the concession annexes, without ruling on their validity. A certified copy could be added to the hearing file; the originals were to remain in the custody of the office holding them until a joint inventory could be made.

“What's in those annexes?” Neria asked.

“We're still comparing them. The evacuation order and some of the works forms use paper from the same print run. That alone doesn't prove forgery.”

“Are you going to ask them to postpone the vote?”

“The magistrate hasn't ordered that. Nor could she do so merely because the paper looks similar. It's a political decision subject to legal limits.”

Neria looked again toward the Council table. They needed an alternative that could be voted on without waiting for a judgment.

In a corridor she found Fara Nel, a representative of the industrial districts, studying the proposal beside a window.

“If you reject the contract,” Fara said, “the factories will be without water while we look for another supplier. Will you undertake to sign a statement that the risks are acceptable?”

“No,” Neria answered. “I'll undertake to sign off on which maneuvers we can maintain and which must remain suspended. We can contract temporary assistance without surrendering control of inspections.”

Fara pointed to the rates in the file.

“And how much would it cost?”

Neria recognized the prices of the components. Some were reasonable; others included unexplained urgency surcharges.

“Make payment depend on materials actually received and hours verified. Not on a blanket percentage of the estimated damage.”

Fara looked at her.

“That doesn't resemble the text they want to vote on.”

“Then they need a different text.”

***

The debate went on until dark. A proposal to suspend the entire concession failed for lack of a majority. The second proposal, introduced by Fara, divided the contract: it would authorize forty-five days of assistance to maintain essential services, while reserving to the municipality the selection of experts, custody of parts, and approval of any irreversible repair.

Mareval would still be able to supply equipment and crews. It would not receive exclusive authority to declare what had happened.

Loden objected that the arrangement duplicated expenses. Other representatives replied that the cost of another accident would be greater.

The measure passed by one vote.

There was no full celebration in the gallery. The workers needed to know whether they'd be paid. The families wanted answers about their missing loved ones. Orin shook Mara's hand but kept looking anxiously at the concession papers.

Neria felt a brief relief. They had bought time to examine the installations without stopping all port activity.

When the session ended, the municipal clerk gave Fara a copy of the earlier file to document the amended clauses. The councilwoman asked Neria to check the technical annex, fearing she might have overlooked a reference to the retaining mechanisms.

Neria read the budget items in the corridor.

There was one section for preventive maintenance, another for energy reserves, and another for compensating vessels. The last listed seven insured transport units at an identical rate. She didn't know their names, only their coverage numbers.

At the foot of the annex was a preparation date preceding the flood.

“It could be an earlier proposal,” Fara said.

“Yes. That wouldn't be unusual.”

Neria pointed to a specific line: “Replacement of retaining assembly at eastern floodgate following emergency discharge.”

The preparation date preceded the maneuver by more than a day.

They might have anticipated a preventive replacement and described the work in new words afterward, without changing the template date. It could be a revised version attached to an old cover sheet. It might also mean somebody had expected a particular discharge.

She had no evidence with which to choose among those explanations.

Fara asked the clerk to identify the version history. He returned with a circulation record: the cover was dated before the accident, but the annex had been replaced the following day. There was no record of who had approved the change.

Neria felt the relief of the vote evaporate.

“This sheet can't be used to prove a prediction,” she said. “But it can be used to ask who changed a budget we're now being asked to pay.”

Fara nodded and requested a certified copy for the court.

As they left the building, the lamps around the square were lighting one by one. Neria saw Dalia waiting beside the dry fountain.

“Did you win?” she asked.

“We made sure someone will examine the parts before they're replaced.”

Dalia pressed her folder against her chest.

“Then tomorrow, ask who also put a price on the boats we still haven't found.”

Neria didn't know what to say.

Across the square, a messenger was leaving the Council with the register of emergency contracts. Under the new agreement, every expense would have to be justified.

For the first time, that might become a problem for those who had expected to profit before the water had even finished receding.
