# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 036 · Barge Seven

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Orin Farel. **Location:** Convoy bound for Costa Interior, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/036-la-barcaza-siete.md`.

***

The riverside convoy carried no passengers on its manifest.

Orin Farel confirmed this by reading the list of vessels scheduled to depart the secondary port for Costa Interior. Seven barges, two tugboats, and an inspection launch had permission to travel through the marsh channels. Number seven referred to a wide, low-sided platform that the cooperative had repaired months earlier.

“I need to board that barge,” Orin said.

The convoy supervisor, Davor Men, didn't look up from the list.

“We don't carry visitors.”

“I'm looking for the record of a lighter that appeared on the route after the flood.”

“Ask the archives.”

“We already did. They only keep the commercial manifest, not each skipper's reports.”

Davor raised his head.

“Then that's not my problem.”

Orin had arrived with a recommendation from Mara and a statement confirming his cooperation with the municipal investigation. Neither document authorized him to board a private vessel. He could request an interview, not demand one.

“I can work as an assistant pilot,” he offered. “I know the current at Willow Crossing.”

Davor studied his hands.

“Your boat is still in dry dock.”

“My boat is. I'm not.”

The supervisor considered the proposal. His regular pilot had been injured, and the convoy had to pass between two pillars shifted by the flood. An experienced sailor could save fuel and prevent an accident.

“I'll pay you for one maneuver,” he said. “But I have a condition.”

He led Orin to a warehouse where boxes of blankets, soap, and kitchen utensils were waiting. One was intended for a marsh dispensary. The delivery note described the goods as used clothing, although the actual contents were empty glass bottles for preparing cleaning solutions.

“There's nothing dangerous,” Davor explained. “They're clean containers. If we declare them now, the port will charge another tariff and delay our departure.”

Orin opened his mouth, then closed it.

He'd recently refused three crates without manifests, and that refusal had cost him wages. Now he was being asked to accept an inaccurate description of a load he could inspect.

“I'm not going to certify something I know is wrong,” he said.

“I'm not asking you to sign the manifest. I'm asking you not to mention that crate while it's on board.”

It was a legal distinction and a moral trap.

***

Mara was waiting beside the ramp. When Orin explained the condition, she stared at the water.

“You could refuse and lose access,” she said.

“I could.”

“Or you could board and pretend you never saw the bottles.”

“And then ask another skipper to trust me when I report a misdeclared load.”

Mara pointed to barge number seven.

“Not every irregularity carries the same risk. An empty bottle isn't a loaded regulator.”

“That doesn't make the description correct.”

They both knew the consequences of rules applied without judgment. The cooperative had spent weeks fighting to obtain contracts, and a minor administrative issue could prevent a dispensary from receiving containers needed to treat the injured.

Orin returned to the warehouse and asked Davor to let him inspect the goods in front of a witness. The bottles were clean, with no visible odors or residue. A health-care aide confirmed that they would be used as containers, not to transport hazardous reagents.

“We need a supplementary declaration,” Orin said. “They can remain grouped with the medical supplies. We don't need to open every box in front of the revenue officers.”

Davor shook his head.

“The fee will go up even if we write a single word.”

“Then the cooperative will pay the difference out of my wages for this maneuver.”

The supervisor looked at him as though he'd lost his mind.

“You're going to work for free to pay a tax?”

“I'm going to work to find out what transport is happening on this route. And so the next skipper won't have to guess what he's carrying.”

Davor called the clerk. They submitted a correction to the contents under the same shipping guide, without changing the destination or recipient identification. The operation lost forty minutes, and Orin lost most of his pay.

Before departure, Davor gave one final warning.

“You can talk to my crew. You can't search cabins or private books.”

“I know.”

***

Barge Seven moved slowly through a channel that seemed made of shadows. Water brushed the reeds, and every so often the tugboat had to reduce power to keep the current from driving the platform against a sandbank.

Orin worked from the bow. He marked the depth with a pole and gave the helmsman instructions through agreed signals. There was nothing extraordinary about the maneuver, except for the patience it demanded.

When they reached the old bridge, a sailor pointed toward the bank.

“Carts came that way the morning after the flood,” he said.

His name was Iral, and he'd worked the inland routes for eight years.

“Did you see them?” Orin asked.

“I saw one. It was covered with a tarp, and two men were following it on foot.”

“What was it carrying?”

“I don't know.”

Orin thanked him and asked him to be more precise about the date and time. Iral remembered that the convoy that morning had left late because a passage signal was out. His personal tide book contained an entry: “Bridge, wait for cart. Passage clear after dawn.”

Orin didn't ask for the notebook. He asked whether he could copy the line and identify its author. Iral agreed.

“Do you know whether it was carrying people?” Orin asked.

“I heard someone coughing behind the tarp, but it could have been anyone.”

The temptation to imagine Erven Tal, injured inside that cart, came on strongly. Orin pushed it aside.

A cough wasn't a name.

Farther along, the barge passed an abandoned vessel tied to a piling. There was a red stripe on its side and a partly erased cargo number. Iral explained that several platforms had been removed from the route after the accident while their permits were being checked.

“Was Seven here that night?” Orin asked.

“Not the one we're sailing now. Back then they used another one with the same convoy number.”

“Where is it?”

“At the Costa Interior depot, according to the owner.”

The distinction mattered. Number seven identified a convoy position, not necessarily a unique hull. The manifest Orin had consulted might record a voyage without precisely identifying which platform had been used.

***

When they reached the landing, Davor let him examine the delivery reports for the day on which the disputed transfer had taken place. These weren't documents seized under judicial order; the supervisor voluntarily placed them on a table and indicated which ones could be consulted.

One page recorded seven bundles of provisions. Another listed a toolbox. The third contained a crossed-out line described as “accommodation equipment.”

“What does that expression mean?” Orin asked.

“Blankets, cots, kitchen supplies. The shipowner uses broad codes.”

“Why was it crossed out?”

“The recipient didn't sign for delivery. The cargo went back onto the route.”

The paper bore a return stamp and a signature that was difficult to read. Orin couldn't be certain these bundles were the same ones listed in Estuary Cargo's assistance contracts. The date and route justified a question; they weren't enough to make an accusation.

A sailor approached the supervisor.

“Two men from the border post have arrived. They want to speak to the passengers.”

Davor spread his hands.

“We don't have passengers.”

Orin remembered the initial manifest. Barge Seven carried no registered passengers that day. His own presence as a temporary pilot was listed on the crew sheet and could be verified.

“Why are they asking about passengers?” he said.

“Someone reported that people were coming from the shelter,” the sailor replied.

Davor stood up.

“There won't be any transfers without permits. That's for the authorities to decide, not my barge.”

Orin followed him to the ramp. Several families were waiting at the landing with bags of clothes and a woman carrying documents in a waterproof sleeve. They weren't trying to board secretly. They were waiting for someone to explain what steps they needed to take.

The woman recognized Orin from the dock assembly.

“We were told we could give statements about the flood here,” she said. “But the patrol won't let us continue.”

Orin looked at the channel separating the two administrative banks.

He'd gained access to a convoy to follow the trail of a transport operation. Instead of finding definitive proof, he'd arrived at another border where papers could weigh more than people.

He put his copy of the tide book away.

For the first time in days, he knew the next step wasn't something a tugboat skipper could take on his own.
