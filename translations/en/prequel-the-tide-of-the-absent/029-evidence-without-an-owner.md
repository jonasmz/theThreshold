# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 029 · Evidence Without an Owner

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Amiel Rusk. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/029-la-prueba-sin-dueno.md`.

***

The seal was intact, but it wasn't the seal they'd applied.

Amiel Rusk studied it for several seconds before accepting what he was seeing. The evidence box arrived at the courthouse from the hut beside the former shelter, with a strip of brown paper around its lock. The inspection inventory listed a blue strip, numbered and signed by the expert Amina Dorel.

“Who authorized the replacement?” he asked.

The carrier looked as bewildered as Amiel felt.

“That's how I received it at the judicial depot.”

“Did you collect it personally from the laboratory?”

“From the delivery office. They gave me a receipt.”

Amiel didn't open the box. He ordered it placed on a separate table under guard and asked a clerk to photograph all six sides. The box contained the physical selector from the auxiliary repeater, dismantled after an authorized inspection. The position of its clamp had helped explain how certain impulses could be diverted away from the municipal route.

It was one of the most important pieces of technical evidence they'd found.

And someone had broken its chain of custody at some point during the transfer.

“I need every receipt from the hut onward,” he said.

The carrier showed him the last one. It had a date, time, recipient, and the carrier's signature. It appeared correct. The preceding document recorded entry into the judicial depot. Beside it was a penciled note: “Repacked for safety.”

There was no name identifying who had done it.

The clerk asked whether they should notify the magistrate.

“Now,” Amiel replied. “And the laboratory. Nobody touches the contents until we decide how they can be examined.”

He didn't know whether the component had been altered, replaced, or simply moved into a sturdier box by someone who'd forgotten to record the procedure. All three possibilities affected its evidentiary value differently.

The problem was that he could no longer choose among them.

***

Yuna Tor arrived with Amina and two depot officials.

The expert recognized the outer packaging but refused to authenticate the brown seal. She produced her copy of the original report: the selector had been photographed inside the hut, dismantled in front of witnesses, and placed in a wrapper marked in blue ink.

“I signed this strip,” she said. “The one on the table isn't mine.”

The depot chief insisted the box had arrived with one damp side. An employee had suggested reinforcing it because the wood was beginning to give way.

“Who removed the seal?” Yuna asked.

The man consulted the ledger.

“There's no entry recording an opening.”

“Who had a key to the room?”

“Three supervisors, the guard shift, and the head of maintenance.”

“Then we'll begin with those who could have intervened and the preservation instructions.”

Amiel didn't want the incident turned into a hastily improvised hunt for culprits. He also knew the institution might try to protect itself by blaming the weakest carrier, an orderly, or an employee who'd never had access to the contents.

Yuna ordered a joint adversarial inventory: representatives of the defense, the investigation, and the expert team could attend the opening at a special hearing. Until then, the object would be isolated, and the intake records preserved unchanged.

Amiel produced the original photographs taken inside the hut before dismantling. Amina explained what they showed: a clamp immobilizing the selector away from the authorized position, a changeover wheel, and marks of manual intervention.

“Are the photographs still usable?” Yuna asked.

“Their provenance is documented,” the expert replied. “But they don't allow me to repeat every measurement I made with the original mechanism. And those measurements were linked to an object whose material identity must now be verified again.”

Amiel felt the force of the words.

The investigation hadn't lost all its evidence. For now, it had lost the ability to present the transported selector as unquestionably the same component Amina had examined.

***

The hearing took place the following day.

The tenant's lawyer asked for the selector to be excluded from the technical file. He argued that any mark discovered after transport could not safely be attributed to the device observed inside the hut. The municipal representative maintained that the photographs, the report, and expert testimony were sufficient to preserve its value.

Yuna heard both sides.

“One thing is to establish that a device had a particular configuration during the inspection,” she said. “Another is to guarantee that the object offered for examination today is the same one and hasn't been altered in custody. We'll decide each issue separately.”

The box was opened before those present.

Inside was a selector that looked identical to the photographed one. The clamp was there. But one screw had a scratch Amina didn't recognize, and a new label partially covered the serial number.

That wasn't enough to prove substitution. It was enough to prevent the expert from certifying its condition without reservations.

“I can't be certain it remained unaltered,” she testified.

Yuna ruled that the object would be preserved as material with disputed provenance. For the time being, it could not be used as conclusive proof of the selector's position during the emergency. The photographs and statements taken in the hut would continue to be examined according to their own chain of origin.

The company's lawyer requested that the entire signal report be discarded.

“No,” the magistrate replied. “A lapse in custody doesn't automatically invalidate documents obtained independently. Nor can we ignore that lapse when evaluating them.”

Amiel wrote down the ruling without lifting his head.

They had obtained a piece of evidence whose examination could be repeated, and then, through a poorly recorded procedure, reduced it to testimony, photographs, and questions.

Nobody had needed to hide the selector beneath the sea.

All it took was replacing a strip of paper without writing down who had done it.

***

Afterward, Vela found him in the corridor. She carried the tower's signal register under one arm and looked more sad than angry.

“Does none of what we measured count?” she asked.

“What we can substantiate from its sources still counts,” Amiel replied. “The configuration photographed in the hut remains an observed fact. But we can't treat the mechanism that reached the hearing as though it had remained continuously sealed.”

“It was the first time I could show the diversion without asking somebody to trust my ear.”

“We still have the drawing, Amina's recorded continuity tests, and the test sheets. We need to compare them with independent records.”

Vela leaned back against the wall.

“Someone's going to say we invented the signal.”

“Perhaps. We'll have to answer by showing what's established and what isn't.”

The inspector had said similar things to families of missing people, workers without contracts, and citizens eager to blame someone before a hearing. They had never sounded so inadequate to him.

Vela produced a sheet.

“Peran found a copy of the hut's energy account. It doesn't depend on the selector they transported. Consumption rises when the commercial route is active.”

“Who signed it?”

“The billing supervisor. We need the original and a calibration test for the meter.”

Amiel took it without treating it as automatic salvation. If the figures withstood comparison, they'd help demonstrate that the circuit had consumed energy during particular intervals. The operator's identity would still be unknown.

“I'll request preservation,” he said.

Vela nodded.

“Then we're not finished.”

***

That night Amiel examined the judicial depot ledger.

He discovered something no one had mentioned at the hearing: on the day the seal was changed, the storage room had been closed for forty minutes for a safety inspection. The entry listed a start and end time, but the responsible person's signature was written on a supplementary sheet missing from the file.

Amiel requested preservation of the original supplement. He didn't accuse the person who'd closed the room. An inspection could have been legitimate; the absent sheet might be the result of incomplete filing.

Leaving the depot, he passed the clinic where the people injured at the dike were still receiving care. A nurse asked whether the investigation had made progress.

Amiel thought of the box, the blue strip, and the brown one.

“Enough to show where we're missing an assurance,” he replied.

The woman shook her head.

“The injured can't eat assurances.”

He had no answer.

Back at his desk, a note from Yuna awaited him. It authorized a strictly limited request for the security ledger and the custody staff's shift rosters. This was an investigation into the integrity of evidence, not yet into the accident.

Amiel placed the note beside the report from the hut.

The central component of a hypothesis had been weakened.

But the question remained alive: who had moved the signal, and why was the record of that movement now so difficult to preserve?
