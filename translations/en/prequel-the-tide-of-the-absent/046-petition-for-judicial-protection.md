# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 046 · Petition for Judicial Protection

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Amiel Rusk. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/046-solicitud-de-amparo.md`.

***

The archive smelled of wet paper and hot iron. Amiel Rusk descended the stairs with a portable lamp and found two employees carrying books onto a table that was beginning to tilt.

A broken pipe was flooding the municipal basement. Building permits, assistance contracts, and the original records for the eastern gate were stored there. The safety office had ordered the building evacuated before nightfall.

“The consortium has offered to take everything to its depot,” Lesten, the archivist, said. “They have carts and space.”

Amiel saw Mareval's seal on the transport form. He had no evidence that the company intended to take possession of the documents. But he couldn't allow a party connected to the files to become their sole custodian without oversight, either.

“What inventory have you prepared?”

Lesten showed him an incomplete sheet. It listed forty boxes, but the shelves held loose books and unnumbered folders.

“If we start counting pages, we'll lose them by waiting.”

The man had a point. The sheets on the bottom shelf were already wet. The emergency was real, and the water wouldn't respect a court procedure.

Amiel photographed the shelves and asked that nobody open the soaked files. Separating pages that had stuck together could tear the ink away with the paper.

“I need a protective order for the series connected to the investigation,” he said. “The municipality must save the rest under its emergency procedure.”

“Why not protect everything?”

“Because the court doesn't administer an entire archive. It can order that identified evidence not disappear without a trace.”

Lesten looked at him wearily.

“Then get your order before the water reaches the stairs.”

***

Yuna Tor received him with the safety report on her desk. Amiel described the risk and submitted a list of document series: hydraulic operations, seal removals, concession annexes, supply orders, and service receipts.

“I'm not asking to close the building to save paper,” he said. “I'm asking for a joint inventory, a neutral repository, and a record of every transfer.”

“What will happen to documents unrelated to your case?”

“The municipal archive will rescue them. We can't turn every document into criminal evidence just because it shares a basement.”

The magistrate confirmed that the safety technician had declared one staircase impassable. No judicial protection could require employees to enter an area threatened by collapse.

Yuna issued a provisional order. It allowed endangered documents to be removed immediately, with photographs of their location whenever safe, external numbering, and a signed receipt for every transfer. Files that might be damaged by opening them would be sealed for specialist conservation.

“Interested parties may challenge the inventory,” she added. “They may not use the emergency to alter the originals or obstruct the rescue.”

The clerk asked who would pay for the neutral repository.

“The administration responsible for the archive, subject to an expense review,” Yuna replied. “We won't obtain justice by leaving the staff who have to move the boxes without wages.”

Amiel returned to the basement with the order under his coat. The rain had started again.

***

Mareval's cart was waiting outside the door. Loden Vair was arguing with Lesten about the number of available workers.

“We can do it free of charge,” Loden said when he saw the inspector.

“The assistance will be recorded,” Amiel replied. “And receipt of the documents will be handled by custodians appointed by the municipality and the court.”

“Do you think we're incapable of moving a crate without stealing it?”

“No. I think a chain of custody must be explainable even when nobody has stolen anything.”

Loden pressed his lips together, but agreed.

The workers formed a line. One employee read out shelf numbers; another photographed covers and seals. The loaders didn't know what the files contained. Their job was to remove them without getting hurt and deliver each package to the designated person.

The emergency generator powered the pump and a few lamps. The reserve battery could keep alarm instruments running, but it couldn't move all the water out of the basement. When the motor began to overheat, the technician slowed the evacuation.

“If we push it, we'll lose the pump,” he warned.

Lesten pointed to the folders that were still missing.

“The water won't wait for it to cool down.”

Amiel wanted to say they could save everything. He looked at the water rising between the legs of a table and stopped himself.

A worker tried to descend a staircase that had been closed off. He carried a rope and insisted he knew where the ceiling supports were.

“Don't go in,” the safety technician ordered. “That wall could give way.”

The man pointed to the book still visible on the other side.

“Those are the construction records from the year of the renovation.”

Amiel felt the urge to authorize the retrieval. Those records might explain why a conduit officially decommissioned was still connected to the shelter.

“We're not going to risk your life for those records,” he said.

He asked for an alternative. The technician proposed shoring up the exterior access and diverting the water through a temporary channel. The workers operated from the safe area, guiding the flow toward a ditch with no nearby homes.

The maneuver took forty minutes. When it was over, they were able to remove two more boxes.

***

By nightfall, Lesten had counted thirty-nine of the forty listed boxes. One small book was missing, marked with the number for requests for emergency intervention submitted before the flood.

“Maybe it was never there,” the archivist said.

“Then we'll record it as an inventory discrepancy,” Amiel replied. “Not as a proven theft.”

An assistant remembered seeing it on a high table when the flooding began. The specialists examined the area from the newly shored structure and found the volume wrapped in a cloth.

The cover was damp. The visible ink hadn't disappeared.

“Don't open it,” Lesten said. “It needs to dry first.”

Amiel nodded. Preserving evidence sometimes began by refusing to read it.

The convoy reached the neutral repository with officials accompanying every cart. The boxes were weighed and inventoried; loose books were kept separate in trays. Loden handed over his part of the receipt and left without trying to enter the conservation room.

Amiel didn't infer general innocence from that behavior. Nor did he invent an accusation simply because the man had offered help.

While reviewing the transport sheets, an assistant noticed that two boxes had the same number. The error had occurred during the second load, when the prepared labels ran out.

“Can we change one?” she asked.

“Yes, with a dated correction that preserves the previous number,” Amiel replied.

The young woman wrote down the reason and signed beside two witnesses.

A small discrepancy had been corrected without erasing its history.

***

The following morning, Yuna reviewed the report. No one had been injured during the rescue. Two series needed treatment for water damage, and several photographs had to be retaken once the covers had dried.

“Was everything saved?” she asked.

“I can't guarantee the contents of the pages until the conservators examine them. We saved the identified materials and recorded what we couldn't verify.”

“And the protective order?”

“It protected the procedure, not the facts we still don't know.”

Yuna pointed to the book recovered at the end. Its index mentioned an extraordinary supply request whose number matched an annex from Kelor. The detail justified a later inquiry, but the inner pages were still stuck together.

“I won't authorize separating them just to satisfy our impatience,” she said.

Amiel left the courthouse and passed a line of employees demanding tools to repair the basement. They'd worked through the night and still didn't know whether they would be paid overtime.

He had their hours added to the cost report. An investigation file rescued through unpaid labor would create another invisible injustice.

Back at the office, he found a note from the archive: the conservators had detected an old reference to the Regulatory Delegation beneath the book's cover. They couldn't determine whether it concerned the Kelor matter or another project.

Amiel put the note away.

The emergency measure hadn't revealed a culprit. It had prevented any future explanation from depending on memories of a box whose whereabouts nobody could establish.

And for that night, it was enough to keep looking.
