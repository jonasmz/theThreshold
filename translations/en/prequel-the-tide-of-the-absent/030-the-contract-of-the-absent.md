# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 030 · The Contract of the Absent

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Neria Calden. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/030-el-contrato-de-los-ausentes.md`.

***

The contract award appeared on a blue sheet pinned to the port noticeboard, between a warning about rats and the tide schedule.

Neria Calden read it while waiting for Irsa to return with inspection results. The Council had authorized forty-five days of technical assistance under municipal supervision. Yet an implementation clause permitted auxiliary services to be contracted whenever the city lacked sufficient personnel.

The port authority had just used it.

The contract was titled “Registration, Transport and Relocation of Personnel Affected by Hydraulic Contingency.” The successful bidder was Estuary Cargo.

Neria recognized the name from railway consignment notes, wage envelopes, and the preservation chamber at kilometer forty-three. Each case file was subject to different verification requirements. None, on its own, was enough to prevent a legally registered company from providing services to the city.

What unsettled her was the start date.

The award was effective from the night of the flood, several days before the vote authorizing the extraordinary expenditure.

“A retroactive contract?” Irsa asked when she found her beside the noticeboard.

“It might be the formalization of emergency work. That does happen.”

“And who certified the work was done?”

Neria examined the notice. It named an official responsible for the works, but listed no inventory of people transferred and no acknowledgment of their arrival.

“That's what I'm going to ask.”

Families were still searching the docks for eleven workers mentioned on an evacuation list. The contract promised to cover transport, accommodation, and meals for those who'd been sheltered. One budget item was payable for every person assisted.

Neria felt a chill.

If the people couldn't be located, who could certify they'd received the service?

***

Fara Nel received her at the Council with the award file on the desk.

“The assistance approval was temporary and restricted,” the councilwoman said. “I don't remember voting to give personal records to a private operator.”

“You didn't. The administrator used an auxiliary procurement power included in the implementing order.”

Fara opened the document at the marked page.

“That permits payment for urgent transport and materials, not unilateral decisions about who was evacuated.”

The procurement official arrived soon afterward. His name was Denor Vask, the same name that had appeared on the withdrawal record for official paper. He didn't deny responsibility for processing the contract.

“We needed to formalize services already provided,” he explained. “The administration couldn't leave rescue operations unpaid while a concession was being debated.”

Neria didn't dispute the principle. Teams had worked during a real emergency, and the city owed them an answer. But the contract grouped services of different kinds: rail transport, shelter, an inventory of those affected, and distribution of payments.

“Who certified receipt of people at the riverside shelter?” she asked.

Denor consulted a sheet.

“The contractor submitted a list of names.”

“The shelter had been closed for months.”

“It might have been used as an assembly point.”

“Then I need the reopening record and the identity of whoever received the evacuees.”

The official grew irritated.

“Not everything gets documented perfectly during a crisis.”

“Which is why we're checking what was documented.”

Fara requested suspension of payments for unverified services while the records were compared, without interrupting genuine aid to those affected. Denor warned that a general hold would put suppliers and families at risk.

“I'm not proposing a general hold,” Fara replied. “I'm proposing that invoices for goods actually delivered be separated from those for people whose arrival nobody can certify.”

Neria studied the budgeted amount for eleven transfers.

It wasn't identical to the value of the vessels' insurance policies. It included supplements for lodging, food, and night work. Someone had calculated specific costs for an operation they still couldn't reconstruct.

***

Orin arrived with Dalia and a copy of the railway register. They'd managed to have the eleven tickets issued by Estuary Cargo preserved, but the company still hadn't identified all the named holders. The scheduled departure had been delayed, and there was no complete boarding list.

“I don't want them paid for my brother as though he'd taken a train,” Dalia said.

Denor raised a hand.

“The amounts will be adjusted when confirmations arrive.”

“What are you going to adjust if he's found injured in another city?” she asked. “What if he never boarded a single carriage?”

Neria saw Fara make a note. That was the difference between treating a person as an invoice number and asking where they were.

The administrator then produced a folder of annexes. It contained personnel mobilization forms, route sheets, and a communications diagram linking the depots.

Neria recognized the identifier of the tower's auxiliary line.

“When was this diagram prepared?” she asked.

Denor pointed to the cover date.

“It's an older contingency template. We use it for different emergencies.”

That was possible. It was also possible that a new copy had been inserted afterward. Neria asked for the version history, not a confession.

The Council clerk searched the circulation receipts. There was a paper request dated three days before the flood and a delivery of forms to Estuary Cargo on the afternoon before the accident. The templates might have been prepared in advance for a legitimate operation.

But one annex contained a specific instruction: “Keep eastern dike crew off the reception list until maneuver completed.”

The wording resembled in meaning the note Jaro had found on a pay sheet. They weren't the same document, nor necessarily from the same source. Their resemblance called for a comparison of originals and signatures.

Amiel, summoned for the review, asked that nobody remove the page.

“The judicial preservation authorization covers documentation of that maneuver,” he said. “We need to identify who added this version to the file.”

Denor looked at Fara.

“This will paralyze the contract.”

“Only the services whose verification is necessary,” she replied. “The rest can continue under supervision.”

Neria read the word “keep” again. Such an instruction might have been written to organize a test. It might also have contributed to leaving real workers off a register meant to protect them.

She wouldn't choose an explanation without evidence.

***

At dusk, the technical supervisors conducted a safety inspection at the eastern station. It was the first maintenance operation under the temporary arrangement approved by the Council.

Neria checked the seals, independent readings, and canal level. The regulators were consuming a steady amount of energy; the accumulator remained above the safety threshold. There was no indication of an imminent flood.

Irsa supervised the replacement of an ordinary gasket, well away from the preserved retainers. Every tool left an inventoried box and went back to the same place. A contractor's technician signed for the receipt of materials before municipal witnesses.

It struck Neria as a simple, necessary scene.

Machines could be repaired through procedures that left a trace. People shouldn't be at the mercy of a procedure that erased their names.

As she closed the register, a messenger arrived from Amiel.

Magistrate Yuna Tor had ordered the award annexes preserved and their origins examined. She hadn't suspended all emergency assistance or found the company guilty. She'd required that payments for the transport of people be tied to genuine records of their arrival.

Neria put the note away.

“Any news of Erven?” Irsa asked.

“Nothing confirmed.”

The operator looked toward the port, where lights were coming on above the damaged vessels.

“Then we keep counting those who are missing.”

***

At the courthouse, Fara delivered a certified copy of the contract to Amiel. Neria had accompanied her to clarify technical references, not to take part in the judicial assessment.

The inspector opened the final annex.

One payment sheet had eleven boxes. Each listed a proposed service for transport and accommodation. Beside them was a preparation date preceding the flood, and below, a space for certifying that the service had actually been completed.

The acknowledgment signatures were blank.

The signature authorizing the design of the operation was not.

It belonged to Denor Vask, acting as processing official, and was accompanied by an Estuary Cargo seal. The signature could establish that someone prepared the form; it didn't prove he'd ordered the emergency or known its outcome.

Amiel separated the original from the copy and recorded the page numbers.

“We need the file that justifies this provision,” he said. “And we must distinguish who proposed the transfer from who claimed to have carried it out.”

Fara nodded.

Neria studied the eleven boxes with no recipient named.

The contract had already been awarded. The company was already entitled to claim payment if it could prove the services were provided. And the investigation still didn't know where the people for whom that service had been budgeted had gone.

Someone had prepared a system for counting them before the night when they disappeared from the records.

Now the city had to show that it had searched for them as carefully as it had planned to charge for them.
