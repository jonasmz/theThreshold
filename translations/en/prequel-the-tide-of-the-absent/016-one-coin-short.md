# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 016 · One Coin Short

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Amiel Rusk. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/016-una-moneda-de-menos.md`.

***

The coin Liron Velk had received weighed less than the others.

Jaro showed it to Amiel Rusk in the clinic courtyard, after waiting for an hour outside the courthouse. It was a brass token bearing the letters of the north depot and the number of crew four. Someone had filed a crescent into one edge, barely visible in the light.

“My brother says that was how they knew which ones weren't to be exchanged at the common cash desk,” Jaro explained. “They promised him money at the end of the week.”

Amiel held the piece on a handkerchief without touching the engraving.

“Where did you find it?”

“Liron kept it in his jacket. I gave it back to him after you took his statement.”

“Then it's the same one we examined.”

“Yes. But yesterday he told me to look at the edge. He didn't remember that mark before.”

The new detail wasn't impossible. An injured person's memory could become clearer after rest. It could also be influenced by relatives' questions. Amiel recorded when the detail had emerged and who had held the token since the first interview.

“I need to ask him about it directly,” he said. “Then compare it with other tokens.”

Jaro pressed his lips together.

“If you wait too long, they'll pay somebody else.”

“That's why I've come to get the redemption ledgers.”

The court had authorized an examination of payments from the week of the accident. Amiel couldn't search the port's entire accounts or detain a clerk merely because names didn't match. He could require the depot to produce its daily ledger, delivery receipts, and redeemed tokens, with an inventory.

At the cashier's office, Osen Briar greeted them with a neatly arranged stack of folders.

“It's all here,” he said. “Just as you requested.”

Amiel studied the sheets. There were columns for the crew, number of tokens, hours worked, and the signature of whoever received payment. Wages were distributed on Fridays to a representative appointed by each team. It was common practice among temporary workers who couldn't afford to lose an entire morning waiting at a service window.

It also allowed one person to collect pay under four names.

“Who collected crew four's wages?” he asked.

Osen indicated a signature. It belonged neither to Liron nor to Erven Tal. The entry read: “Settlement Desk, Estuary Cargo.”

“Is that a person or an organization?”

“A service company. It contracts crews and distributes the money.”

“Why is it listed as the beneficiary if the book records individual workers?”

“Because the depot doesn't employ them directly. It pays the company for the service.”

Amiel compared the total with the allocated hours. The depot had paid for a double shift, but Liron's token certified an ordinary one. There might be a difference for night work, taxes, or materials. The contract was missing.

“I want the order authorizing that company to collect wages for the crews,” he said.

Osen took a copy from a gray folder.

The company had its registered office in a building near the salt mills. It was not formally owned by Mareval, although a small stamp indicated that the consortium guaranteed performance of its service. The authorization bore the signature of a senior port administrator.

“And who took the money to the workers?” Amiel asked.

“That's not recorded here.”

“Then you can't be certain they were paid.”

Osen straightened.

“I can be certain our depot settled the account.”

“I didn't say you were lying. I'm saying settling an account and paying a person are not the same act.”

The cashier watched him without answering.

***

On the ground floor of the Estuary Cargo building, a man in a dark waistcoat served haulers from behind a grille. Sacks of flour, crane parts, and boxes of salt were piled beneath notices listing the rates. The business was operating: carts came in, receipts went out, and somebody was keeping genuine accounts. This did not look like a company invented to conceal a single payment.

Amiel asked to speak to whoever was responsible for wages. The man introduced himself as Silven Rea and produced a commercial power of attorney authorizing him to act for the company.

“Your request must be submitted in writing,” he said.

“I have a limited production and preservation order.”

Silven read it carefully.

“It applies only to the work carried out by crew four.”

“Exactly.”

He led Amiel into a room containing a table and three cabinets. A clerk opened a subcontract ledger. The indicated page listed the dredging service, four positions, and an hourly rate. The names did not match those Liron had given.

“We use internal codes,” Silven explained. “Surnames are checked when workers are registered.”

“Where are the registration records?”

“An outside personnel office keeps them.”

Amiel looked at the transfer receipt. The company had received the depot's money that same Friday. The next entry recorded redistribution to another account, identified only by the abbreviation RC-7.

“What does that mean?” he asked.

“Contingency reserve, section seven.”

“Who administers the reserve?”

“The company's treasury.”

“Where is the record of actual payment to the workers?”

Silven turned a page. There was a cash authorization for the amount of four wages, but no receipt signed by the four recipients.

“Perhaps they were paid in cash,” he said.

“I need records, not possibilities.”

The clerk looked at Silven before speaking.

“The foreman used to collect the envelopes for casual crews. The collections were noted in the messenger log.”

The man gave her a warning look. Amiel noticed but did not interpret the gesture in front of them.

“Please preserve that log,” he said. “I'll request an extension of the court order to examine it. We'll also need to identify the foreman and check the sequence of deliveries.”

The clerk nodded. Silven took note of the request without losing his smile.

As he left, Amiel passed a group of loaders waiting to collect an advance. One had a bandaged hand. Another had fallen asleep on a sack. They were not props in an accounting mystery: their families depended on this office continuing to pay them while he investigated.

He understood that freezing the entire treasury would be a quick way to punish people who had done nothing wrong.

***

Back at the clinic, Liron confirmed that he had been given the marked token on the shift before the flood. The foreman had told him not to exchange it at the common cash desk: filed tokens had to be presented to the cargo company because they carried a bonus for work in a restricted canal.

“Did you collect it?” Amiel asked.

“No. The accident happened before Friday.”

“Do you know anyone who did?”

“Erven collected for all of us once. He brought envelopes with our names on them, but money was missing.”

“How much?”

Liron thought for a moment.

“One coin per man. He said it was for a protection fund.”

Amiel recorded the statement and its date. The filed token, the difference in wages, and the reserve entry were different kinds of facts. They could be connected, but he still had no complete reconciliation of the amounts and beneficiaries.

Jaro, sitting beside the bed, asked whether he could recover his brother's money.

“If we establish an employment debt, there will be a claims procedure,” Amiel replied. “This investigation doesn't replace it.”

Liron raised a hand.

“I don't just want the coin. I want to know why the name collecting my pay wasn't mine.”

Back at the courthouse, Amiel found a partial response from the commercial registry. Estuary Cargo was a legally registered company, active before the accident and contracted by several depots. Among its administrators was a woman who was also listed as an authorized representative of a vessel-insurance broker.

The name meant nothing to him.

What he recognized was the broker's address: the same alley that appeared on the seven policies Breda Lin was auditing.

Amiel placed the two facts on separate sheets. Sharing a street wasn't enough to turn two businesses into a conspiracy. But he now had a precise question for the Mutual.

As he closed the folder, he heard the token strike softly against the gauze in which they had wrapped it.

One missing coin for every man seemed a small thing.

Multiplied by hundreds of shifts, it could become something considerably larger than a lost wage.
