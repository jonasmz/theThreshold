# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 059 · Public Correction

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Orin Farel. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/059-enmienda-publica.md`.

***

Orin Farel knew the correction would cost them support before he had finished reading it.

The document ran to two pages. On the first, Vela Noren and Neria Calden corrected a phrase in the public report circulated after the civic hearing. The auxiliary signal line had been described as “withdrawn from service.” The phrase was true in administrative terms but incomplete in physical terms: the official service had been decommissioned, while a conductor and a repeater had remained connected to a secondary power supply.

On the second page, Amiel Rusk clarified that the copy challenged in court was a working sheet, not the original record. Vela's added legend was imprecise and could not be used to claim that the warning had reached the external channel. Nor was there sufficient evidence to claim that the signal operator had deliberately altered a medium.

“If we publish this, they'll say we're backing down,” Orin said.

“We're backing away from a phrase that wasn't accurate,” Neria replied. “Not from what we measured.”

“People won't separate those two things.”

Vela kept her eyes on the document.

“Then we have to write it so they can.”

***

The meeting took place in the cooperative hall. Dockworkers, relatives of missing people, two representatives of the Mutual, and several merchants who had joined the original petition had come. Half the attendees were carrying copies of the Estuary Gazette.

Orin explained that the correction did not withdraw the evidence concerning the gate retainer, inventory discrepancies, or payments still awaiting reconciliation. Nor did it resolve Erven Tal's disappearance. It corrected only two technical statements and the status of one copy.

A man from the loading crew raised his newspaper.

“So now the signal never existed?”

“No,” Vela said. “There is a record of pulses. What we cannot say is who originated them or whether they reached the final receiver.”

“And the line that was withdrawn?”

“It was decommissioned as a public service. The conductor remained connected to an auxiliary repeater. We should have explained that distinction in the first report.”

“So you lied to us?”

Neria answered before Vela could.

“We summarized it incorrectly. That isn't the same as proving a deliberate lie. But the effect was that some people understood something the data could not support.”

The man put the newspaper down on the table.

“Then what is left of all this?”

“The original records, the preserved components, the payment discrepancies, and the statements,” Amiel said. “What changes is the limit of what we can claim.”

***

The discussion grew harsher when a relative asked whether the correction helped Mareval.

“The company will use it to say everything was a misunderstanding,” he said. “They'll say the water rose by itself and nobody touched anything.”

Breda explained that the correction did not affect the policy audit or the payment reconciliations. The seven claims still had to be checked individually. The correction did not turn questionable records into valid documents.

“But we also can't present an incomplete signal as proof of an order,” she added. “If we do, the company's lawyer will have good reason to challenge the rest.”

The relative looked at her suspiciously.

“You already signed a report you hadn't verified.”

“Yes,” Breda said. “And that's why I won't do it again.”

Orin saw some people nod and others turn away. The meeting, which had begun as an attempt to hold the community together, was becoming an argument over who deserved to keep speaking on its behalf.

A woman stood up.

“My husband never came back. I don't need an explanation of the difference between an administrative line and a physical one.”

Orin did not try to contradict her.

“It shouldn't be an answer for you. The search for your husband is still open. But if we publish a false certainty, tomorrow someone may use it to close an inquiry we still need.”

The woman picked up her coat.

“Then publish whatever you want. I'm not signing anything else.”

She left without waiting for the meeting to end.

***

After the meeting, Orin walked Vela back to the tower. The signal operator carried the corrected draft in a transparent folder.

“Do you think we should wait for the full laboratory report?” she asked.

“If we wait, the headline will remain the only public version.”

“And if we publish now, people will think we're admitting that I falsified the record.”

Orin stopped beside the canal railing.

“I can't choose for you. But I won't ask you to stand by a sentence you know is wrong.”

Vela watched the water.

“The original didn't change. The copy was wrong. I wrote the legend.”

“Then that's what the document has to say.”

“It won't be enough to clear my name.”

“No.”

The answer surprised her. Orin did not know what else he could offer.

“People want a story where someone did something and someone else uncovered it,” he continued. “The truth we have is less orderly. If we try to make it simpler, we'll end up resembling the people who gave us the incomplete forms.”

Vela folded the folder.

“I'll sign.”

***

The amendment was published the following day on the municipal noticeboard, at the signal tower, and in the open digital document registry. It included a three-column table: previous statement, corrected clarification, and available supporting material. It named no suspect and attributed no intentions.

The first correction stated that the line had been withdrawn from public service but not physically disconnected. The second clarified that the central record showed an entry and an intermediate repetition, with no final acknowledgment confirmed. The third identified the challenged sheet as working material and acknowledged that Vela had written an incorrect legend. The original remained in custody and did not contain that annotation.

Amiel added a procedural note: the correction did not prejudge the authenticity of other documents or the responsibility of any person. Orin signed as the cooperative's representative, not as a technical expert.

The reaction came before noon.

Two workers removed their names from the list supporting the citizens' commission. A group of relatives issued a statement saying the amendment weakened the investigation. The cooperative lost access to a hall lent by a merchant who considered the correction a concession to Mareval.

Orin received calls from captains asking whether he still believed the port had been manipulated.

“I believe there is evidence of failures, omissions, and payments that need explaining,” he replied. “I don't know who did each thing.”

“Then you don't have an answer.”

“I don't have a complete answer.”

Some callers hung up without saying goodbye.

***

That afternoon, journalist Leda Var published a shorter item. She did not withdraw her earlier article or apologize. She added that the signal operator had acknowledged an imprecise annotation and that the court had not established a deliberate alteration of the original. The Gazette kept the headline in its archive.

Vela read the update in silence.

“It doesn't say the headline was false,” she remarked.

“She isn't required to say that if she doesn't want to,” Amiel replied. “We've already published what we can support.”

Neria arrived with a copy of the eastern station report. The measurement of the external adjustment remained valid. The preservation order was still in force. The North Brine retainer remained in custody, and the buoy branch had been isolated without losing the activation record.

“Nothing has collapsed,” she said.

“Some of the support has,” Orin replied.

Neria looked at the list of signatures that had been removed from the noticeboard.

“We can't keep it with a false statement.”

Orin thought of the names they had gathered in the square, the more than two hundred people who had demanded help and documentation. Some had joined because they believed the investigation would prove a simple conspiracy. Now they would have to accept that the facts could be serious without fitting into a single story.

“I don't know if they'll come back,” he said.

“They don't have to come back for the record to remain open.”

***

At dusk, Orin found a note from Erven's family at the cooperative office. They were not withdrawing their search petition, but they no longer authorized the commission to use his brother's name in public statements without consulting them.

Orin placed the note in the family file and removed the name from the distribution list. It was a legitimate request, even if it complicated communication.

Then he went to the municipal noticeboard. The amendment was still there, its corners curled by the wind. Someone had written underneath: “And who gives us back what we've lost?”

Orin had no answer.

He straightened the page so it would not come loose. The correction did not restore trust, money, or people. It did not resolve the conflict with the press either.

But it stopped an imprecise phrase from continuing to occupy the place of a fact.

When he returned to the quay, the captains were still working. Two did not greet him. Another handed him a fuel receipt to add to the cooperative's accounts.

Orin put it away.

The community had not broken apart completely. It had become harder to bring together.

And perhaps, he thought, that was the price of beginning to tell the truth in fewer words than everyone wanted to hear.
