# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 050 · Faces in the Courtroom

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Dalia Tal. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/050-rostros-en-la-sala.md`.

***

Dalia Tal had prepared three questions, and none began with the word *guilty*.

She wrote them on the back of a soup recipe while waiting for the courthouse doors to open. She wanted to know whether anyone had recorded her brother Erven's departure, who had authorized a rail ticket in his name, and why the assistance forms seemed to describe a journey her family couldn't confirm.

Dockworkers, merchants, journalists, and relatives of missing people gathered outside the building. Some carried portraits. Others held signs demanding that those responsible for the flood be arrested.

Dalia hadn't brought a portrait.

She'd spent too many days showing Erven's face to people who answered with a story about someone who looked like him.

Orin Farel appeared beside the steps.

“The room will be full,” he warned. “I got seats reserved for the families, but I can't control who turns up.”

“I don't need you to protect me from the public,” Dalia said.

“That's not what I'm trying to do. I just want you to be able to listen.”

The hearing was the first public judicial session intended to organize evidence and objections concerning the contracts, signals, and transfers linked to the emergency. It wasn't the final trial into the cause of the flood. Magistrate Yuna Tor had refused to turn it into a blanket declaration of guilt.

Dalia understood the distinction.

Even so, she felt that at last there was a place where the offices would have to answer in front of the people who had suffered from their decisions.

***

Inside, the rows were divided by a narrow aisle. Estuary Cargo's representatives occupied one table; Mareval had sent lawyers and technicians. The municipality brought folders bearing seals in several colors. Amiel Rusk sat beside the clerk, not with the accused or the families.

Yuna opened the session with a warning.

“Today we'll examine the provenance and scope of documents, the preservation of evidence, and requests for further inquiries. This room won't decide the entire cause of the accident or the fate of every missing person in a single day.”

A journalist raised his hand.

“Will the hearing be open?”

“The general proceedings, yes. Clinical information and protected addresses will be handled under the current access rulings.”

Dalia heard murmuring behind her. Some people resented the fact that certain information couldn't be copied for the newspaper. Talia, the shelter coordinator, sat two rows ahead, watching every document placed on the table.

The first lawyer asked to exclude all signal records because the selector from the booth had lost continuity of custody during a transfer.

Amina Dorel explained what she'd photographed and measured before that incident. She acknowledged that the object held afterward couldn't be presented as intact, but distinguished her firsthand observations from the box whose seal had been changed.

Yuna didn't decide the objection by applause. She ordered the sources to be separated, allowed adversarial examination, and maintained the limitation on the transferred mechanism.

Dalia felt frustrated. She'd expected someone to say once and for all how the signal had been altered.

Instead, she heard that a piece of evidence could answer one question but not another.

***

The railway was next.

Vela Noren presented the train's consist registers. It had carried seventy-eight ordinary passengers; the carriage reserved for eleven people had no confirmed group reception at the destination.

“So none of them traveled?” the families' lawyer asked.

“I can't conclude that,” Vela replied. “I can describe the reservation, the lack of documented receipt, and the independent statements. Someone may have traveled by another means or on another date.”

Dalia gripped the edge of the bench.

Erven's name was linked to a ticket and a piece of luggage. There was no record that he'd boarded. The explanation didn't bring him any closer to home, but it stopped anyone from using that paper to claim he was already safe in another city.

Amiel then presented the identification of Baret Luno, a worker who had died in Port Cendra before the scheduled departure. His brother, Taren, had authorized disclosure of the minimum civil details needed to explain the contradiction, not the details of his medical care.

The company's lawyer argued that a reservation could be canceled after it was issued.

“That's not in dispute,” Amiel said. “We're examining whether the settlement treated the reservation as a completed service and whether the advance was reconciled.”

The room stirred. A woman shouted that they'd charged for a dead man.

Yuna called for order and offered Baret's family a short recess.

Dalia watched Taren. His face showed something different from the anger on the signs: the exhaustion of having explained too many times a fact no one should have to prove again.

***

During the recess, Dalia went out into the courtyard. A journalist recognized her surname and asked whether she thought Erven had been murdered.

“I don't know,” she replied.

“But the list is false?”

“There are documents that don't prove what they claim to prove. That doesn't give me the right to tell you what happened to my brother.”

The man insisted that the public needed a clear answer.

Dalia thought of her three questions.

“So do I.”

She went back inside before the interview could become a headline she'd never wanted to say aloud.

The session resumed with a witness from the shelter. Irena Valen had agreed to testify about a bread delivery documented on the morning after the flood. Her lawyer requested that the settlement's exact access route not be disclosed.

The company's lawyer challenged the delivery time. The evidence didn't establish an exact minute and therefore didn't rule out every later journey.

Irena didn't defend herself with a grand story.

“That day I received bread for the kitchen,” she said. “I signed for it. I don't authorize anyone to say I boarded a train somewhere else to justify an invoice.”

Dalia saw Talia bow her head.

A small statement could demand as much courage as a public accusation.

***

They then heard testimony about signal maintenance.

Yarik Sem, the assistant technician, admitted that he'd installed a clamp on a supervisor's instruction. His cooperation had been agreed within a limited legal scope. He didn't say he'd known of a plan to cause the flood.

Mareval's lawyer tried to ask him about conversations Yarik hadn't witnessed. Yuna required the question to be rephrased.

“A witness's statement can't be stretched to cover what someone else might have thought,” she said.

The prosecutor then submitted the materials voucher and original storeroom ledger, whose consistency still needed expert verification. The opposing party requested access to both records for examination.

Yuna granted it.

Dalia felt the hearing moving slowly along a shore that always seemed to recede. She understood why every step needed support. She also knew families couldn't wait forever while contracts continued to accrue interest.

When representatives of the affected parties were allowed to speak, she asked for the floor.

She hadn't prepared an accusation. She took out the back of the recipe and read her questions.

“Which institution received my brother if the train doesn't prove his arrival? Who put his name on the ticket? Who can tell me what was done to look for him afterward?”

No one answered at once.

Yuna looked straight at her.

“The first question remains open,” she said. “The second is the subject of document requests. The third requires a specific report from the search authorities, and I will request it.”

Dalia swallowed.

“I don't want his file closed because a company wrote that a service was completed.”

“Nor will I close it for that reason,” the magistrate replied.

It wasn't a promise to find him.

It was a decision not to confuse an invoice with a person's return.

***

The hearing ended at dusk with several limited measures. The original railway records, contract versions, and relevant technical logs would be preserved. The parties could challenge the expert reports. Verified assistance payments had to continue; disputed services would be subject to reconciliation and review.

Yuna didn't order every contractor arrested or the port shut down. Nor did she clear anyone simply because there was no complete explanation.

As she left, Dalia found Orin beside the fountain.

“Did you get any answers?” he asked.

“I got them to ask some questions in a way that can't easily be made to disappear.”

Orin offered to walk her home. She accepted.

Before they crossed the square, a court employee caught up with her carrying a notice. The search authority had received the magistrate's request and wanted the family to confirm which items of railway luggage they recognized.

Dalia looked at the list: a blanket with a blue patch, worn boots, and a tool case.

“I can identify the blanket,” she said. “I can't swear he packed the case.”

The employee recorded the distinction.

As she walked away, Dalia remembered Erven teaching her to mend a rope when they were children. He had a way of tying a knot too tightly, one that was always difficult to undo.

She thought of the canvas found beside the remains of the skiff. She'd stopped looking at it because each time it seemed like a farewell.

Now she understood that she had to keep it as a question.

Many faces had appeared in the courtroom, but his was still missing.

Dalia put the recipe back in her pocket.

The next morning she'd take the canvas to the expert. Not because she could guarantee she'd find her brother, but because she'd decided that no form would speak for him while she still had a legitimate way to search.
