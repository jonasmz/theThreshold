# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 008 · The Tugboat's Debt

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Orin Farel. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/008-la-deuda-del-remolcador.md`.

***

Orin Farel had spent nineteen years repairing his tugboat, and just one night trying to prove he had repaired it well enough to deserve compensation.

The boat was called Good Shadow. It wasn't an elegant name for a vessel that hauled enormous freighters with an engine far too old for the job, but Orin's father had painted it on the stern after buying the boat on credit. Since then, they had replaced the boiler, the propeller, and nearly every plate on the deck. The name remained.

The current from the eastern floodgate had bent the starboard support and split a seam below the waterline. Orin managed to bring the tug into dry dock with the help of two sailors. There, the master shipwright pronounced that it could return to the water in a week, provided Orin had the money for the iron.

He didn't.

At the port office, a woman behind a service window examined his claim without looking up.

“We can't acknowledge damage resulting from unauthorized maneuvers,” she said.

“I was following an emergency signal.”

“The report says you entered the discharge zone after the closure had been ordered.”

“Because the signal arrived after I'd entered the bend.”

The clerk looked for a sheet of paper.

“It says here that the warning went out at 1:58.”

Orin put his hands on the counter.

“At that time I was unloading provisions at the canal mouth. Two officers and a harbor pilot saw me. I received no signal.”

“I can't change the time in the record.”

“I'm not asking you to change it. I'm asking you to note that I dispute it.”

The woman looked up, surprised. Her badge said Elva, and she seemed to have handled too many claims that morning.

“I can add a comment and give you a copy,” she said.

Orin took off his cap.

“That'll do.”

It wouldn't pay for a single screw. But it was something that couldn't disappear without leaving another mark.

Outside the office, his sailors Solen and Mir were waiting for him. They had worked together long enough to argue without taking offense. Solen had a scar splitting one eyebrow and a habit of making decisions before he knew what they would cost.

“Are they paying us?” he asked.

“They've acknowledged that I filed paperwork.”

“So they paid us in air.”

“Air with a very fine seal on it.”

Mir, who never laughed at jokes until he'd figured out how much truth they contained, pointed toward the list of vessels moored across the quay.

“The cooperative has three boats laid up. If we don't get back to work, the big freighters will hire the consortium.”

Orin knew the figures. Members of the tugboat cooperative paid into a common fund for accidents, illness, and replacement parts. That fund could support a family for a week; it couldn't replace three engines at once. Every day out of service gave Mareval another chance to charge a higher rate and then claim that the cooperative was no longer needed.

“Let's call the captains together,” he said.

“To ask them for money?” Solen asked.

“To decide how much we can owe one another without ceasing to be partners.”

***

The meeting took place in a net shed where wind blew through six holes in the roof. There was no platform. Orin preferred to stand beside a crooked table, with the repair estimate spread open and the names of those affected beside it.

Mara Veis, captain of a small tug, spoke first.

“My deck was damaged too. And I've got two men who can't work because of scalding burns. If we advance the whole fund for your hull, what am I supposed to do about them?”

Orin didn't try to convince her that his need was greater.

“Let's record all three breakdowns and the wages lost. Then vote on a limit for each case.”

An older man asked if they could take out a loan. Another reminded them that the common fund already owed two installments to the coal supplier. Mir read the figures aloud. Solen proposed working double shifts on the boats still available.

It offered a partial solution. It also increased the danger of accidents caused by fatigue.

“No double shifts,” Orin said. “If we finish this week with another boat at the bottom, we won't have fixed anything.”

“And if we turn down more work?” Solen replied. “How do we pay?”

Orin studied the order book. Four towing jobs were scheduled for the early hours, and only two boats were fit to sail. They needed outside help, or they would have to refuse contracts and hand customers over to the consortium.

Mara planted both hands on the table.

“I can lend my boat for two maneuvers. But the fund will have to cover my injured sailor's wages, even if he doesn't come aboard.”

“That's fair,” Orin said.

“I didn't ask whether it was fair. I asked whether we're going to put it in writing.”

Orin gave a faint smile. He picked up the pen and wrote it down.

The decision didn't solve the repairs to Good Shadow. It allowed his men to keep earning something while he sought proof that the port ought to bear part of the damage.

As the meeting ended, a woman approached carrying a piece of canvas.

“My husband was with Erven Tal,” she said.

Orin recognized the missing skipper's sister. Her name was Dalia, and she worked in a kitchen near the shipyards. They had spoken during the night, when nobody yet knew which vessels had been caught.

“They found this among the lighter's broken planks,” she explained.

It was a short length of canvas bearing the vessel's painted number. There were no marks that could establish what had happened to its owner.

“I want it on record that he was working,” Dalia said. “The port says he isn't listed as authorized personnel.”

Orin took hold of one edge of the canvas.

“I know Erven. I didn't need anyone to show me a contract to know he worked.”

“But the court does.”

The words hung between them like an uncomfortable debt.

“I can testify where I saw the lighter,” Orin answered. “And who gave the signal when we started heading out.”

“Did you see him?”

Orin hesitated.

He had seen a figure at the helm before the current lifted the bow. It might have been Erven. The distance and the smoke from a tugboat wouldn't let him swear to it.

“I saw someone at the steering position,” he said. “I couldn't identify him under oath.”

Dalia lowered the canvas.

“Thank you for not inventing what I want to hear.”

***

That afternoon, Orin returned to the office with the dry-dock estimate and a statement from his crew. A different clerk received him. This one had the contract governing damage caused by safety maneuvers on his desk.

“The authority pays if the maneuver was ordered by the emergency service,” he explained.

“There was a signal.”

“We have no record that it was addressed to your vessel.”

“The signal tower can verify that.”

“Then bring me that certificate.”

Orin remembered the way the light had flashed above the dike. He didn't know the entire code, but he could tell an order to withdraw from a general warning. The latter had arrived when withdrawing without danger was already impossible.

The clerk added that compensation would be limited to direct damage. It wouldn't cover lost wages or the portion of the hull that had already been worn before the flood.

“And who decides how much of it was worn?” Orin asked.

“The assessor.”

“Whose assessor?”

“The port's.”

Orin put the estimate away.

“I want an assessor from the cooperative present when they inspect the hull.”

“That could delay your claim.”

“The boat's out of the water. The only thing that can sink now is my right to dispute your figures.”

The clerk wrote down the request.

When Orin came out, he found Solen waiting beside the edge of the dock. The sailor held a sheet of paper he'd retrieved from among the damaged timbers.

“It's from the signal log Erven kept,” he said.

Barely two lines were legible. One referred to the withdrawal warning. The other, written earlier, carried a time and a startling note: “Await scheduled opening.”

Orin looked toward the eastern station.

The floodgate had been opened during an emergency. Nobody had explained why the skipper of a lighter had been instructed to wait for it.
