# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 031 · Toward the Marshes

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Neria Calden. **Location:** Marsh service canal, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/031-hacia-las-marismas.md`.

***

The canal wasn't shown on the map in front of Neria Calden. Yet water was still flowing through it.

She heard it before she saw it, beyond the reeds stretching west of the former riverside shelter. The sound was low and steady, like breathing beneath the ground. Beside her, Irsa held a measuring rod and a mud-stained notebook.

“The plan from the last renovation ends here,” Irsa said.

Neria traced the printed line with her finger. A conduit led from Kelor Dam, passed through industrial reservoirs, and stopped at a box marked “filled in.” On the earlier plan, preserved by Elsia, that same conduit continued toward the marshes and emptied into a service canal.

“Filled in doesn't mean they actually filled it,” Neria replied. “It might just mean they stopped maintaining it.”

A rusted floodgate rose between the reeds. It had an operating wheel and a plaque covered with lichen. The municipality had recorded its closure a year earlier, but there was no dismantling report.

Neria had obtained authorization to inspect public infrastructure along the riverbank. The permit didn't cover entering homes or private storage facilities. Nor did it allow her to turn a wheel whose condition she didn't know. She could measure levels from the walkway and examine what was visible.

Irsa wiped the plaque with a cloth.

“Relief Canal Seven,” she read. “Auxiliary service.”

Neria looked toward the flooded ground.

“The diversion starts farther upstream. If it received water during the emergency, some of that water had to pass through here.”

“Then why isn't it in the distribution reports?”

“That's the question.”

They drove a portable gauge into the bank and took two readings fifteen minutes apart. The level changed little, but the flow was perceptible. There hadn't been enough recent rain to account for the movement on its own.

“It could be seepage from the reservoirs,” Irsa said.

“It could. We need to measure the flow and locate its source.”

A man approached in a flat-bottomed boat. He carried eel nets and a hat so battered it barely shielded his forehead.

“You shouldn't stay there when the return flow rises,” he warned.

“What time does it rise?” Neria asked.

“When they send water from the heron reservoir. It doesn't follow a fixed tide.”

He introduced himself as Edran, a marsh fisherman of twenty years. Neria wrote down his name and asked if he could describe when he observed the changes.

“It used to happen once a week,” he said. “Since the night of the accident, almost every day.”

“Can you show us where it starts?”

Edran pointed toward a bend in the reeds.

“I can take you to the old bridge. There are houses beyond that. Don't go entering them on my say-so.”

***

The boat moved against a narrow current. Low trees grew along both sides, their roots sunk deep in the mud. Neria recognized an artificial channel beneath the vegetation: the banks still held stone blocks at regular intervals, though many were cracked.

Edran rowed in short strokes.

“When they closed the canal, they stopped cleaning the gates,” he said. “Then families arrived and laid planks across it. If somebody opens it again without warning, people could get trapped.”

Neria studied a walkway built of boards in different sizes.

“How many families?”

“I don't count them. Some stay one night; others have been here for months. Nobody appointed me to take their census.”

His answer reminded her of the eleven empty boxes on the transport contract. The administration had budgeted payments for people it still couldn't locate. Here lived people who might not want to be found by that same administration.

“We're not here to register anyone,” she said.

“That's what everyone with a notebook says.”

Irsa put hers away.

Edran didn't seem satisfied, but he kept rowing.

Beneath the old bridge they found a secondary valve connected to a masonry reservoir. A recent chain immobilized its shaft. Water entered through a side pipe and escaped toward the marshes. A water mark on the stone stood well above the present current.

“On the night of the flood, it reached that line,” Edran said.

“Did you see it?” Neria asked.

“I saw water over the first plank of the bridge. The mark was there when it went down. I don't know how long the level stayed high.”

Neria distinguished the two facts in her notebook. The fisherman's observation and the physical trace could support one another, but neither replaced a continuous flow record.

At the base of the valve was a metal plate bearing manufacturing numbers. The code matched a family of municipal equipment predating the renovation, not the retainers contracted from Mareval.

Irsa examined the chain.

“Looks like it was put on recently.”

“We don't know who placed it there, or whether the valve is open inside.”

“Shall we test it?”

“Not without reducing the pressure and securing access to the reservoir.”

Edran showed them a dirt path leading toward an empty hut.

“There used to be a keeper. Now a cart comes by some Tuesdays. They bring coal, flour, and sometimes blankets.”

Neria looked at him.

“For whom?”

“The families on the other side.”

“Who signs for the deliveries?”

“Nobody asks me. I don't ask which company they're from when they leave food.”

***

The path ended beside a small lock. There was a handwheel, a hydraulic meter, and an inspection table under a sagging roof. The meter had stopped but wasn't disconnected: its needle rested on a graduation, and the thread on its casing seal was broken.

Neria photographed its condition without touching it.

Irsa took a sample of the flowing water to compare its sediment with the riverside reservoirs. A mineral resemblance would not be enough to establish the source; the conduit ran through similar ground. They would need simultaneous level readings and, if possible, an innocuous tracer test authorized by the reservoir operators.

“The station's listed as closed,” Irsa said. “Why is somebody keeping the valve clean?”

Unlike the nearby stone blocks, the wheel had fresh grease between its spokes.

Neria didn't answer immediately.

It could be safety maintenance omitted from the records, work done by a resident, or an unauthorized service maneuver. All three explanations required identifying dates and responsible parties.

There was a thud on the far side of the lock.

A woman carrying two buckets had tripped on a broken board. Neria ran to the walkway to help. The stranger stepped back when she saw Neria's municipal badge.

“Don't take our water away,” she said.

“I'm not going to shut anything off. Are you hurt?”

The woman shook her head and gripped the buckets tightly.

“If they close this intake, the shelter well turns brackish.”

Neria studied the conduit. Fresh water coming from the reservoirs might be sustaining more than an abandoned canal.

“I need to know how much water the shelter receives, so we can make sure it doesn't run out,” she replied. “I don't need your name right now.”

The woman hesitated.

“What if you write it down and then they come to throw us out?”

“I can't promise what other offices will decide. But I can make it clear this is a hydraulic safety inspection, not a register of residents.”

The stranger pointed toward a path between the trees.

“Talk to the person who distributes the water. But come without guards.”

***

Back at the boat, Edran offered to take them as far as the shelter crossroads. Neria checked her permit. She could follow the public watercourse and speak to residents who agreed to talk; she wasn't authorized to cross fences or inspect homes.

She agreed to go as far as the main path.

The tide was beginning to change. Irsa noticed the current quickening, although the gauge level remained within a safe margin. They recorded the time of both instruments and the difference in their readings.

Beyond the final bend stood a timber structure covered with canvas. Mugs, children's clothes, and little bells made from scraps of metal hung beneath the roof. Farther away, huts stood on raised platforms.

Edran stopped the boat.

“This isn't on the new plans,” Neria said.

“People rarely are.”

A girl appeared by the walkway and shouted that port technicians had arrived. Several doors closed.

Neria slipped her badge into her pocket, without concealing who she was, and stayed where people could see her. She didn't want a technical visit mistaken for a raid.

An older woman emerged from the shelter carrying a hard-covered notebook. She looked first at Irsa, then at the measuring rod.

“If you've come to close the canal, you'll have to tell us where we'll get water tomorrow.”

“We've come to find out why it's still open,” Neria replied.

The woman raised her notebook.

“Then perhaps you need to listen to the people who've kept it running while the city said it didn't exist.”

Neria looked toward the watercourse she'd followed through the reeds. She now had a physical connection to investigate. What she'd just found, however, wouldn't fit into a pipe section or a construction drawing.

An entire settlement depended on infrastructure officially declared closed.

And someone would have to decide how to repair it without erasing the people who survived because of it.
