# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 006 · A Door Under Lock and Key

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Amiel Rusk. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/006-una-puerta-bajo-llave.md`.

***

Amiel Rusk discovered that it was possible to lock a door and lose authority over what lay behind it.

It happened at seven in the morning, when he arrived at the eastern floodgate with a court order, two officers, and an iron chain he had ordered from the courthouse hardware supplier. The building was awake. In the courtyard waited a maintenance crew, a cart of spare parts, and an official holding a folder so thick it was as though the weight of paper could stand in for the law.

“The control room cannot be shut down,” the official said.

“The order requires us to preserve its condition.”

“And the port must keep operating.”

“There's no contradiction if we leave the lines supplying the containment system operational.”

The man showed him an identification card from the Mareval Consortium. His name was Loden Vair, and he managed the repair contracts. Amiel wrote down the name without commenting on the fact that a private representative was directing the workers before the technical supervisor had even appeared.

“Who oversees the reserve circuit?” he asked.

“My engineers.”

“I didn't ask who pays them.”

Loden pointed toward the main entrance.

“Miss Calden has gone to inspect the secondary station. If you want to debate jurisdiction, wait until she gets back.”

Amiel studied the control-room door. The lock showed old scratches, but one of its screws gleamed as if it had just been fitted. Someone had replaced a component during the night, after the emergency and before the inspection.

“Open it,” he ordered.

“I don't have the key.”

“Then send for whoever does.”

Loden sighed and stepped away toward the outside telephone. Amiel asked his officers to photograph the lock. A woman from the maintenance crew, her arms covered in grease, brought over a lamp to improve the lighting.

“That screw didn't look like that yesterday,” she said quietly.

“Can you identify the previous one?”

“It was rusted. I tightened it last month.”

“I'll need your name and a statement when your shift ends.”

Her name was Irsa. She nodded without enthusiasm and went back to the cart.

Amiel had learned not to confuse cooperation with trust. People offered information for different reasons: professional pride, resentment, fear, or a desire to make someone else carry the burden of a decision. What mattered was finding out which parts could be verified independently of their word.

A quarter of an hour later, Neria arrived from the secondary station and stopped in front of the lock.

“That's new,” she said.

“Irsa thinks so too.”

“Who changed the screw?”

“We don't know yet.”

Neria examined the municipal seal they had placed during the first inspection. It was still attached to the doorframe, but the paper strip had lifted at one end.

“This door can be opened from inside,” she said.

Amiel glanced at the installation diagram.

“The plan doesn't show a second entrance.”

“Because that plan covers the renovation. The building is older.”

The engineer led him around to the rear facade. Beneath an iron staircase was a narrow opening, almost level with the ground, covered by a ventilation grille.

“It connects to the inspection passage,” she explained. “Nobody uses it to enter the control room. It's designed to let heat escape from the conductors.”

“Could a person fit through?”

“If they removed the grille. Not a very large person.”

Amiel knelt down. Two bolt heads bore recent scratches. The grille could be moved without forcing the main door or damaging the seal.

The problem was no longer deciding who had the key.

***

Neria refused to let them seal the ventilation opening.

“The backup accumulator is discharging into the containment line,” she explained. “If we close the duct, the temperature will rise. If we cut off all the power, one valve will lose its assistance and could jam under pressure.”

“How much time do we have?”

“I can't give you a figure without measuring it. And I'm not going to promise it will last until an expert arrives from the capital.”

Amiel asked her to show him the branches of the circuit. One power line supplied the winch and had been isolated after the emergency operation. A smaller one maintained regulation of the return flow. The second circuit didn't have anywhere near enough power to open a floodgate, but it could hold a position that had already been set. The heat it generated needed to escape through the duct.

“We can immobilize the controls and leave the cooling running,” Neria proposed. “But someone will have to check the regulator every hour.”

Loden returned from the telephone.

“The company will take on that work.”

“No,” Amiel said.

The contractor looked at him.

“Excuse me?”

“A company whose personnel may have had access to the room will not be solely responsible for preserving the evidence inside it. You may provide technicians. They will work alongside someone appointed by municipal maintenance, with every entry recorded.”

“That will cost money.”

“So will losing a valid chain of custody.”

Amiel drafted a report in the courtyard. He described the lock, the screw, the seals, and the grille bolts. He recorded why the ventilation needed to remain open and who was authorized to approach the equipment. An officer would read each entry aloud in front of a maintenance-crew witness.

Loden demanded a copy. He got one.

Then two Consortium guards arrived carrying a sealed tool chest. One said they had brought parts to replace a faulty pressure gauge. Neria ordered him to put the chest on the ground.

“Who requested the replacement?” she asked.

“It's in the internal work order.”

“Show me.”

The paper authorized replacement of the main indicator needle and bore the signature of a supervisor whose shift had ended before the accident. It specified no time for the work to be done.

Amiel decided he would not open that chest without an inventory.

“Let's secure the installed component first,” he said.

“If it's faulty, its readings are useless,” Loden objected.

“Which is why we need to examine it before removing it.”

The contractor raised his hands, as though everyone else's stubbornness were a domestic spectacle. But he didn't press the matter.

The control room was finally opened with the key held by the shift supervisor. Amiel entered with Neria, an officer, and a municipal clerk. Nobody touched the panel until the floor, gauges, and lever positions had been photographed.

The flow-recorder strip was still inside its casing. Neria recognized it by the chalk mark beside the needle. The notebook in which she had signed for opening the gate was there as well.

“That's how it was,” she said.

“Can you be certain nobody moved it?”

“I can be certain of what I see. Nothing more.”

Amiel was grateful that she didn't try to turn an observation into certainty.

As the clerk numbered the pages, he noticed a clean line between two layers of dust on the central gauge's mounting bracket. Something had been removed or shifted since the emergency ended. There was no way to know when.

He entered the observation in the report.

Before closing up, he also had the instruments' serial numbers photographed. A needle could be replaced; so could an entire housing. The numbers would make it possible to check later whether they were still looking at the same mechanism.

The clerk read the final sentence aloud. Loden asked to add that sealing the room would hinder repairs. Amiel accepted the comment and recorded beside it the obligation to maintain ventilation and shared monitoring.

The door was shut with two numbered seals and the iron chain.

At last, the inspector had a room in custody.

While the officers gathered their cameras, an elderly woman approached the gate with a letter in her hand. She asked whether the court could force the port to pay for her grandson's medicine. Amiel couldn't promise compensation. He gave her the address of the assistance service and wrote down her name to check that she received help. His own folder contained injuries, breakdowns, and signatures, but the damage was already spilling beyond every classification.

When he returned to the courtyard, Irsa was waiting beside the cart.

“You're preserving the wrong door,” she said.

Amiel felt a stab of exhaustion.

“Explain.”

The woman pointed to the wall separating the control room from the neighboring warehouse.

“The gauge shaft runs through there. There's an adjustment box on the other side. Anyone who knows how to use it doesn't need to enter this room to alter the reading.”

Amiel looked at the chain he had just installed.

The lock no longer felt like a victory.
