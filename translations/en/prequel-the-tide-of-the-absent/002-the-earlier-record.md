# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 002 · The Earlier Record

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Amiel Rusk, with a marked scene break. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/002-el-acta-anterior.md`.

***

At five in the morning, Amiel Rusk received two reports about the same accident.

The first bore the port administrator's signature and claimed that an unforeseen mechanical failure had caused partial flooding in the basin. It mentioned no injuries.

The second had been written in pencil by a municipal guard who had spent the night pulling people out of a shattered lighter. It recorded three injured people, a girl suffering from hypothermia, and the provisional disappearance of two dockworkers.

Amiel read both reports on the tram. He had three stops to go before reaching the docks. He didn't mark either document with the word “false.” He had learned that reports written from different places almost never described the same world, even when no one was lying.

Even so, it was hard for someone to be injured in an accident that, officially, had injured nobody.

He put the papers into the blue folder he used for investigations under municipal jurisdiction. He had to choose the right color from the outset. If he classified the matter as an incident involving arcane regulation, a supranational office could hold the instruments for weeks. If he classified it as bodily injury and possible negligence, he would have the authority to take statements and preserve civil records.

The distinction wasn't philosophical. It was the difference between entering a warehouse with an order and waiting outside for someone in uniform to take pity on him.

The tram stopped at Fish Square. The stalls were opening despite the turbulent night. One man was mending a net; another was arguing over the price of a shipment of sardines he had never seen. Amiel passed between them and reached the clinic.

Inside, the air smelled of warm disinfectant.

“I'm looking for the injured people from the eastern basin,” he told the nurse.

“There are more than the incident report lists.”

“How many?”

“Seven treated here. Two have left. One has a broken arm, and another can't remember his address because he hit his head. Don't write that last part down as a mysterious illness. He's dizzy.”

Amiel almost smiled. The nurse pointed him toward a small ward.

On the third bed, a red-haired young man held a tight bandage against his forehead. Beside him, an older man kept telling him not to speak.

“I'm Amiel Rusk, municipal inspector. Are you Sivan?”

The young man nodded.

“I need to ask you a few questions. You can decline until a workers' representative arrives.”

“He doesn't know anything,” the older man interrupted.

“Your name?”

“Toben. I'm his foreman.”

“Then you'll know which procedure you followed.”

Toben folded his arms. Amiel waited. Silence worked better than a threat when someone had something to say but hadn't yet decided how much.

“We went to close the return line,” Toben said at last. “The control showed closed.”

“And the valve?”

“Open. Someone had removed the stop.”

“Did you find the part?”

Sivan raised a hand.

“No. The retaining piece was there. The safety pin was missing. Without it, pressure could turn the stop.”

Amiel wrote down the correction. That wasn't the same as dismantling an entire valve. A pin might be missing through negligence; a valve taken apart during a flood suggested a different scale of interference.

“Who had access to the underpass?”

“Maintenance,” Toben said. “And outside inspectors, if they had authorization.”

“How many employees were on duty?”

“Eight.”

Amiel opened the list attached to the first report. It named six.

He didn't show it to them yet.

“Who can confirm those eight names?”

“The chief. Neria Calden.”

“Was she the one who opened the discharge gate?”

Toben looked at the injured young man before answering.

“If she hadn't, there'd be houses under water.”

Amiel put the sentence in quotation marks. It wasn't a technical conclusion. It was testimony about a decision. The courts would need to distinguish one from the other.

As he left the clinic, a woman in a soaked messenger's uniform was waiting for him, her hands raw from the cold. She held a receipt book as if it were a plank that could keep her afloat.

“You're the inspector,” she said.

“That depends on what you need.”

“My supervisor told me not to give this to anyone until someone from investigations arrived.”

Amiel examined the book without touching it.

“Is this municipal documentation?”

“A copy of the evacuation register. It was brought to the duty office before the first siren.”

“Who brought it?”

“A man from the cargo service. I didn't see his badge.”

“Who received the document?”

“I did.”

“At what time?”

“2:03.”

Amiel felt the floor tilt slightly beneath his feet. The first siren had sounded at 2:12.

“Do you remember the time, or did you write it down?”

The young woman opened the book carefully. One column showed an entry at 2:03 beside a receiving-clock stamp.

“I stamped it. Then I wrote down the time in case the mechanism failed.”

“Did you use the clock in the lobby?”

“The one at my desk. It's set every morning using the coastal signal.”

Amiel examined the mark: a small rectangular impression bearing the day's number. That strengthened the document's provenance. It didn't prove that the clock had been set correctly or that the messenger could identify the man.

“Is the order still in your office?”

“A copy was sent to maintenance. The port administrator took the original.”

“Did he give you a receipt?”

The woman took a moment to answer.

“He said it wasn't necessary.”

Amiel closed his blue folder. There was one possible route: ask a judge to preserve the copies and identify the person who had removed the original. There was also a faster route: ask a guard to search the administrator's office at once. The second would give him a victory that lasted a few hours and a case whose procedure would be thrown out forever.

“I'll need you to give a statement,” he said. “And no one is to use that clock again until we've examined it.”

“Did I do something wrong?”

“I don't yet know who did something wrong. You made an entry. I want to preserve it.”

***

Neria Calden received him beside the eastern floodgate, her eyelids swollen with exhaustion and a cup of hot water clasped between her hands.

Amiel had seen her only once, during a hearing on port safety. He remembered her correcting a lawyer three times, then apologizing for having corrected him only twice in the official minutes.

“Are you here to arrest me?” she asked now.

“I'm here to reconstruct the night.”

“They might amount to the same thing.”

The gate remained partially open. Beyond the mechanism, the canal was slowly receding. Wooden barriers had been installed to keep the curious away from the damaged transmission.

“I need the shift log and the evacuation order you received,” Amiel said.

Neria handed them over without hesitation.

“I want a receipt.”

“You'll have one.”

He examined the order's seal. It was sharp. Too sharp for a sheet of paper that, according to the messenger, had crossed the docks in the middle of an emergency.

“Is this your signature?”

“It looks like it.”

“I didn't ask whether it looks like it.”

Neria set her cup on a bench.

“I did not sign that document.”

“Can anyone confirm where you were at 1:58?”

“Toben. Sivan. There were three other workers in the workshop.”

“Did all of them see you at that exact time?”

“We don't keep a guard following every person around.”

“Then don't offer me certainty you don't have.”

Neria clenched her jaw. Then she pointed to the open regulator.

“What I can demonstrate is how much power the motor used. The traces are still there. The water came from the return line, not the sea.”

“And can you demonstrate that someone tampered with the installation?”

“I can demonstrate that a pin was missing. Determining why requires an inspection of the mechanism.”

“That's what I'm going to request.”

“The company wants to repair it before noon.”

Amiel looked up. Workers were waiting beside a box of new tools.

“Why the rush?”

“Because if the return line stays out of service, the port loses maneuvering capacity. And because every hour it's immobilized costs money.”

“Do you have the authority to stop them?”

“I can declare the equipment unsafe. I can't order a forensic examination.”

“I can request one. I can't make the water wait for the court.”

For the first time, Neria seemed to listen without preparing a retort.

Amiel wrote a provisional preservation order covering the shift records and called two officers to document the condition of the site. He didn't touch the place where the pin was missing. He ordered photographs of its housing, measurements of its edges, and the control panel sealed until an independent technician could attend.

One of the workers protested that this would delay service.

“I know,” Amiel said. “That's why two officials are here, so the delay and the reasons for it will be recorded too.”

As he left, Neria walked with him to the stairs.

“There's something else,” she said.

She showed him a second sheet, folded into quarters. It was a list of people evacuated under the 1:58 order. The destination column read “riverside shelter.”

“Who compiled this?” Amiel asked.

“I don't know. But the shelter has been closed since last winter.”

Amiel checked the signatures again. The list didn't say who had received the evacuees, only who had authorized their transfer.

Neria's name appeared at the bottom.

He looked at her.

“I need you to come with me to check the shelter.”

Neria put the empty cup on a shelf.

“How many people are on the list?”

Amiel counted the lines.

“Eleven.”

“Then perhaps we should start with a simpler question,” she said. “Where are they?”
