# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 045 · The Impossible Return

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Neria Calden. **Location:** Port Cendra and the marshes, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/045-el-retorno-imposible.md`.

***

Neria Calden had prepared two maps of the marshes. One showed pipes, water levels, and gates. The other included the walkways where families lived, families the land registry considered nonexistent.

On the municipal office table, both maps occupied the same space. On the first, a mechanical failure could be identified without revealing anything about the people who needed water. On the second, every path ended beside a home, a kitchen, or a school.

The Civic Council had called a hearing to present preliminary findings from the flood investigation. The news drew merchants demanding accountability, skippers seeking compensation, and relatives of the missing.

The chair asked for every map and list obtained during the investigation to be published.

“The city has a right to know what happened,” the lead representative said.

Neria agreed. For weeks she'd shown that readings couldn't be replaced by convenient statements and contracts had to reflect work performed.

But full publication could reveal the shelter's entrances, water stores, and routes used by people fearing employer retaliation.

“I propose presenting the hydraulic data without identifying private residences,” she replied. “The personal-protection annexes can be reviewed under judicial supervision.”

“Are you trying to hide the findings?” a councilor asked.

“I want to distinguish which findings prove an operation and which information merely helps locate someone who's lost their home.”

Fara Nel took notes. Beside him, Amiel Rusk remained silent, the railway transport file under his arm.

The inspector could explain the legal limits. Neria's job was to identify which data were essential to repair the canal.

***

The first part of the hearing dealt with information that could be disclosed without restrictions.

Neria explained that the Kelor conduit had supplied industrial reservoirs during the emergency. The records could verify an extraordinary increase, but storage, losses, and transit times still had to be compared before the flood could be attributed to it. She described the relief-channel gate, its worn retaining mechanism, and the gauges whose readings differed from independent scales.

Nobody had asked for a name yet.

Vela then presented the signal records. She explained the differences between transmission, repetition, and reception, and recalled that the physical selector removed from the booth couldn't be treated as conclusive evidence because its seal had been replaced without documentation. The earlier photographs and energy records retained their own evidentiary value, subject to assessment.

“So there was a deliberate diversion?” a journalist asked.

Vela distinguished the observed mechanical intervention from the identity and purpose of whoever had carried it out.

Neria saw some in the audience sigh in frustration. They wanted a sentence that would turn the accident into a finished story.

Amiel then presented the railway documents. A train had run, and a carriage had been reserved for eleven people, but there was no reliable record that the group had been received. The case of Baret Luno showed that at least one of the listed names belonged to a man who had died in Port Cendra before the scheduled departure.

“And the other ten?” a woman shouted from the back.

“They still require individual verification,” the inspector replied.

Neria saw Dalia, Erven Tal's sister, clutch a folder against her chest.

She had no easy answer.

***

The conflict began when the chair asked to project the complete canal map and the provisional register of marsh residents.

Talia was present as a representative of the shelter assembly. She stood before the clerk could open the annex.

“We haven't authorized the release of names to contractors,” she said.

“They're necessary data for assessing damage and encroachment,” a representative replied.

“Then explain who needs which information and for what decision. Don't put our children on a list beside the access routes.”

Fara asked to suspend the annex until its legal status was clear.

A man from Mareval objected. He argued that the company needed to know which structures stood within the maintenance strip in order to plan the works.

“You can receive the route plan and the technical restrictions,” Neria replied. “You don't need each resident's employment history to calculate a pipe section.”

The representative insisted the works required removing obstacles.

Neria unfolded the first map.

“There are alternatives: repair the inlet valve, reinforce the inspection walkway, and maintain a temporary drinking-water branch while we assess the channel. None has been approved yet. But it hasn't been established that every home must be removed, either.”

The tone of the room changed. The question was no longer whether people were living where the map showed an empty strip, but what solutions could protect the flow, public safety, and the right of those already living there to remain.

Fara requested an alternatives study involving the shelter and the hydraulic authority.

The chair didn't agree immediately.

“The city needs results, not another committee.”

“An irreversible excavation produces results, too,” Neria replied. “The fact that they're damage doesn't make them acceptable.”

***

During the recess, Talia found her in the corridor.

“If they publish the walkway paths,” she said, “tomorrow we'll have rent inspectors, creditors, and companies looking for people who don't want to return to the port.”

“I can't promise that no authority will ever have access,” Neria replied. “There are legitimate requests and dangerous situations. We can require grounds, limit copies, and prevent everything from being made public.”

Talia lowered her eyes.

“Some families could go back if they received their wages and papers. Others have nowhere to go.”

Neria thought about the word *return*. Official forms used it as though the road were reversible: a person left the port, received assistance, and then returned to the box where they'd been registered.

But homes had disappeared, employment contracts had been broken, and some children knew only the shelter's school. Physically going back didn't mean recovering what had been lost.

“We mustn't decide for them,” she said. “Or force them to stay for the sake of our investigation.”

Amiel approached with a proposal from the magistrate. The annexes containing personal data could be preserved under controlled access; technical reports would be published in a form sufficient for public scrutiny and challenges from the company. Families would retain ways to provide specific information voluntarily.

“It's not an absolute secret,” the inspector explained. “It's a separation of purposes. What's relevant to a repair goes to whoever must carry it out; what's needed to investigate a possible crime remains under procedure; private information doesn't become a spectacle.”

Talia listened without nodding yet.

“And the food?” she asked.

Breda, who was taking part in the accounting review, had documented several actual deliveries and reduced the outstanding discrepancy to two sacks whose destination was still unconfirmed. Assistance didn't have to wait for the release of an entire register.

Amiel confirmed that the magistrate had provided for that separation.

They'd found a partial way forward: not justice guaranteed, but a way to prevent new harm while the investigation continued.

***

The hearing resumed at dusk.

Fara presented two agreements: publish hydraulic maps and inspection findings with their technical limitations clearly stated, and reserve the nominal annexes for specific proceedings under access controls and review. He also requested a temporary suspension of irreversible work on occupied homes until a social assessment and safety alternatives had been completed.

The first proposal won a large majority. The second passed by a single vote.

The Mareval representative announced that he would seek a review of the deadlines. The chair warned that the city couldn't be brought to a standstill. Neria agreed that the final document should allow urgent work: repairing leaks, keeping gates safe, and guaranteeing water to the shelters.

As they left, Dalia approached her.

“Does this help find Erven?” she asked.

Neria held her gaze.

“It helps ensure the records can be investigated without putting other people in danger. I don't have any confirmed news about your brother.”

Dalia nodded wearily.

“When you learn something, don't make me find out from a noticeboard.”

“I'll tell Amiel.”

Neria didn't promise to control communications that weren't hers to manage.

***

That night she returned to Willow Crossing with Irsa. They carried an authorized level gauge and a copy of the urgent maintenance agreement. The water moved slowly between the pilings. Farther away, lights were burning in the shelter kitchen.

Talia was waiting beside the gate.

“Did we win?” she asked.

“We got them to stop calling a home empty land without first examining the consequences of the work,” Neria replied. “There's still a long way to go.”

The coordinator showed her a ration notebook. They had received enough food for the next few days, but several families were debating whether to stay or look for work in other cities.

“Some want to return to the port,” she said. “Others think that if they go back, the same records will deny they were ever here.”

Neria watched the canal. The records showed flows, not human destinations. Engineering could improve a conduit, control a valve, and prevent a flood. It couldn't restore a lost home or turn a forced return into restitution.

Irsa read the final gauge.

The level was within the permitted range.

A boat crossed toward Costa Interior without stopping. She didn't know who was aboard and didn't ask.

In the inspection notebook, she wrote: “Passage operational; provisional supply secured; residents not enumerated under this procedure.”

Beneath it, she left a blank space for the next measurement.

It wasn't an ending. It was a decision about what must not be sacrificed to reach the next one.
