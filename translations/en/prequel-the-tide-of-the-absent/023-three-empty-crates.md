# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 023 · Three Empty Crates

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Orin Farel. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/023-tres-cajas-vacias.md`.

***

The three crates looked empty until one of the men tried to lift the third.

Orin Farel watched the cargo from the repair dock. Good Shadow was still out of the water, propped on beams, and he'd negotiated for his crew to use Mara Veis's tugboat for two smaller jobs. It was a way to pay wages without sinking the cooperative any deeper into debt than necessary.

That morning's job offered an attractive price: move a platform from the south depot to the railway landing. The distance was short, the tide favorable, and the client offered to advance the fuel money.

There was one condition.

“You won't need a manifest,” the intermediary said. “They're three empty containers being returned to the depot.”

The man wore an Estuary Cargo identification badge. He introduced himself as Varo Sen and pointed to wooden crates on a cart. They had iron-reinforced edges and old labels covered with gray paint.

Orin looked at Solen, who kept the provisions accounts.

“We need a description of the load, even if it's empty,” he replied. “And a declared weight for the tow.”

“I'm telling you: they're empty.”

“Then writing it down shouldn't be difficult.”

Varo smiled as though Orin had asked him an unnecessary favor.

“It's an internal delivery. We don't want to complicate the paperwork.”

Orin knew internal deliveries. They were common between docks belonging to the same company. Even so, the skipper remained responsible for calculating his platform's stability and knowing what substances it carried. The crates might hold tools, waste, discharged accumulators, or ordinary goods. Each demanded different precautions.

A loader put his hands beneath the third crate and raised one end. The wood struck the ground with a sound far too solid for an empty container.

“It's heavy,” he said.

Varo stepped in immediately.

“There's an inner support left inside.”

“Then there's something to declare,” Orin replied.

The intermediary moved the loader aside and told him to wait by the cart.

“Do you need money, or do you need a piece of paper?” he asked quietly.

Orin thought of his boat lying open on the beams and the wages he still owed. He didn't answer immediately.

***

Mara arrived before they loaded the first crate. She knew the job's terms because her vessel would provide the towing power.

“I haven't received a manifest,” she said.

Orin explained the conversation. She examined the painted-over labels and noticed a railway transport mark on the third crate.

“If there's an inner support, they should know how much it weighs,” she observed.

Varo raised his hands.

“You can weigh them here. That would settle the argument.”

There was a set of scales beside the dock. Orin agreed to weigh the crates without opening them. The first two weighed about as much as reinforced packing containers. The third was considerably heavier.

“Perhaps the entire frame was left inside,” Varo said.

“Then record that,” Mara replied.

The refusal came without a raised voice.

“My client doesn't authorize the disclosure of component details. It's industrial property.”

Orin felt the deal slipping away. He could accept a manifest with a generic commercial description and hazard conditions; he didn't need secret designs. What he couldn't accept was towing cargo of unknown mass and nature.

“We aren't asking for drawings,” he said. “We're asking for mass, the nature of the goods, the delivery point, and the person responsible for receiving them.”

Varo pulled a sheet from his folder. It was a service order, not a manifest. It listed the price, route, and a space for the skipper's signature. A clause in small print stated that the carrier accepted the load “as presented, without liability on the consignor's part for losses resulting from inadequate identification.”

Orin read the sentence twice.

“I won't sign this.”

“We've already hired the tug.”

“We have a provisional booking. Acceptance was subject to cargo documentation.”

Varo pointed toward Mara's boat.

“The company will cover the fuel if you make the trip. Otherwise we'll give the job to another skipper and claim the delay from you.”

Mara looked at Orin. She didn't ask him to give in, but her silence carried weight. Two families were counting on the day's wages.

Orin removed the pen from the sheet and laid it on the table.

“You can give it to someone else. I want it on record that we offered to do the job with a proper manifest and the company refused to provide one.”

Varo closed the folder.

“That won't keep you from losing the contract.”

“I know.”

***

The other skipper arrived an hour later. His name was Cevan Dorr, and he'd been working independently for only a short while. His boat was smaller than Mara's but authorized to operate in the transfer canal.

Orin stopped him before he signed.

“The third crate weighs a lot more than the others,” he said. “The consignor won't declare what's in it.”

Cevan looked at him with hostility.

“Trying to take the job away after turning it down?”

“Trying to keep you from doing it blind.”

“I've got debts that can't wait for an entire port investigation to end.”

Orin recognized the argument. It was the same one he'd almost used to justify the choice to himself.

“Ask for a weight and a recipient's signature,” he said. “Nothing more.”

Cevan replied that his contract included insurance and that he didn't need advice.

Orin stepped aside. He couldn't stop the man on his own authority. He could report a cargo irregularity involving a missing manifest to dock control, and he did so in writing. The traffic inspector replied that she would have to check the documents before authorizing departure. That review belonged to the authorities, not the cooperative.

Varo was furious when he saw the officer arrive.

“Now you're trying to shut down the dock as well,” he accused.

“I asked them to check a shipment,” Orin replied. “If it's in order, it can leave.”

The review established that the crates had no signed document acknowledging receipt at their destination. The record had to be completed before transport. No one opened the packaging, and no criminal infringement was alleged.

Cevan eventually accepted a new form that the consignor prepared in haste. Orin never saw what it said and couldn't be certain it resolved all the risks.

The tow departed around noon.

His cooperative earned nothing.

Solen came over as the platform disappeared behind the warehouses.

“We lost two days' work,” he said.

“I know.”

“Will you tell Mir we can't pay his advance?”

Orin felt a stab of shame.

“I'll tell him myself.”

***

At the cooperative shed, the members argued again about the common fund. Mara explained the lost job and asked that Orin's decision not become an excuse to blame the entire flotilla.

“The contract was irregular,” she said. “That doesn't erase our need for income.”

Solen suggested accepting more fuel and provisions deliveries even if they paid less. Mir proposed selling a spare part from Good Shadow to cover wages. Orin clenched his teeth at the idea. He'd bought that part precisely to avoid depending on the consortium.

“If we sell it, repairing the boat will take longer,” he said.

“If we aren't paid this week, we might not have a crew left to sail it,” Mir replied.

Orin opened the accounts box. He separated the money available, outstanding wages, and the cost of the most urgent repair. There wasn't enough. He proposed paying every sailor an equal portion and recording the rest as a cooperative debt, with a review date.

Nobody celebrated the proposal. But nobody asked him to conceal the shortfall.

When they finished, Mara handed him a copy of the refusal notice signed by the consignor. On the back was a description added to justify switching skippers: “Three empty packages, return of regulating accessories.”

Orin read it.

“Now they admit there are accessories,” he said.

“They wrote it after we weighed them. That doesn't prove what they carried.”

Orin nodded.

The corner of the document bore the original shipment's consignment number. He recognized it from a copy of the missing-parts claim Neria had shown at the assembly.

He didn't know what was in the crates. But he did know that the code belonged to the same transport batch that had left retainers missing from the port machinery.

He asked Mara to keep her copy and prepared a notice for Amiel. He didn't want to turn a suspicion into a grand accusation. It was enough to ask why one consignment note was being used both to return supposedly empty containers and to account for spare parts that couldn't be found in the machines.

Before he could leave, Solen called him from the doorway.

“Cevan came back without the platform,” he said.

“That means he delivered the crates.”

“Maybe. I saw him unload near the railway landing. Then he went straight to the clinic.”

Orin went still.

“Is he hurt?”

“I don't know. He said he had a terrible headache and the traffic officer needed to inspect a leak on deck.”

Orin picked up his coat.

He'd refused an undocumented load to protect his crew. Now he needed to find out whether another man had taken the risk he wouldn't accept.
