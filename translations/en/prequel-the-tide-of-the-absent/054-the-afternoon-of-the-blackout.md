# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 054 · The Afternoon of the Blackout

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Orin Farel. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/054-la-tarde-del-apagon.md`.

***

The first lamp went out in the middle of a question.

Orin Farel was sitting in the last row of the courtroom, beside Solen and Mir. He had come to hear Vela's testimony and expected the session to end before the cooperative's shift change. Just as the lawyer asked for the network diagram to be projected, the room went dark.

Someone cried out. A chair toppled backward. The hum of the ventilation fans stopped abruptly.

“Everyone stay where you are,” Yuna Tor ordered from the bench.

The emergency lamps took a few seconds to come on. A yellow light illuminated the aisle and left the rest of the room in shadow. The court recorder clicked and went dead.

The clerk checked the auxiliary accumulator.

“The lights are on reserve,” he said. “The recording system isn't responding.”

Yuna suspended the hearing until safety conditions and the continuity of the record could be verified. It was not a dramatic decision. Without a reliable recording, testimony could be left incomplete; without ventilation, the room would become unsafe if it remained crowded.

Orin went outside with the others. The market had lost its lights, and the drawbridge stood motionless. A rumble of engines died away in a chain toward the harbor.

“It isn't just the courthouse,” Solen said.

Mir pointed toward the warehouse row.

“The cold-storage depot is dark too.”

Orin felt the afternoon's work change all at once.

***

The cooperative's tug was moored at the lower quay. Its main engine could run without the public grid, but the electric mooring winch and the shed lights depended on municipal power.

When Orin arrived, he found Mara Veis arguing with a warehouse supervisor.

“I can't open the cold rooms without power,” the man was saying. “The backup generator is reserved for the main refrigeration system.”

“If the tug can't get out, the grain barge will be left across the channel,” Mara replied.

Orin looked toward the basin. Barge Seven was moored at an awkward angle, and a cross-current was pushing it against the wooden fender. It was not in immediate danger, but if the water rose during the unloading operation, it could block the passage.

“How much fuel do we have?” he asked.

“Enough for the maneuver and not much more,” Solen said. “The tank hasn't been refilled since yesterday.”

Orin ordered the engine prepared and the towline checked. They would not use the electric winch. Mir and another sailor ran a safety line through the quay's manual capstan, while Mara asked the warehouse to keep the access clear for a mechanical crane.

The supervisor refused to disconnect the cold-storage room.

“I've got goods worth more than the tug.”

“And I've got a grain barge that can't be left across the channel,” Orin said. “I'm not asking you to switch off the refrigeration. I'm asking you not to connect another motor until we know what's happening.”

The man looked at the distribution box.

“I don't know when the power will come back.”

“That's why we're going to work as if it won't.”

***

The cooperative's radio ran on an independent battery. Orin received a notice from the distribution station: the northern feeder had tripped on overcurrent, and technicians were isolating the section. There had been no scheduled shutdown order and no switching signal from the gate station.

The operator asked industrial users not to reconnect equipment on their own.

“How long will it take?” Orin asked.

“There is no estimate. First we have to inspect the outgoing breaker.”

Orin relayed the instruction to the basin's captains. No one was to operate cranes, pumps, or electric winches until authorization was given. The cooperative would work with its own engine and manual equipment.

The tug pulled away from the quay at minimum speed. Solen handled the helm; Mir and Orin watched the towline. The barge responded slowly, but the line stayed taut. They moved it to a side berth that did not depend on the electric winch.

A toolbox fell overboard and sank. Mir swore. Orin would not let anyone jump into the water to retrieve it.

“We've lost it,” he said. “We're not going to lose anyone chasing it.”

At the quay, several workers had begun moving food crates out of a storage room. The emergency generator was running, but its capacity was enough to keep the main compressor and the temperature alarm operating, not to light the entire warehouse.

Orin organized the crew to move only the products that could be kept in insulated containers. They had to work quickly, but they could not open every cold room at once.

A woman who worked in the depot approached with a list.

“If we move these crates, I need to know where they're going. I don't want anyone saying tomorrow that they disappeared in the blackout.”

Orin took the pencil.

“We'll count every one.”

***

At the eastern station, Neria had ordered that no gates be operated during the power cut. The regulation accumulators were holding the system in its safe position and the manual controls remained available, but the reserves were not to be spent on unnecessary maneuvers.

Orin found her beside Irsa, checking a portable panel.

“Did the grid go down because of an order?” he asked.

“The distribution log shows a feeder trip. There was no remote disconnection order,” Neria replied. “The technicians are inspecting the breaker.”

“Can we use the tug's generator?”

“Not to power the station. That circuit isn't authorized, and we could create a dangerous phase difference.”

Orin nodded. He had learned not to offer power where it was not needed.

“The barge is clear of the channel. The cold-storage depot is running on limited backup.”

“I need you to keep the passage clear. If the station has to discharge by gravity, we can't have a vessel blocking the way.”

“We will.”

A distribution technician arrived with an assistant and a toolbox. He confirmed that the feeder breaker had opened because of an overcurrent. The initial inspection had found an overheated contact on one phase, with prior wear and signs of electrical arcing. There was no indication of external interference, although the final report would have to wait until the assembly was dismantled.

“How long will the repair take?” Neria asked.

“We won't close the feeder until we replace the contact and measure insulation. It could take several hours.”

Neria looked at the reserve panel. The station's accumulator had lost a small fraction of its charge to keep the regulator running, within the expected limits. If the outage continued, they would have to reduce nonessential functions and check the levels manually.

Orin understood that the night would not be over when the lights came back.

***

At the courthouse, Yuna received the technical notice. She ordered the suspension record to include the time, the general failure, and the condition of the recording equipment. The parties could submit written observations, but no testimony would be treated as complete unless the system had preserved the full recording.

Mareval's lawyer asked for the session to resume with a manual transcript.

“We have no guarantee that a written record would reproduce every statement,” the magistrate replied. “We can continue with scheduling matters, not with testimony that must be preserved in full.”

Dalia Tal protested from the second row.

“Every day this is postponed, the documents keep deteriorating.”

Yuna listened to her.

“The documents are preserved under the measures already in force. I will not pretend an incomplete hearing is a valid hearing.”

Orin noticed the woman gripping a folded recipe between her fingers. He approached her as the room began to empty.

“The blackout doesn't seem to have targeted the courthouse,” he said.

“I didn't say it had.”

“People at the port are already saying someone cut the power to stop the testimony.”

Dalia looked at the dark windows.

“People need an explanation.”

“They need one that's true.”

The answer did not comfort her. Orin had not expected it to.

***

Power returned later that night, first to municipal buildings and then to the warehouses. The northern feeder remained disconnected while technicians replaced the contact. The cold-storage room had kept its temperature within range thanks to the generator; two crates of fresh produce were set aside as a precaution and recorded as a potential loss, not as stolen goods.

Barge Seven remained at the side berth until the current stabilized. Orin handed the maneuver log and the crate inventory to the port office.

Neria came by the quay to check that the return of power would not start the motors all at once. The circuits were reconnected in stages, with voltage and temperature measurements. No gate opened automatically and no water level changed. The system had remained stable because no one had tried to force it.

“Will the hearing resume tomorrow?” Orin asked.

“I don't know. First they have to certify the courthouse recorder and feeder.”

Orin looked at the still boats.

The city had suffered a real blackout. There was no invisible hand behind every switch and no new culprit lurking in every shadow. There was a worn contact, an excessive load, and maintenance that had not been done in time.

But the failure had interrupted a session that mattered.

When he got home, he left his boots by the door and wrote in the cooperative's logbook: “Barge relocated. No injuries. Two crates under observation. General grid outage; preliminary technical cause pending.”

He did not add that the courthouse had gone dark just as the evidence was being discussed.

The coincidence was real.

The reason was still unknown.
