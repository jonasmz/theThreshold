# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 047 · The Jurisdictional Boundary

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Saria Enel. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/047-la-linea-de-competencia.md`.

***

Saria Enel arrived at the courthouse with an empty box and an order nobody wanted to sign.

The Conclave delegate had spent the night reviewing energy-regulation licenses. One of the cold-storage chamber's batteries at kilometer forty-three had been installed under a regional certification; another, the small relay bank at the old shelter, was listed as municipal equipment withdrawn from service.

The Conclave's register showed a third component that neither office had inventoried: a stabilizer module associated with the port's signal lines.

“We request its surrender for a technical examination,” Saria told Magistrate Yuna Tor.

Yuna examined the order.

“Under what authority?”

“The safety of regulated devices and the preservation of their components. We aren't investigating the deaths or the workers' whereabouts.”

Amiel Rusk, seated beside the clerk, looked up.

“One of the objects you're describing may be part of our evidence,” he said.

“That's why I won't remove it without a coordination record.”

Saria had seen similar conflicts at facilities in several cities. A precision instrument could be privately owned, subject to municipal inspection, and contain a component whose license fell under the Conclave. None of those relationships canceled out the others.

Yuna pointed to the disputed seal on the selector examined by Amina Dorel.

“This object has an interrupted chain of custody. Do you intend to take it as though it were intact evidence?”

“No. I request an examination of its physical identity where it's being stored, with the parties present. If we need to dismantle a component to prevent a hazard, that will require separate justification.”

The magistrate laid the order on the table.

“Then let's start by establishing what you're actually claiming.”

***

The jurisdiction hearing took place in a small room. Saria brought an inventory of licensed equipment, with installation dates and serial numbers. It contained no refugee lists or policyholder data.

Loden Vair attended on behalf of Mareval. He argued that the Conclave shouldn't interfere in a port contract made under municipal jurisdiction.

“Repairing a valve is the port's business,” he said.

“Repairing an ordinary valve, yes,” Saria replied. “A regulated stabilizer module needs its own certification, whether it's installed in a factory, a dam, or a rented room.”

“There is no proof that the module caused the flood.”

“I'm not claiming that, either.”

Saria was irritated at having to repeat such a simple distinction. The intervention didn't depend on proving a crime. A safety license could be reviewed because of a faulty installation, an incomplete history, or an operational risk.

Neria Calden presented the feeder diagram. She had documented rising consumption in the cold-storage chamber and a procedure requiring more power than authorized. The batteries had finite reserves. A badly calculated discharge wouldn't create additional energy; it would cause overheating, loss of regulation, and potential harm to equipment or people.

“If you remove the module immediately,” Neria warned, “you could push thermal storage outside its safe range.”

Saria nodded.

“We first need a safe configuration and a certified replacement.”

The tenant's lawyer asked that the examination be postponed until the commercial disputes were resolved.

“Ownership of an asset doesn't suspend a safety obligation,” Saria replied. “But any intervention must be proportionate and documented.”

Yuna asked that the technical agreement be recorded. Conventional refrigeration would remain in operation, use of the regulator would be restricted, and no component would be removed until it was safe to disconnect it.

The empty box Saria had brought would remain empty.

***

During the recess, Amiel showed her a reference from the emergency-intervention requests rescued from the flooded archive. The cover mentioned the Regulatory Delegation, but the pages were still undergoing conservation.

“We don't know which file it belongs to,” he said.

Saria studied the numbering.

“I can check whether our office received a communication with that number. I can't reconstruct the book's contents just because I recognize the series.”

“That's what I need: an independent source.”

Saria called her office and asked them to consult the incoming register, not every operator's private files. The clerk read out three entries. Two were routine maintenance authorizations. The third concerned an inquiry about modifying signal routes for industrial purposes.

The date was before the flood.

“Who submitted it?” Amiel asked.

“A municipal technical office. The register identifies only the requesting unit and says a response was pending.”

“Was it approved?”

“No approval is recorded. Nor was it denied. We need to locate the file and establish what was actually submitted.”

Saria wrote the information on her own sheet. A number on a cover and an entry in another register might indicate a connection, but they weren't enough to establish who had carried out an operation.

Amiel seemed to accept the incomplete answer.

“Could the Conclave request every document from the port?” he asked.

“Not through this process. And I have no intention of trying.”

She said it more forcefully than necessary. She had worked for an institution accustomed to deference whenever its technicians used the word *risk*. That habit could become a dangerous privilege if extended to matters that belonged to judges and citizens.

***

The depot inspection took place under Amina Dorel's supervision. The signal selector remained in a box with a seal different from the one attached when it was originally removed. The expert explained the break in the chain of custody again.

Saria requested photographs, external measurements, and a comparison of manufacturing numbers. She didn't touch the clamp that had immobilized the mechanism during the initial observation.

“Do you recognize the stabilizer module?” Yuna asked.

“I recognize a housing compatible with one of our models. I can't certify that the interior is the same without opening it, and opening it would require a procedure that preserves the other evidence.”

The technician accompanying her checked, without powering the device, that no dangerous charge remained in the accessible connections. He recorded the margin of uncertainty.

“We can prepare a joint opening,” he suggested.

Mareval's lawyer objected that the operation would alter the disputed object. Amina replied that there were examination techniques that could document every movement and keep the parts separate. Yuna didn't authorize dismantling it that afternoon; she requested a written protocol and an opportunity for the parties to appoint observers.

Saria accepted.

She had arrived with an order that seemed sufficient to take an object away and was leaving with an obligation to explain how to examine it without destroying its value.

That, too, was an exercise of authority.

***

That night, the Conclave's regional director summoned her to his office. An official had reported that the magistrate hadn't handed over the equipment.

“The safety of these mechanisms cannot depend on every local court,” the director said.

Saria placed the technical report on the table.

“It doesn't. Power and connection conditions are inspected under our license. Custody of an object as evidence in a civil proceeding also depends on the court.”

“There are deaths connected to the infrastructure.”

“And it's not our place to investigate them without jurisdiction. Demanding the equipment doesn't give us authority over the bodies.”

The director looked at her for a long moment.

“Do you know what it will cost to coordinate three inspectors, lawyers, and technicians for a single component?”

“Less than making it unusable as evidence or causing a discharge by removing it improperly.”

She received authorization to proceed, with a written warning about deadlines. Saria signed the acknowledgment. She disliked losing days to meetings, but had learned that institutional haste could conceal decisions no one would dare justify in public.

As she left, she found a note from the registry: the municipal inquiry filed before the flood mentioned a signal branch intended for a stabilizer module in a depot listed as closed.

The address belonged to the old riverside shelter.

Saria read the detail twice, then checked the final line.

The application hadn't been approved. It had been returned for completion of the safety assessment.

Someone might have carried out a modification without waiting for authorization. It might also have been a project never implemented, unrelated to the connections observed later.

Saria kept both possibilities in her notebook.

The line she had to draw wasn't merely a boundary between institutions. It was the distance between proving that a device existed, claiming that it had operated, and assigning it responsibility for a life lost.
