# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 034 · The Closed School

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Vela Noren. **Location:** Marsh school, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/034-la-escuela-cerrada.md`.

***

The school hadn't closed because its roof had collapsed. It had closed because nobody knew what name to put on the rent receipt.

Vela Noren found the classrooms empty when she reached the southern end of the marshes. The desks were stacked beneath a tarpaulin, and a five-digit sum was still written on a blackboard. A gray-haired woman was sweeping the floor without taking her eyes off the doorway.

“I'm Alna Peret,” she said. “I taught here before they announced the relocation.”

“I'm here about the warnings on the night of the flood,” Vela replied. “I was told some children remembered a signal.”

The teacher set down her broom.

“They remember many things. Some happened here; others were told to them by their parents. If you're going to ask questions, don't put them all in one room so they can repeat one another's answers.”

Vela appreciated the warning. She had spent days separating signals sent, signals received, and signals reconstructed afterward. Human memory deserved the same care, especially when the witnesses were children.

Permission to visit the school came from the shelter coordinator and each child's guardian. It didn't grant access to their civil identities or permission to take them to court. Alna insisted on being present for every conversation.

“Why is it closed?” Vela asked as they prepared the tables.

“The building contract is in the old shelter's name. Now they say the building can't operate as a school because its use isn't registered.”

“How long had you been teaching here?”

“Two winters.”

The woman pointed to a row of notebooks.

“The children learned to read before somebody discovered that the building didn't exist in the right category.”

***

The first to enter was Milo, a ten-year-old boy with a shiny pebble in his pocket. Vela explained that she wasn't looking for a correct answer, only what he remembered himself. If he didn't know something, he could say so. If he wanted to stop, they would stop.

“Where were you when you heard the sirens?” she asked.

“At the house by the willow. My mother was heating water.”

“What did you hear?”

Milo struck the table three times, with uneven pauses.

“One short, two long, and another short,” he said.

Vela recorded the rhythm without translating it into a code. She couldn't ask a child to reconstruct an exact sequence after so many days; what he produced might be an approximation.

“Where was the sound coming from?”

“From the bridge road.”

“Did you see it?”

“No. I saw lights afterward.”

The teacher raised a hand.

“Milo, you said earlier that you saw the lights from the window. Was it open?”

The boy thought.

“No. My sister opened the door when it sounded the second time.”

Vela separated the observations in her notebook. There was a memory of sound and another of light. They didn't necessarily belong to the same device or the same instant.

A girl named Orel said the school bell had rung that night. Alna explained that the building hadn't had a bell for months: the metal had broken, and they now used an iron plate to call the children to class.

“Could you have heard another bell from the village?” Vela asked.

“Maybe. It was windy.”

The answer didn't erase her memory. It placed it among several possibilities.

Vela didn't show the tower's codes or mention the decommissioned siren. If she supplied the expected answer first, any later match would lose some of its value.

***

Alna allowed her to speak to two siblings who had spent the night on the northern walkway. The older child remembered a green light in the bridge hut; the younger remembered a yellow one. They argued about the color until the teacher gently separated them.

“You don't have to agree,” she said.

Vela asked them to draw what they could see from their home. Both placed the hut to the left of the canal, behind a tree. Only the older child included the lamp. The younger drew a boat beside the steps.

“That was Edran's boat,” the boy said.

“I didn't ask whose boat it was,” Vela replied. “Did you see it that night, or do you know it's usually there?”

He erased a line.

“It's always there. I don't know if it was there that night.”

Vela felt a surge of admiration for his ability to correct himself without treating it as defeat.

The teacher then took out an attendance notebook. It didn't contain accounts of the emergency, only ordinary notes: rainy days, unfinished assignments, and which children had arrived late. On the page following the flood, one sentence read: “Pupils report four-beat signal from bridge.”

“I wrote that the same morning,” Alna explained. “I hadn't read the port's announcements yet.”

“Did they all say four beats?”

“No. Three children repeated that pattern. Others only remembered a noise.”

Vela requested permission to photograph the entire page and its context, not just the sentence. The date would need to be checked against the preceding and following entries. A contemporary record could be valuable without guaranteeing the accuracy of the recollection.

When they were finished, Alna closed the notebook.

“Will this help find someone?”

“It might help us establish which warning reached this part of the marshes,” Vela replied. “I can't promise more than that.”

The teacher nodded.

“Then don't tell the children they've saved the investigation. Let them go back to playing.”

***

Vela walked to the old bridge with Peran, who had come from the tower to measure the acoustic range. The wind was blowing inland. At that hour, the hut was closed and the auxiliary line remained under judicial preservation.

They couldn't activate the siren or recreate the emergency sound at will. They could measure distances, the orientation of horns, and obstacles posed by the vegetation. Even an approximate propagation calculation would also require the wind direction and the device's condition on that night.

Peran studied the siblings' drawing.

“You can see the hut's lamp from here,” he said.

“When there are no leaves on the tree. Right now it's hidden.”

“The station might have been lit for a test.”

“Yes.”

Vela compared the circuit sketch with a drawing of the bridge. The auxiliary repeater in the shelter hut could carry impulses toward the freight line, but that didn't mean every sound people heard had come from it. There were factory sirens, canal warning devices, and household bells.

A woman watched them from the walkway. She carried a sleeping child against her chest.

“Are you looking for the signal from that night?” she asked.

“We're checking what could have been heard from here,” Vela replied.

“My son got out of bed because he thought we had to leave. My husband thought it was a factory alarm.”

“What did you do?”

“We stayed. The little one had a fever, and we couldn't carry him through the mud.”

Vela recorded the account only after asking permission. The woman agreed to speak but didn't want her name in a public document.

“If they say we disobeyed an evacuation, we'll lose our assistance,” she explained.

“I'm not determining any family's responsibility. I'm examining the conditions under which warnings were received.”

The woman pointed toward the distant tower.

“At the port, they think a signal reaches every house the same way. Here, the wind decides who wakes up.”

Vela looked at the walkways and canvas roofs. She understood that a warning could be transmitted correctly and still fail because of an ordinary obstacle: distance, noise, illness, or confusion.

***

When she returned to the school, she found Alna gathering drawings. The teacher had set one aside. It wasn't by a child interviewed that morning but by a pupil who had left the shelter with her family two days after the flood.

The paper showed a boat, three houses, and a sequence of strokes beside the word “bridge.”

“The girl said they were the lights she saw,” Alna explained. “She drew it before you came.”

Vela examined the drawing. The strokes didn't exactly reproduce the code they were seeking. They might represent movement, flashes, or simply a way of filling an empty space.

“Do you know where the family is?” she asked.

“Only that they took the road toward Costa Interior. I don't have a reliable address.”

Vela handed the paper back.

“Keep it here. I'll record that it exists, but I won't take it without the family's permission.”

Alna slipped it between the notebook pages.

“Will they reopen the school?” she asked.

“That isn't up to me.”

“Hardly anything is up to the people who have to live with the decisions,” the teacher said.

Vela left as evening approached. From the bridge came four strikes of metal, spaced unevenly by the wind.

She turned.

It wasn't a siren. Alna was calling the children with the iron plate that had replaced the broken bell.

Vela stood still for a moment. Sounds could resemble one another in memory and have entirely different origins.

But now there was a written record from the morning after the flood, made before the port published its account of the warnings.

It didn't prove which machine had sounded.

It proved that this neighborhood had heard something—and that the city still hadn't asked what people here needed in order to understand it.
