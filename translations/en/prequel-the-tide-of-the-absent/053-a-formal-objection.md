# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 053 · A Formal Objection

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Amiel Rusk. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/053-objecion-de-oficio.md`.

***

The lawyer did not raise his voice when he asked for the copy to be withdrawn.

Amiel Rusk had expected an argument about the contents of the record. Instead, Estuary Cargo's counsel had placed three sheets on the table: the reproduction used in the preliminary report, the tower's copier log, and a certified copy of the original that Vela Noren had submitted months earlier.

“I move to exclude this attachment as an unauthenticated reproduction,” he said. “There is no record of who copied it, which document was used, or whether the annotations were present before reproduction.”

Magistrate Yuna Tor asked for the pages to be brought closer.

Amiel recognized the sheet. It was the table Vela had prepared to compare pulses and time marks. They had included it in a working folder because it made several sequences easier to read. Along the lower edge was a typewritten phrase: “Receipt confirmed by central.”

The certified copy did not contain that phrase.

“Where did you obtain this record?” Yuna asked the lawyer.

“From the copier log and the documents provided for comparison. The machine recorded a reproduction at 5:42 p.m., two days after Ms. Noren's testimony. There is no second witness or certification seal listed. The submitted copy also lacks the identification of its source file.”

Vela, seated beside her counsel, looked at the page. She did not try to deny it.

Amiel felt the room leaning toward a conclusion that no one had yet stated.

“Are you saying the document was altered?” the prosecutor asked.

“I'm saying it cannot be attributed to the original in the form in which it has been presented. I'm not claiming to know who added the phrase or why.”

Yuna ordered that no one remove the sheets.

***

The copier log belonged to the signal tower. It recorded the number of prints, the assigned user, and the time, but it did not retain an image of each page. Three employees had used the machine during that shift. The access code belonged to a shared departmental account.

“Who had that code?” Amiel asked.

Peran raised his hand from the witness row.

“Several operators. I used it to prepare comparison sheets.”

Vela's counsel asked him to explain what he had copied.

“One page from the working table. Not the console original. The table already had annotations we'd added to make the sequence easier to understand.”

“Who added the marginal phrase?” the lawyer asked.

Peran looked at Vela. She stood.

“I wrote it on the working sheet. I wanted to indicate that one mark matched the central system's record.”

“Did that match mean the message had been received on the external channel?”

“No. It meant the central system had recorded an acknowledgment at an intermediate stage.”

“But the text says ‘receipt confirmed by central.’”

Vela lowered her eyes to the page.

“The phrase is imprecise. I should have written ‘intermediate acknowledgment recorded.’ It doesn't mean final receipt.”

The lawyer asked that the answer be entered into the record.

Amiel felt the urge to explain that everyone knew how the network worked. He held back. The copy was in a case file and could be read by people who did not know the difference between an intermediate station and a final receiver. The ambiguity was not a minor one.

***

Yuna requested the original table and the certified copy. Vela produced the former, which retained her handwritten marks. The certified copy had been made before the disputed reproduction and carried two signatures, a date, and a source reference.

The magistrate placed both beside the attachment.

The marginal phrase appeared only on the disputed reproduction. A line identifying the source was also missing from the footer, cut off by the edge of the paper. There was no evidence that the text had been added to deceive anyone; there was sufficient reason, however, not to treat it as a faithful copy of the earlier medium.

“Can you identify who made this print?” Yuna asked.

The log showed only the shared account.

“Not with certainty,” Peran replied.

“Can you demonstrate that the attachment was made from the certified copy?”

“No. It may have been made from the working table.”

Estuary Cargo's lawyer requested that it be excluded from the evidence and that every document reproduced under the same account be reviewed.

The prosecutor opposed a blanket exclusion. The defect concerned one specific sheet, not every document that had passed through the copier. She asked that the attachment be retained as an investigative antecedent, visibly labeled as an unauthenticated reproduction.

Amiel supported that distinction.

“The sheet helped us locate a sequence that must be checked against the original,” he said. “It cannot, by itself, prove final receipt or establish that anyone issued an order to open the gate.”

Yuna made a note of the proposal.

***

The hearing continued with an examination of the original sequence. The laboratory reader had produced a separate technical report, including calibration data, the condition of the medium, and the limits of what could be read. Not every mark could be recovered. The disputed phrase did not appear on the original.

Vela explained that the system recorded four stages: origin, transmission, repetition, and receipt. On certain devices, an intermediate acknowledgment printed in the same column as a final acknowledgment unless the operator selected the correct template. The console software was old and required a manual legend to prevent confusion.

“Was the legend added to this copy?” Yuna asked.

“Yes. And it was worded incorrectly.”

“Was it an attempt to conceal the absence of receipt?”

“No. But I understand that the phrase could lead to that conclusion.”

The company's lawyer insisted that evidence whose meaning changed depending on who copied it should not be used.

“The original did not change,” Vela replied. “The reproduction was defective. I can explain what I read and what I could not confirm.”

The magistrate asked the expert, Amina Dorel, to confirm the condition of the medium. Amina reviewed the laboratory report and the preservation signatures.

“The original has retained its identification and chain of custody since it was transferred to the repository,” she said. “The disputed copy does not replace that medium. Its defect does not alter the measurements already recorded during examination of the original.”

Yuna accepted the explanation.

***

The ruling was brief. The uncertified reproduction would be excluded as evidence of the exact contents of the record. It could remain in the file as working material, clearly labeled and without independent evidentiary value for proving transmission or receipt. The certified copies and the original would remain subject to separate adversarial examination.

She also ordered a limited review of attachments prepared under the shared account, restricted to documents related to the case. She did not order a general investigation of every print made in the tower.

Peran agreed to provide the list of users who had access to the code. Vela asked that her working note be preserved alongside the correction, so that no one could replace the phrase without leaving a trace.

As they left, Amiel caught up with her in the corridor.

“You could have said you didn't remember who wrote it.”

“I wrote it.”

“That wasn't what I asked.”

“I know.”

Vela tucked the folder under her arm.

“The signal didn't change because I wrote a label badly. But if we let the label become evidence, the investigation turns into the very thing we're questioning.”

Amiel nodded.

They had not lost the record. They had lost a copy that should never have been presented as faithful.

That difference was not enough to celebrate. It was enough to correct the procedure before the error spread to other pages.

***

In the archive office, Peran handed over the access list. Four people had used the shared code, including an assistant who no longer worked at the tower. No name established who had made the particular print.

Vela reviewed the correction form. In the “cause” field she wrote: “Legend added to a working copy without being distinguished from a reproduction of the medium. Identification corrected; original neither altered nor replaced.”

She signed it and asked for the excluded copy to be stored in a separate sleeve.

Before closing the file, she found a note from the maintenance office: the laboratory had requested a new reproduction of the repeater reading, this time with dual control and a time stamp.

She did not want to see her name beside another ambiguous copy.

She asked that the new operation be carried out in front of two witnesses, with the source page visible and the machine identified. It was not a guarantee against every error. It was a way to make sure the next one could be detected without arguing over who had written a phrase in the margin.

The corrected sheet left the tower with a clear label.

Vela read it before putting it away.

This time, no word claimed more than the instrument had recorded.
