# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 018 · Iron Mile

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Neria Calden. **Location:** Port Cendra and the Seleris railway corridor, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/018-kilometro-de-hierro.md`.

***

The crate that was supposed to contain six iron retainers weighed less than two.

Neria Calden didn't discover that through intuition. She checked it on the scales at Port Cendra railway station after comparing the weight on the dispatch manifest with the figure recorded on the port's receiving receipt. The difference was too large to explain away as packing timber or moisture.

“Someone wrote one hundred and eighty kilograms here,” she told the cargo supervisor. “The receiving scales recorded fifty-two.”

The man looked at the figures and shrugged.

“Sometimes loads are delivered in parts.”

“Then there should be more than one receipt.”

“Perhaps there is.”

Neria had been hearing that word for three days. Perhaps an order had been drafted too early. Perhaps a crate had been switched. Perhaps a component inside a floodgate had lost material through negligent repair. Each possible explanation required a different check, and none of them automatically restored a safe component to the port.

The documentation from Salmuera Norte indicated that the modified retainer came from a batch purchased from a manufacturer in the Kharven Highlands. That same batch was supposed to have supplied the eastern station and the floodgate at the riverside reservoirs. Yet photographs of the mechanisms showed installed components with different profiles, some marked with numbers that did not match the catalog.

The cargo supervisor took her to the railway warehouse. There were pallets of piping, crates of ceramics, coal sacks, and a row of containers with lead seals.

“The batch was delivered four months ago,” he said. “It's no longer here.”

“Who collected it?”

“The consignee company.”

The register bore a Mareval Consortium stamp and a signature Neria didn't recognize. The departure weight had been copied from the original manifest, not measured at the station.

“Do you have a scale reading for the actual load?” she asked.

“The wagon is weighed at the transfer point.”

“Where?”

“At kilometer forty-three on the iron branch line.”

Neria wrote down the location.

That didn't prove the company had switched components. It did mean they could check whether the wagon had lost cargo on the way, or whether the error had occurred before it reached the port.

***

At noon she traveled to kilometer forty-three with Irsa and a railway inspector authorized to examine transport records. The train passed grain warehouses and fields covered in low mist. At every stop, workers with tools climbed aboard, along with women carrying baskets to market and children in school uniforms.

Irsa opened the repair notebook on her knees.

“If the parts never arrived, what did they install in the floodgates?”

“That's what we have to find out.”

“Maybe they used spares from the old storeroom.”

“Then there should be a stock withdrawal record.”

“There should.”

Neria looked out the window. She didn't want the absence of a record to become an answer in itself. The municipality had disorganized storage rooms, foremen who worked from memory, and accountants who completed forms at the end of the week. She'd seen bad practices that weren't crimes, and crimes concealed beneath impeccable paperwork.

The weighing station stood beside a metal bridge. Its scales used a platform of levers and counterweights, with no arcane components. Each train stopped for a few minutes to verify its total mass, identify the wagon, and record its seal. The mechanism could fail, but it left a trail of entries independent of the port's contracts.

The attendant showed them the ledger from four months earlier.

The initial figure was consistent with the manifest. The departure weight, following a technical stop, was one hundred and thirty kilograms lower.

“Did you unload something?” Neria asked.

“One crate, under diversion authorization,” the man replied. “It went on a service platform toward the inland depot.”

“Where's the authorization?”

“In the transfer file.”

It was a sheet with three signatures and a destination number. The first belonged to the railway supervisor. The second was a carrier's. The third was illegible. The stated reason was “adjustment of damaged cargo.”

“Did you inspect the damage to the crate?” Irsa asked.

“Damage to the packaging was recorded. We don't open commercial goods without a representative of the consignee.”

Neria studied the attached photographs. One corner of the wood was splintered. The main seal was intact, but the nails on the lid were a different color from those on the sides.

“A crate could have been struck and repaired,” she said. “We can't deduce what was inside from those nails alone.”

The attendant nodded.

“What I can certify is that it was removed from the load. The wagon's weight before and after proves it, within the tolerance of the scales.”

Neria requested copies of the entries, including information about the instrument's calibration. The railway inspector signed the request and asked that the originals be preserved until comparison was complete.

Outside, Irsa pointed to the destination number.

“That's not a port machinery depot.”

“Do you know it?”

“It's an intermediate cold-storage freight station. It takes materials that mustn't be exposed to temperature changes.”

Neria looked at the consignment note.

The retainers were ordinary iron components. They had no need of a refrigerated warehouse.

***

Back in Port Cendra, they went to the municipal spare-parts depot to compare the documents with what had actually been installed. The supervisor, a woman named Tessa, granted them access to the inventory on one condition: they were not to remove any materials without a signed request.

The shelves held old crates with incomplete labels. The newer spare parts were arranged by serial number and size. Neria identified a batch of retainers made in Kharven, but their numbers didn't match those in the photographs from the eastern dike.

“Where's the batch from four months ago?” she asked.

Tessa searched her ledger.

“According to this, it was allocated for preventive replacement. It should have been installed.”

“The components we've examined don't bear those marks.”

“Perhaps they were used at another station.”

“Then someone should have corrected the destination on the withdrawal record.”

The supervisor grimaced.

“That can take months.”

Neria asked to open a reserve crate in the presence of witnesses. Inside was a retainer with the correct profile and numbers engraved on its side. She set it beside a photograph of the mechanism at Salmuera Norte.

The difference was clear: the reference component retained its square slot. The installed one had been filed down. But the manufacturing pattern was different as well.

“They aren't the same model,” Irsa said.

“Their external dimensions are compatible,” Neria replied. “Their retention systems aren't.”

That difference could explain why someone had filed down a corner: the wrong part might have rubbed during assembly. It didn't justify the repair, but it offered a concrete technical cause that needed examination.

Tessa checked the work order.

“The contractor reported installing six new retainers and returning the old ones for disposal.”

“Where did the old ones end up?”

“I have no receipt for their return.”

Neria closed the crate. What had begun as a discrepancy in weight was becoming a question about materials that never made it into the machinery.

***

That evening, she met Amiel at the eastern station. The inspector examined the consignment notes and chain of weight records while a municipal technician noted the regulators' temperatures.

“Can you be certain the diverted parts are the ones that should have been installed here?” he asked.

“The manifest links them to the replacement contract. We still need to identify what was actually in the diverted crate. The scales record mass, not an inventory.”

“And what parts were fitted?”

“At least one belongs to a different model from the one contracted. We need to compare its markings with the original depot.”

Amiel nodded.

“I'll request preservation of the transport file and replacement orders. I don't have authority to search a cold-storage facility without specifying what evidence we're looking for.”

Neria showed him the destination number.

“I want to know why a crate of iron ended up in a depot for sensitive materials.”

“That's a precise question.”

The duty technician approached carrying a sheet.

“The riverside floodgate has a load test scheduled for the day after tomorrow,” he announced.

Neria read the timetable. It was a maneuver authorized months earlier as part of preventive maintenance.

If another incorrect component had been fitted there, the test could damage it before the expert arrived.

“Request suspension until the retainer has been verified,” she ordered.

The technician pointed to an authorization signature.

The test wasn't assigned to the municipality. The document entrusted it to the company that had carried the parts from kilometer forty-three.

Neria looked at the company's name.

It was Estuary Cargo.
