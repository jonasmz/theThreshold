# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 024 · A Municipal Order

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Amiel Rusk. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/024-una-orden-municipal.md`.

***

The municipal order bore three seals, two signatures, and a sentence that could not be carried out.

Amiel Rusk read it in the courthouse anteroom while a messenger from the Civic Council waited for him to sign for receipt. The text demanded immediate surrender of all merchandise held in the cold chamber at kilometer forty-three, along with its customer ledgers, energy records, and operating codes.

The reason was understandable: a facility without a complete local license was storing sensitive material associated with an investigation into port damage. What the Council intended to do on those grounds, however, exceeded its authority.

“I can't execute this,” Amiel said.

The messenger gripped his folder.

“The Council approved it by a majority.”

“A majority can order an administrative investigation within its jurisdiction. It can't make me seize every object behind a door without judicial authorization.”

“They say the crates will be taken away if we wait.”

“There's already a preservation order on the crate identified by the railway consignment note.”

Amiel took a copy of the resolution and returned the original. The Council had mixed three distinct matters: commercial property, accumulator safety, and evidence of a possible irregularity in floodgate repairs. The same building could be subject to three sets of rules without giving one office dominion over them all.

Magistrate Yuna Tor received him before the morning hearing.

“What do you propose?” she asked.

“That we require the Council to specify what it wants inspected and on what grounds. Industrial safety has already restricted the regulator. We've preserved the shipment crate. The depot may contain other people's goods unrelated to this case.”

Yuna studied the seals on the order.

“There's a reference to the Regulation Delegation here. Did they consult it?”

“There's no record of a reply.”

The magistrate touched the edge of the paper.

“Then before demanding the shutdown of equipment we don't understand, let's ask someone with technical authority over it to attend.”

Amiel wasn't sure the visit would appease the Council. The Regulation Delegation answered to the Conclave on matters involving strategic instruments and specific licenses. Its intervention might add constraints instead of removing them.

Yuna signed a coordination request. The municipal order was received as a petition for action, not as an enforceable judicial writ.

***

At noon, Delegate Saria Enel arrived at the courthouse. She wore an unadorned dark jacket and carried a thin folder. She was accompanied by an energy-inspection technician, not an escort.

“The chamber uses a regulator that requires supplementary certification,” she explained. “Our authority extends to the stabilization circuit, the origin of certain components, and preventing a dangerous release of energy. It does not automatically extend to wage accounts or the port's civil claims.”

Amiel appreciated the precision.

“The city wants to inspect the entire depot.”

“It may inspect commercial conditions and general safety within its powers. If it intends to open the main chamber, it must respect the hazard protocol and third-party rights.”

“Can you identify who the rings are intended for?” Yuna asked.

“That information falls under the technical register. With appropriate judicial grounds, our office can certify the identity of the authorized operator or indicate which body must do so. I won't disclose information on other contracts simply because one crate is disputed.”

The magistrate slid Neria's documentation about the accumulator toward her. The depot's prescribed procedure demanded more power than the discharge limit stated on the equipment plate.

Saria examined both figures.

“That justifies an urgent inspection,” she said. “Not necessarily confiscation. An improperly performed shutdown could destroy the very evidence we're trying to preserve.”

Amiel thought of the telegram in which an authorized agent had tried to collect the rings after learning of the preservation measure.

“What if the consignee asks to collect them again?”

“The identified merchandise remains subject to the existing court order. Movements of other goods must comply with the applicable licenses. We can impose technical supervision without becoming the owners.”

The distinction would irritate the council members, but it offered a defensible procedure.

***

The coordination meeting took place that afternoon in a Council chamber. Fara Nel, the representative who'd managed to restrict Mareval's concession, sat beside the chair. Loden Vair occupied a seat reserved for contractors and pretended to study a rate catalog.

Fara spoke first.

“Families don't understand why a chamber in our region can fall outside municipal inspection,” she said.

Saria answered without raising her voice.

“It does not fall outside all municipal inspection. The city can examine the building, transport, and ordinary safety conditions. My office must certify the part of the circuit that could affect regulated equipment. Property and the civil investigation remain within their respective jurisdictions.”

The chair struck the table with his palm.

“If the Conclave can stop work, who's responsible for the trade we lose?”

“The measure must be justified, recorded, and subject to review,” Saria replied. “What we can't do is continue an unsafe operation simply because stopping it costs money.”

Loden requested the floor.

“The rings are industrial components. The depot provides a preservation service. If every city decides when to open a crate, trade routes will cease to function.”

Amiel looked toward Fara. She kept her eyes on the reports.

“Nobody has asked to open every crate,” she said. “We've asked to inspect a facility operating under a license with the wrong address and account for components in a contracted shipment that never reached the port in full.”

Loden turned a page of the catalog.

“My company doesn't operate that chamber.”

“Then perhaps answering for it isn't your responsibility,” Fara replied.

Amiel saw several representatives lean over their papers. It wasn't a criminal accusation. It prevented the consortium from speaking as though it represented everyone with an interest in the building.

Yuna explained the reach of the court order. The crate identified by the consignment note would remain preserved. A joint inventory could document its exterior condition and, if technicians considered it safe, its contents under a separate formal record. The city would have access to the safety records within its competence; the Delegation to regulated technical data; the court to documents relevant to its investigation.

“And the rest of the depot?” the chair asked.

“It continues operating under the necessary restrictions,” Yuna replied. “None of these measures amounts to shutting down all its operations.”

The proposal satisfied no one seeking a quick victory. It was approved as a coordination procedure by a narrower majority than the one that had supported the original order.

Amiel requested that the dissenting votes be recorded as well.

***

Outside, Fara handed him a copy of the outstanding requests from the dock assembly.

“The families' requests still haven't been answered,” she said. “Now we have another agreement full of offices and signatures. What good does that do them?”

“If it's carried out, it will preserve evidence without destroying equipment. But it doesn't replace the search for people.”

“I know. That's why I'm asking what's next.”

Amiel remembered the eleven tickets issued to a company account. No record confirmed that their named holders had boarded. The company had also booked a wagon for records from the payment office. Those were lines of inquiry that had to be verified separately.

“I'll request the complete movement records associated with those tickets,” he said. “And ask the railway to preserve its staff rosters.”

Fara nodded without looking satisfied.

When the inspector returned to his office, he found a preliminary report from the Delegation. The chamber's regulator had a backup connection to a rural feeder. It was not yet known whether the line was active or merely an old provision of the installation.

The attached drawing showed the route inland, following a water conduit also marked on municipal maintenance maps.

At its far end was a name familiar to every hydraulic technician in the region: Kelor Dam.

Amiel called Neria.

“We have a new reference that needs technical examination,” he said. “It's not proof of a crime. It's a connection I don't understand.”

The engineer asked him to send the plan through an authorized channel.

“I know that dam,” she replied. “If the line is active, we have a reason to check where it draws its energy.”

Amiel hung up and looked once more at the municipal order. They had spent an entire day deciding who was entitled to open a door.

Now the investigation was leading to a place where opening a door wouldn't be enough.
