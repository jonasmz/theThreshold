# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 048 · The Basement Witness

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Yarik Sem. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/048-el-testigo-del-sotano.md`.

***

Yarik Sem had kept the key in a soap box for nineteen days.

It was still there, wrapped in a rag, when the municipal patrol arrived in the basement of the building where he rented a room. The metal had a notch at one end and grease stains from a machine that was no longer where he'd repaired it.

“I want to give a statement,” he said before anyone could ask him anything. “But I need to know what they can do to me for going in without logging the shift.”

Amiel Rusk didn't touch the key.

“I can't grant you immunity,” he replied. “I can take an initial statement and ask a lawyer to explain your rights before you speak about anything that might implicate you.”

Yarik looked at the stairs. A laundry operated upstairs; below were old boilers, empty crates, and tools borrowed from neighbors. He'd chosen the place because the port's machine room reminded him too much of the night of the flood.

“I don't want them to forgive me for what I did,” he said. “I want them to stop paying me to keep quiet.”

Amiel wrote down the sentence without interpreting it.

Yarik had worked as an assistant technician for an Estuary Cargo contractor. His name appeared on a maintenance payroll, but not among the operators authorized to enter the old shelter's signal booth. He had replaced a component on a verbal order and filed no report afterward.

That might constitute a labor or administrative violation. Its seriousness would depend on which component he'd touched and what he'd known when he did it.

“Let's start with your employment and the date,” Amiel suggested. “Don't tell me yet what was said in a conversation you should have with your lawyer.”

***

The lawyer assigned to Yarik was named Ivena Rell. She arrived with a new notebook and asked to speak with him without officials present. After the private interview, she requested a formal meeting with the competent prosecutor and Magistrate Yuna Tor.

“My client can provide information about an undeclared modification,” she explained. “He's willing to acknowledge his involvement, provided the treatment of any minor violations is clearly defined. We are not asking for exemption from any responsibility connected to serious injuries or deaths.”

Yuna listened from her desk.

“Immunity isn't something you buy with an interesting story,” she said. “The prosecution must assess the facts, and the court must verify that any cooperation is voluntary, limited in scope, and corroborated. Nor can we guarantee that an injured party won't bring a civil claim.”

Yarik felt his stomach tighten. He'd imagined a signature that would separate him from everyone else. Instead, every sentence came with a limit.

The prosecutor presented a written proposal for limited cooperation. If Yarik testified truthfully and provided documents whose provenance could be verified, the prosecution would consider declining to pursue certain violations involving shift records and administrative access, provided no intentional participation in serious crimes emerged. False statements, destruction of evidence, and acts of violence were not covered.

Ivena read it aloud.

“Can my client stop the interview if an issue comes up that isn't covered?” she asked.

“He can consult his counsel,” the prosecutor replied. “The protection agreed upon won't expand beyond its scope without a formal decision.”

Yarik signed after the conditions had been explained to him.

He felt no relief. He felt that, at last, someone had told him the exact price of speaking.

***

His statement began in a room with pale walls, not in the basement of his home. Yuna allowed the lawyer for the affected parties and a company representative to ask questions through the proper channels.

Yarik described the shelter booth as he'd found it weeks before the flood. There was a relay bank with a three-route selector, a small battery, and a plate indicating the direction of the tower.

“They called me because the mechanism kept returning to its resting position, which made it impossible to complete a test,” he said.

“Who called you?” the prosecutor asked.

“A service supervisor named Belnor. I don't know whether he was directly employed by the consortium or by the cargo company.”

“What did he tell you to do?”

“Fit a temporary clamp to hold the selector in a test position. He said the supervisor would remove it when the test was over.”

Amiel remembered the photographs of the booth. The clamp observed by Amina Dorel had a shape consistent with Yarik's account. The object later transferred had suffered a break in its chain of custody, so it couldn't be used without qualification to identify the installer.

“Did you put it there?” the prosecutor asked.

“Yes. I didn't log my shift because they told me it was a load test and the office would complete the record.”

Yarik took a deep breath.

“I don't know what signal passed through that circuit afterward.”

The company's representative asked whether he knew enough to interpret the transmission diagram.

“I know how to fit a bracket and check that a contact won't move. I don't know who decided which messages should go through each line.”

“Did you see an order to sabotage anything?”

“No.”

“Did anyone tell you they would cause a flood?”

“No.”

His lawyer reminded him to answer only what he knew. Yarik was grateful for the warning. He'd spent whole nights imagining that his little clamp had caused everything. Now he had to separate that possible guilt from the facts he could actually support.

***

The most important evidence didn't come from his mouth.

Yarik handed over a copy of a materials voucher he'd kept as proof of the tools he was supposed to return. The voucher listed the clamp, two screws, a fuse, and a work-order number. It contained no victims' names or details of any messages.

The prosecutor asked him to explain where it came from.

“They gave it to me when I left the storeroom. It has one signature for receipt and another for issue. I kept my copy because they deducted the cost from my wages whenever a tool went missing.”

Amiel compared the number with the booth maintenance invoice submitted to the municipal office months earlier. The reference matched, but the authenticity of every signature hadn't yet been verified.

The company provided the original materials-issue ledger under court order. The number appeared there, along with an entry for work performed outside normal hours.

“It's consistent with those parts having been issued,” Amina said, appearing as an expert. “It doesn't prove by itself where they were installed or who authorized leaving them in place.”

Yarik nodded.

“I can show you where I fixed them. I also remember which screw jammed and how I replaced it. But I don't have a photograph from that night.”

Amina asked him to draw the mechanism before showing him the inspection photographs. The drawing identified the clamp's position and the side access point. It was then compared with the original photographs, whose provenance didn't depend on the disputed selector.

Some details matched. There was also a difference: Yarik drew the spring intact, while one coil in the photograph appeared deformed.

“Did you damage it?” the prosecutor asked.

“I don't remember doing that. It may have bent afterward, or I may not have noticed.”

The answer didn't fill the gap. It left it visible.

***

When the session ended, Yuna asked that Yarik's personal information be protected in copies intended for the public while the risk of retaliation was assessed. She didn't declare him an anonymous witness. His identity would be available to the parties and to the relevant proceedings.

Ivena explained that cooperation didn't guarantee him a job or financial security.

“The company can suspend him,” she said. “We can challenge retaliatory measures through the appropriate channels.”

Yarik looked at his hands.

“They've already suspended me. They pay me a sum to remain available, but they don't assign me any shifts.”

Amiel asked to see the agreement, if the witness chose to submit it. It might contain confidentiality clauses that weren't automatically illegal, but whose scope had to be examined against a statement required by a court.

Yarik submitted it the following day through his lawyer. The text provided a standby payment for six weeks and prohibited discussion of “operational incidents” with third parties without administrative authorization.

The start date was two days after the flood.

“It could be a normal suspension while they investigated,” Amiel said.

“It could,” Ivena replied. “We need to know whether it was applied to other technicians, and why.”

A footnote also mentioned an obligation to return tools to the depot before the contract ended.

Yarik took the key out of the soap box and handed it over with a receipt. Its maker's mark matched the type of screw used in the clamp, but it was a common tool.

As he left the courthouse, he found a woman waiting beside the stairs. She was the sister of one of the men injured at the dam. She didn't insult him.

“Did you do it?” she asked.

Yarik took a moment to understand the question.

“I installed a part,” he replied. “I don't know whether that was why someone ended up hurt.”

The woman lowered her eyes.

“Then find out. Don't hide behind a key.”

Yarik watched her walk away carrying a bag of medicine. The limited cooperation protected him from certain administrative consequences. It couldn't spare him from living with what still needed to be established.
