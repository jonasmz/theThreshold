# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 013 · The Cracked Seal

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Amiel Rusk. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/013-el-sello-partido.md`.

***

The municipal seal had been cracked for seventeen years.

Amiel Rusk discovered it in a windowless room while comparing an evacuation order with an archive register he'd requested for an entirely different reason. The bronze stamp had been dropped during a move. Ever since, it had left a small break in the edge of the balance engraved at its center.

The damage was famous among veteran clerks and completely unknown to the citizens who received certified documents.

“Look,” the archivist said. “Here's the same defect on a market permit from nine years ago.”

Amiel brought both sheets closer to the lamp.

The order delivered to Neria Calden bore a perfectly intact municipal seal.

“That might mean a different die was used,” he said.

The archivist nodded.

“There are four. One for the court, one for port administration, one for the general registry, and this one, which is used for historical files. They haven't all worn in the same way.”

“I need a catalog of their differences.”

“One exists. But it's an internal document.”

The woman's name was Emina Sorel. She knew the archive better than the officials who approved its expenses, and she didn't seem inclined to surrender a register without knowing how it would be used.

Amiel showed her the judicial authorization for comparison. It did not permit him to remove the dies, but it allowed him to examine certified samples and take impressions under supervision.

“I'm trying to determine which office produced this document,” he explained. “Not prove it's a forgery just because one mark is different.”

Emina led him to a locked cabinet. She took out a sample book of seals, recording dates of use, changes of official, and repairs. Each page held a reference impression and the signature of the person who had taken it.

The seal on the order matched the one used by port administration.

“Then that's where it was issued,” an officer said.

“Not yet,” Amiel corrected. “It matches their die. That doesn't prove where it was stamped or by whom. Someone could have had authorized or unauthorized access to the instrument.”

Emina regarded the officer with silent approval.

The distinction was not as small as it seemed. If the order bore a genuine seal, the investigation would have to establish who had had custody of the object. Forging a signature was one problem; abusing an authentic seal was another.

Amiel asked to inspect the die-movement log. The archivist consulted the index and took him to another table.

The ledger contained a line for every issue and return. On the night before the flood, the port die had been taken from its usual case to authenticate a batch of construction permits.

Its return was recorded at 1:40.

It was possible to stamp an order at 1:58 if someone had access to the die again. It was equally possible for the order to have been prepared earlier and dated when it was issued. Either scenario required more than a visual comparison.

“Is the batch of permits still kept?” he asked.

Emina pointed to a reference in the index.

“In the port's storage facility. We only have the delivery receipt here.”

Amiel copied the reference and the return time. The receipt was signed by a duty clerk whose name did not appear on the list of people present during the emergency.

An absence wasn't proof of a crime either. But he had another person to interview.

***

Magistrate Yuna Tor received him at noon.

Her office had no solemn emblems, apart from a small wooden balance she used as a paperweight when the wind came through the window. Amiel laid out the discrepancies without stopping to attribute intentions.

“A disputed signature, an order timed before the alarm, and a seal whose origin we can narrow down,” she summarized.

“And the original was taken from the receiving office without a receipt.”

“That's an irregularity in custody. Don't let me hear you call it a forgery until you can distinguish the two.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“I'm reminding you because tomorrow everyone in the city will be saying something like it.”

Amiel handed over the certified sample book and the movement log.

Yuna studied the dates.

“Have you established whether the clocks were synchronized?”

“We have references from the tower and the municipal registry, with margins of tolerance. I can't guarantee the port's clock used the same correction.”

“Then have a technician estimate the possible deviation. An order appearing ten minutes early might be a clock error; an order prepared for something not yet authorized is much more serious, but we have to prove it.”

Amiel pointed out the differences between the versions of the evacuee list. One recorded eleven people; another contained no acknowledgment of their arrival at the shelter. The magistrate asked for independently sourced copies, with an account of where each had been kept.

“I also want to know what happened to the people on that list,” she said. “We mustn't turn a matter of identity into an argument about ink and forget the people themselves.”

Amiel thought of Liron Velk and his immobilized leg. He had obtained a municipal certificate to cover his medical care, but he still didn't know who had redeemed his crew's work tokens.

“I'm investigating the cargo depot.”

“Request an extension of the order on the court's authority. I'll grant it for the token-redemption books covering the week of the accident, not for all of Mareval's accounts.”

Amiel accepted the limit. A broad search without grounds could delay the entire case.

As he was about to leave, Yuna returned one of the sheets.

“Look at the upper corner,” she said.

A watermark was visible against the light: a small wheel crossed by three lines.

“It's municipal printing paper,” Amiel explained.

“We don't know which print run. The archive can find out.”

It wasn't an extraordinary discovery. Official orders were printed in batches and stored for months. But every batch had a distinct mark to track expenditure.

The magistrate laid the sheet on the desk.

“Investigate the paper too. A signature can be imitated; a seal can be lent. The materials have histories of their own.”

***

Emina found the print-run register that afternoon.

Paper with the wheel-and-three-lines watermark had been purchased for the port works office, not the evacuation department. The batch was recent and had arrived at the storage facility three days before the accident.

Amiel examined the receiving ledger. The works office had withdrawn fifty forms without printed text, intended for temporary intervention permits.

“Blank forms?” he asked.

“Watermarked paper, yes. The offices completed them on their own machines. That's how the system worked before the reform.”

“Why is it still used?”

“Because it takes the municipality longer to change its habits than its laws.”

Amiel requested the batch number. The order received by Neria had a guillotine-cut edge identical to that of the sheets in the series. It didn't prove the order came from that particular batch, but it made requesting the inventory reasonable.

Emina also showed him a form from the same office, issued the afternoon before the flood. The watermark was in exactly the same position, although the distribution of fibers differed.

Amiel compared the seals once more.

On the works form, the balance of the port seal had a small gap in the outer circle. On the evacuation order, the notch appeared twice, as though the instrument had been pressed down twice without the paper being fully removed.

“It's the same defect,” Emina said. “That helps identify the die.”

“And it might indicate a double impression, not a copy.”

He wrote it down without turning it into an expert conclusion just yet.

When the comparison was finished, Emina handed him a receipt for the withdrawal of paper. The batch had been collected by a works employee named Denor Vask. The stated purpose read: “Preparation of annexes for maintenance concession.”

Amiel read the words in silence.

Evacuation orders and the annexes to a concession did not necessarily belong in the same case file. The offices might simply have used the same paper for convenience, especially in an administration with tight budgets. It was also possible that somebody had been preparing documents for an emergency and a contract at the same time.

The difference was too important to fill in with guesses.

“I need the concession file,” he said.

Emina checked its index card.

“It's circulating. It was sent to the Civic Council this morning.”

Amiel asked that his request be recorded and that the source sheets be preserved. Then he placed the certified copy of the order in a fresh envelope.

Neria's signature was still in doubt. The seal was probably authentic. The paper came from a batch used for another administrative purpose.

Three facts compatible with more than one story.

On his way out, he found a notice posted on the courthouse door. The Council would vote on the concession in two days.

The inspector folded his copy of the receipt and realized that the questions had acquired a deadline nobody in the investigation had chosen.
