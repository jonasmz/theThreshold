# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 033 · The Double Names

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Amiel Rusk. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/033-los-nombres-dobles.md`.

***

Amiel Rusk found two women with the same name in a room with only one empty chair.

One was a former port worker, born in the salt district. The other had arrived from Costa Interior with a little girl and a bag of clothes. Both were listed as “Lina Arven” on assistance forms, although their dates of birth, signatures, and addresses did not match.

The municipal office had suspended both women's rations.

“They say one of us invented her identity,” the older woman explained.

“We aren't going to decide that because two names happen to match,” Amiel replied.

The younger woman kept her eyes down. She had arrived with a partially illegible transit document and the child's birth record, written in another jurisdiction. The older one carried a work identification card and rent receipts.

Amiel arranged for them to be interviewed separately, with a clerk to preserve their statements and no demand that they surrender original documents without receipts. The judicial investigation was examining possible forgeries associated with transfers; the right to urgent assistance had to be assessed by the appropriate social service.

“Are you going to leave us without food while you decide?” the older woman asked.

“I'll request that the municipality decide on provisional assistance today. I can't authorize spending from this office.”

The younger woman tightened her grip on her daughter's hand.

Amiel had the impression that, to them, the distinction mattered far less than the next bowl of soup.

***

The problem of duplicate names had begun when the eleven railway tickets were compared with the transport contract's forms. Some names appeared twice, with different spellings. Others belonged to people without work credentials but with ration certificates issued by relief organizations.

Amiel had requested a discrepancy table, not a list of suspects.

He arranged three folders on his desk: civil identification, work documents, and transport receipts. A name could appear in any of them without appearing in the other two.

His assistant, Daren, pointed to an entry.

“This one says ‘Iren Val,’ but the cargo company's list says ‘Irena Valen.’ Same family number, different age.”

“Where does the age come from?”

“The distribution ledger. There's no birth certificate attached.”

“Then we don't know whether they represent different people or a copying error.”

Daren moved to the second case. A man had submitted three claims for transport payments using the names of two missing workers. Each form carried an almost identical signature and a commercial account for collection.

“This looks different,” Daren said.

Amiel studied the dates. The requests had been registered at separate service windows; neither alleged beneficiary appeared in any arrival record.

“It's a possible deliberate duplication of a claim,” he admitted. “But before we say the people were invented, let's check whether someone was legitimately representing them.”

The distinction was crucial. A mother might collect for her injured son. A foreman could receive a crew's wages if duly authorized. An intermediary could also exploit those customs to create fictitious beneficiaries.

Daren returned with a response from the civil registry. Two identities in the second case belonged to real people, both alive according to the latest available records, but their addresses were out of date.

“We still don't know where they are now,” Amiel said.

“Or whether they authorized the payments.”

The inspector requested statements and documents establishing representation. He ordered no arrests.

***

The younger woman who called herself Lina Arven said she'd lost her original birth record in a fire at a railway barracks. Someone at the transit station had written down her name as they heard it, without asking her how it was spelled. She'd used that variant for rations ever since.

“My mother called me Liria,” she said quietly. “Nobody here pronounced it properly.”

Amiel recorded both names, distinguishing the identity she reported from the documentary variant. He didn't ask her to choose which was real.

“Why did you take the surname Arven?”

“It was my husband's. He died before our daughter was born. The register in my village was never updated.”

She had no death certificate. There was a letter from a midwife and an incomplete marriage record. All of that required verification, but no missing document automatically made her an impostor.

“Who helped with your forms?” he asked.

“A clerk from a cargo company. He said if I wrote a different name, I wouldn't receive food.”

Amiel asked her to describe the employee and the place, without suggesting names. She remembered a green desk and a broken lamp; she couldn't identify the man.

“Were you paid any money for being transported?” he asked.

“They never gave me any money. They put me on a cart as far as the marsh bridge.”

“When?”

“I can't remember the exact day. It was after a night when sirens sounded at the port.”

The account might relate to a relief operation, but it didn't by itself establish that she was part of the eleven billed transfers. Amiel recorded the uncertainty.

On her way out, the woman's daughter stopped in front of Daren's desk.

“My mother knows how to write her name,” she said. “She just doesn't write it the way you do.”

Daren stared at the paper in front of him.

***

The older Lina Arven had worked in the salting workshops for thirty years. Her identity was thoroughly documented. The dispute arose elsewhere.

Someone had used her employee number to record hours on a cleaning shift she'd never worked. The payroll listed a temporary substitution but didn't preserve the name of the person who'd taken her place.

“I was caring for my sister,” she said. “I can bring neighbors who saw me.”

“You don't need to prove where you were if we can establish who collected the wages in other ways,” Amiel replied. “But your account will help narrow it down.”

She showed him a wage receipt. An insurance contribution had been deducted for a shift she hadn't worked. The money had gone into a shared workshop account.

“Could it be an unrecorded substitution?” Amiel asked.

“It might. The women covered one another's shifts. But we were usually told.”

The inspector remembered the shift notebook Neria had brought to the court. Some informal arrangements had arisen from necessity; others might have exploited those arrangements to charge for work never performed.

Instead of placing all of them under the word “fraud,” he separated three questions: who occupied the position, who authorized the substitution, and who received the money.

The older woman drew a breath.

“I don't want a coworker fired for covering a shift to help me.”

“I can't guarantee what your employer will decide. But we can distinguish genuine assistance from a payment for work that never happened.”

She seemed to accept that no easy guarantees were available.

***

In the afternoon, Amiel went to the assistance service with a provisional recommendation for both women. The office could establish that they were different people through interviews, witnesses, and independent documents without demanding that every discrepancy in their records be resolved before they could eat.

Helva, the official in charge, read his notes.

“If we accept name variants without rules, anyone could request double rations,” she said.

“That's why each case needs individual verification. I'm not asking you to abandon checks. I'm asking you not to turn a coincidence into an automatic rejection.”

Helva gestured toward the line growing behind the door.

“I have forty applications and three employees.”

Amiel looked at her tired hands.

“What would you need to decide them without improvising every time?”

“A form that lets us record aliases, work names, and civil names without making us choose just one. And somewhere to note who verified each fact.”

It was a better administrative answer than any speech the inspector could have made.

Amiel agreed to propose the form and request temporary staff for the comparisons. He didn't promise immediate approval.

Back at the courthouse, he found a list sent by Estuary Cargo. The company claimed to have identified every beneficiary of eleven transfers. Three names were marked “regularized” after the date of the flood.

Daren pointed to a signature.

“The same authorized representative approved all three changes.”

Amiel compared the dates and found another detail: one correction had been recorded after the court ordered preservation of the file but before the joint inventory. Depending on what had happened to the original, the alteration might have been legitimately authorized or might have breached the preservation order.

He requested the revision history and acknowledgment receipts. He didn't want to confuse the need to correct a human error with permission to alter evidence.

As they closed the folders, Daren asked which name they should put on the young woman's new card.

“All the ones she's used,” Amiel replied, “each with its source. And a separate field for what we still don't know.”

Daren raised his eyebrows.

“That won't fit on the old card.”

“Then we'll change the card.”

Through the window, Amiel saw the two women leave the assistance service through different doors, each carrying a provisional receipt. They still shared a name in some documents. They no longer had to pay for that mistake with their children's hunger or the denial of their own existence.

On his desk, however, the signature of the cargo company's authorized representative still demanded an explanation.
