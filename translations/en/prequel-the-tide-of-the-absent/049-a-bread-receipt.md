# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 049 · A Bread Receipt

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Talia. **Location:** the marshes and Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/049-un-recibo-de-pan.md`.

***

Talia found the receipt beneath a stone that kept the oven lid shut.

It was a hand-sized piece of paper, with two grease stains and the round mark of a cup. It had belonged to the old kitchen at Willow Crossing, the one that distributed bread before the shelter organized its own flour sacks.

Alna recognized the handwriting. The teacher had recorded the bread received from a Port Cendra bakery on the morning after the flood.

“We used it to settle the account,” Alna said when Talia showed it to her. “I never thought anyone would come looking for it again.”

A brief signature appeared in the margin: “I. Valen.” Below it was a number of rations delivered to the kitchen, not to any particular person.

The name sounded familiar to Talia. A woman at the shelter had arrived with a work credential identifying her as Irena Valen; at a transit office, she was also listed as Iren Val. She'd spent weeks trying to correct her documents without exposing where she slept.

Talia couldn't conclude that the signature was hers.

“We need to ask her,” she said.

Alna placed the paper inside a clean book.

“And explain first what it might be used for.”

The shelter had learned to distrust lists. Now a piece of evidence nobody considered valuable might prove that a woman had been in the marshes on a date when a company had recorded her as transferred to another station.

The discrepancy mattered, but it could also put her in danger.

***

Irena was mending a net beside the canal. A red ribbon was tied around her wrist, and she counted knots under her breath.

When Talia asked if she remembered the receipt, she laid the net across her knees.

“I signed something in the kitchen,” she said. “Who wants to know?”

“An investigation into the transfers. We haven't handed over your name or the paper.”

Irena looked toward the houses.

“They told me I'd taken a train. Then they tried to deduct the cost of the trip from a settlement I still haven't been paid.”

“Did you travel on that train?”

“No. I walked from the depot to the bridge and came here with a vegetable cart.”

The answer was clear. Its documentary basis wasn't, not yet.

Talia explained that a voluntary statement could be added to a case file under limited-access safeguards. She also told her that an authentic signature might need examination, and that the company would have the right to challenge any claims made against it.

“I don't want to become a witness to everything the port has done,” Irena replied. “I want them to stop charging me for a trip I never took.”

Talia accepted the distinction.

“Do you remember what you signed?”

“Receipt of bread for the kitchen. I helped unload it because Alna had her hand bandaged.”

Alna confirmed that she'd cut herself lifting a tray, though she couldn't be sure of the exact date until she checked her class notebook.

Irena described the baker's bag and the rope that held the packages together. She said they'd received twenty-four large loaves, several of which were sliced and shared among the children.

The receipt listed twenty-four.

The match wasn't a complete identification. But it connected memory, quantity, and a written record.

***

The baker was still working in the north market. His name was Doven Ar, and he had a small bakery with a coal-fired oven, not arcane devices.

Talia went with Alna to speak to him. They didn't initially reveal the woman's name.

“We're trying to confirm a delivery to the Willow Crossing kitchen,” she explained. “Have you kept your ledgers?”

Doven pointed to two boxes on top of a cupboard.

“If they didn't pay me, I certainly kept them.”

He found an invoice for twenty-four loaves, issued on the morning after the flood, bearing the seal of a neighborhood association and a note about cart transport. Payment had arrived two days later through a collection.

“Who received the delivery?” Talia asked.

“A young woman and a teacher. The young woman had a red ribbon on her arm, holding her sleeve in place. I didn't ask for identification.”

Talia felt the urge to give him Irena's name. She held back.

“Can you describe the place and the time?”

Doven remembered leaving after kneading the dough and before opening the shop. The rain had soaked two sacks of coal. At the bridge he'd passed a cart loaded with blankets.

His ledger had an entry for dispatch and a payment receipt. They could compare it with the association's accounts, but couldn't establish the exact minute without another clock.

Alna showed him the kitchen paper.

Doven recognized his own seal. He didn't claim to know Irena's signature; that was for the person who had received the bread to confirm.

“I can attest that I delivered what I invoiced,” he said. “Not what happened to every loaf afterward.”

Talia thanked him for his caution. They'd come to confirm a simple act, not to manufacture a witness to the railway journey.

***

Back at the shelter, Irena asked to speak with an advocate before deciding whether to submit the receipt. Talia arranged the meeting through the assistance service without taking the paper to the court.

The lawyer explained that her statement could be limited to her own presence and signature, and that they could request that her current address not be disclosed in a public copy. They couldn't prevent every cross-examination or promise that a civil claim would be decided in her favor.

Irena listened without interrupting.

“If I testify, can people from Estuary Cargo come looking for me?”

“They may learn your identity through the proceedings. We can request measures against threats or retaliation, but we can't erase the fact that you're taking part.”

“Then I need it recorded that I never agreed to let the company speak for me.”

The advocate wrote down the sentence.

The following day, Irena signed a statement before a clerk. She confirmed that she'd received the bread, identified her signature on the copy, and described how she'd reached the shelter. She didn't claim to have seen the other ten supposed passengers or to know Erven Tal's whereabouts.

The original receipt remained in the neighborhood association's custody, with a certified copy for the court and photographs of its condition.

When the clerk finished, Irena held the paper for a moment.

“I never thought a piece of bread could be worth more than my credential.”

Talia gently corrected her.

“It isn't worth more. It proves something different.”

***

At the preliminary hearing, Amiel compared the times. The reserved train had departed after six fifty, delayed. The bread delivery had taken place that morning, but the baker's ledger didn't establish a precise minute that would make it physically impossible for Irena to board another form of transport later.

The evidence, on its own, didn't rule out every subsequent journey.

It did place the woman at a specific location in the marshes during the period when the company's spreadsheet presented her as continuously receiving assistance in Costa Interior.

“We can't claim she never crossed the border in her life,” Amiel said. “We can establish this delivery and compare it with the service invoiced for that day.”

Irena's lawyer asked that the document be examined without publishing the shelter's address. The court agreed to use a geographic reference sufficient for the comparison and restrict the exact location in the public file.

The company's representative argued that a worker could receive bread at the shelter and still be eligible for transport at another time. The point was possible in the abstract, but it had to be reconciled with the dates on the invoice.

Amiel recorded the objection without treating it as a defeat for the testimony.

Talia had expected a moment when the evidence would bring down an entire lie at once. Instead, she found a table of dates, signatures, and disputed accounts.

It was less dramatic. It was also harder to erase.

***

That night, at the shelter, Alna taught the children to add fractions using slices drawn on a blackboard. The oven smelled of fresh dough.

Irena returned to mending nets. She no longer wore the red ribbon; it had torn, and she'd used it to tie a box.

Talia asked whether she regretted testifying.

“I don't know yet,” Irena replied. “Today they looked at me as if I were a misplaced figure. But at least I had the chance to say where I was.”

A little girl came over asking for bread. Irena cut off a slice and handed it to her without asking her name.

Talia watched.

The receipt didn't say who had caused the flood. It didn't explain what had happened to the other people or why a company had declared services it still couldn't substantiate.

It proved that a woman had been there, receiving food for others, while an official paper tried to tell her story from another office.

And that small, verifiable presence was enough to keep her from being erased without challenge.
