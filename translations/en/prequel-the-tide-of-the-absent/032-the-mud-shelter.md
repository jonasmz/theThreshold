# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 032 · The Mud Shelter

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Neria Calden. **Location:** Marsh shelter, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/032-el-refugio-de-barro.md`.

***

The shelter's first rule was written on a wooden board: no one would be asked for their name before being offered water.

Neria Calden read it as the woman with the notebook opened a wicker gate. Her name was Talia, she volunteered, and she coordinated the settlement's repairs. She'd lived in Port Cendra until the rent on her home exceeded what she could earn carrying fishing nets.

“I don't speak for everyone,” she warned. “Some residents want to talk. Others don't. If you have a court order, show it. If you're here about the canal, we'll start with the canal.”

“I only have permission to inspect the public conduit,” Neria replied. “Not to enter your homes.”

Talia studied Irsa's muddy boots.

“That's something new.”

The shelter was built on little islands of firm ground. Its houses combined timber, waxed canvas, and salvaged bricks. Raised walkways connected the yards so children could reach the improvised school when the water rose. There were communal kitchens and a shed where flour was kept.

Neria saw damp marks on the walls, but also carefully fitted pipes and a sand-filter system built with care. It didn't resemble a camp expecting to disappear in a week. It resembled a neighborhood nobody had granted that name.

Talia led them to a circular tank fed by the service canal.

“Fresh water comes in here,” she explained. “Some is filtered for drinking. The rest is used for washing and irrigation. If you cut it off, the well won't be enough.”

Neria checked the position of a small hand-operated gate. It was made from recovered parts. Its closures might function for years, but the shaft showed enough wear to need replacement.

“Who repaired it?” she asked.

“Two mechanics who lived with us. One went to Costa Interior. The other works at the port when there's work.”

Irsa asked whether they kept maintenance notes.

Talia showed them her notebook.

It contained dates, approximate water quantities, and cleaning records. They weren't based on calibrated instruments; some measurements used the number of buckets filled per shift. That information had been enough to run the shelter.

For an official inspection, the units couldn't be compared reliably.

“We can take measurements together without taking control of your records away,” Neria offered.

Talia watched her cautiously.

“You talk as though this installation belongs to us.”

“The main floodgate is public. You built this tank. We need to distinguish the two before deciding who is responsible.”

***

A young woman arrived with two children and asked Talia for coal for the kitchen. Her name was Emina, though Neria couldn't know whether it was her birth name or the one she used here. She had old scars on her hands and a blue blanket across her shoulders.

“The lady's from the port,” Talia said.

Emina stopped.

“I'm not going back.”

“I haven't come to take anyone away,” Neria replied.

“Everyone says that. Then someone turns up with a list.”

The woman said she'd once cleaned railway wagons at a depot. A fire in the workers' barracks had left her without accommodation, and the employment offices had suspended her credentials while the records were reconstructed. She'd brought her children to the shelter because she could work here for food and a small weekly payment.

Her story was not necessarily connected to the Port Cendra flood. The settlement took in people driven out by different misfortunes, some old and some recent.

“Do you have documents for the children?” Irsa asked, trying to understand whether they needed help with schooling.

Emina stepped backward.

“I'm not giving you their names.”

Neria raised a hand to stop the exchange.

“You don't have to answer. We can request support for the school based on the number of children attending, without publishing a list of names. Identification might be necessary for other applications, but we aren't making any today.”

Emina studied them for a moment.

“The last aid delivery came with forms asking us to sign that we'd left our homes voluntarily,” she said. “Nobody explained what that meant.”

Talia nodded.

Neria remembered the document Dalia had refused to sign at the port. The wording might look administrative to someone whose home wasn't at stake. To the person signing, it could become a waiver impossible to undo.

“Did you keep the forms?” she asked.

“Some of them,” Talia replied. “But they aren't yours to take away.”

“You can ask for independent advice before deciding whether they're useful for a claim.”

The older woman smiled without humor.

“People here need bread before they need advice.”

Neria didn't argue.

***

At lunch, they allowed her to sit beside the communal kitchen. A girl brought her a bowl of thick broth and asked whether she really repaired floodgates.

“I try,” Neria replied.

“Can you fix the one that makes noise at night?”

Neria glanced toward Talia.

“Which floodgate?”

“The one at Willow Crossing,” the girl said. “When the wheel turns, the dogs bark.”

The description might fit ordinary pressure changes. Yet the canal wheel was listed as immobilized in the public records, and no nighttime operations had been reported.

“When did you hear the noise?” Neria asked.

“Last night. And other nights.”

“Did you see it move?”

The girl shook her head.

“I only heard iron.”

Neria didn't want to turn a child's recollection into an improvised inspection. She asked Talia whether adults had noticed changes in the flow at those times.

The coordinator opened her notebook. Several entries marked nighttime increases that coincided with carts crossing the old bridge. Not every entry had been made on the day itself; some had been filled in from memory.

“The latest rise came after they closed the Cendra docks,” she said.

“Was it connected to the emergency?”

“We don't know. What matters to us is that the water doesn't wash away the walkways.”

Neria wrote a request for continuous monitoring to submit to the water authority. The reading equipment could be installed on public land without recording residents' movements.

Talia insisted on accompanying any technical team.

“We don't want to find out about construction work after they've already pulled up the boards.”

“That will be in the proposal,” Neria said.

***

Near the shelter exit, a man waited until Irsa was a few steps away. His shirt had been patched, and he looked older than his posture suggested.

“At the port they called me Valco,” he said.

Neria didn't recognize him.

“Did you work on the floodgates?”

“Unloading grain. Sometimes we took spare parts to the old shelter.”

He watched the path before continuing.

“After the flood, three new families arrived. One came with a young man who had trouble walking. I don't know which boat they were from.”

Neria remembered Liron, who was still at the clinic. It was easy to seek an immediate match when so many people were injured. She had no grounds to assume the young man at the shelter belonged to crew four.

“Can you be more precise about when they arrived?” she asked.

“Before dawn the next day. A cart brought them from the service bridge.”

“Who was driving?”

“A woman. I didn't see her face.”

Valco bit his lip.

“Someone asked whether they had work tokens. When none were found, they were told to wait before being added to the ration register.”

“Who told them that?”

“A supervisor who doesn't live here. He had a list from the warehouse.”

Neria wanted to ask another question, but Valco stepped away.

“Don't use my name at the port,” he asked. “They owe me two weeks' wages. If they know I'm here, they'll use it to say I abandoned my job.”

“I won't put it in a public report without first explaining what protection may be available,” she replied.

Talia approached.

“Did you ask him what you needed?”

“He told me what he chose to tell me.”

The coordinator seemed to approve the distinction.

From the bridge, the shelter tank was visible: water flowed through a pipe the official maps said was closed. Beyond it, a dozen children crossed the walkways carrying containers and books.

Neria had thought she'd find a path toward the missing. Instead she'd found people with reasons not to be found.

“We can't close the conduit without an alternative,” she told Irsa.

“Even if it's illegal?”

“Safety doesn't cease to matter because a home isn't drawn on a plan.”

Talia handed them a copy of the water readings, written in her own hand. It included no names or homes, only quantities and dates she'd authorized them to share.

As Neria put it away, a boy came running from the ration shed.

“Talia!” he shouted. “A letter came from the port. They want the complete list of everyone sleeping here by tomorrow.”

The older woman pressed her notebook to her chest.

“Then they'll have to learn what a shelter is for first,” she said.

Neria understood that the next threat wouldn't necessarily come from the water. It might arrive on a form, with a perfectly legitimate signature and a destination nobody had bothered to explain.
