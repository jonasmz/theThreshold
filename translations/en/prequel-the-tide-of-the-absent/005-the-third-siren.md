# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 005 · The Third Siren

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Vela Noren, with a marked scene break. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/005-la-tercera-sirena.md`.

***

The siren on the storage canal had been removed two years earlier, after a regulator malfunction woke half the city.

Vela Noren remembered that night. People came to their windows believing the breakwater had collapsed; one man ran to the dock with his horse still saddled, and the animal ended up drinking water from a fire bucket. The next day, the municipality ordered the device disconnected and its service number moved to the historical archive.

That was why the early-morning entry seemed more serious to her than an inexplicable sound.

She had no right to enter the old storage shed. She could, however, ask an inspector with the proper legal authority to check whether the circuit was still connected.

Amiel Rusk arrived at the tower shortly after four in the afternoon. His coat was spattered with dried mud, and he carried a blue folder so swollen with papers that its clasp was bent out of shape.

“When are the tapes recycled?” he asked.

“Tomorrow, after the shift change.”

“Good. We have a little time.”

“Not if the director decides to bring the procedure forward.”

Amiel opened his folder and showed her a document bearing a judicial seal.

“Provisional preservation of signals connected to injuries and disappearances. It doesn't authorize a search of every archive, and it doesn't allow me to inspect restricted hazardous equipment.”

Vela read the conditions carefully.

“The timing tape is included.”

“Yes. I want two certified copies, one for you and one for our evidence storage. The original stays here, under seal.”

“Why a copy for me?”

“Because you know how to interpret it. If someone asks me in a week what evidence I had before the medium deteriorated, I'd rather not have to rely on my memory.”

Vela decided that perhaps not all inspectors were enemies of machines. Some understood their limitations better than the people who used them.

Director Koss received the document without enthusiasm. He didn't dispute the order, but insisted that Peran certify every step of the copying process and that no active equipment be disconnected during the inspection.

That last requirement was reasonable. A transmitter warning harbor pilots couldn't be shut down simply because another record raised questions.

“The decommissioned station is municipal property,” Vela said. “We need to examine its power supply.”

“The station is out of service,” Koss replied.

“It appears on the transmission sheet.”

“Then the sheet contains an error.”

“That's why we want to check.”

Koss held her gaze for what seemed to Vela a calculated length of time. Finally, he wrote an authorization limited to the shed and the return line. It excluded neighboring warehouses and any premises outside the communications department's property.

It wasn't much. It was enough to begin.

***

The shed stood at the end of a road of sunken cobblestones, between a net depot and a cannery. The door was painted green, though salt air had turned it gray. Above it hung the empty bracket of a mechanical bell.

Peran carried a continuity meter and a test accumulator, both personal-class devices. Vela had a notebook, an oil lamp, and a copy of the plan signed by the administration.

Amiel walked behind them with a municipal officer.

“Before we go in, what are you expecting to find?” the inspector asked.

“A cable connected where it shouldn't be,” Vela said.

“And if we find it?”

“We'll know that something could transmit from here. Not who did it or what instruction they gave.”

The officer wrote down the answer.

Peran opened the power-supply box. Inside, he found two thick conductors cut and capped with ceramic plugs. The installation appeared disconnected, in accordance with the decommissioning record.

Vela compared the conductors' positions with the plan.

“Those are the original ones,” she confirmed.

“Then our little excursion is over,” Peran said.

She didn't answer. The service sheet showed a brief transmission, not a sustained alarm command. All it would take to produce one was a test contact, a small power source, and a link that reached Central. The main cables could be severed while an auxiliary line remained.

She knelt beside the baseboard. Beneath the paint were marks more recent than those on the rest of the wall.

“Look at this.”

Peran scraped a corner with the tip of a screwdriver. The coating came off in a thin sheet. Behind it appeared a small copper tube that wasn't shown on the plan.

“It could be an old repair,” he said.

“It could.”

Amiel asked the officer to photograph the discovery before they dismantled anything. Vela silently thanked him for the interruption. After the previous night, she wanted any conclusion they drew to survive a hostile reading.

Peran connected the meter to the accessible ends of the tube. The needle moved.

“There's continuity to the upper box.”

“Any charge?” Vela asked.

“Very little. There must be some auxiliary circuit left.”

Vela chose not to test it with the accumulator. If the other end was still connected to unknown equipment, injecting power might activate it or destroy evidence.

“Let's take the passive reading,” she decided. “Then we'll isolate the segment with an authorized technician.”

Peran looked at her in surprise.

“Don't you want to know whether it sounds?”

“I want to find out in a way that doesn't kill anyone.”

Light filtered through a crack in the roof. Vela examined the upper box and found a relay mounted on wood newer than the rest of the shed. It bore a commercial label, not a municipal one. The mechanism still had a tiny soot mark on one of its contacts.

“It's been used recently,” she said.

“Soot doesn't carry a date,” Amiel reminded her.

“I didn't say when.”

Vela identified the model. It was a switch used by private depots to share warning lines with Coastal Central. It didn't require a channeler, just an operator who knew the code and a charged accumulator. The power was far too low to move hydraulic machinery, but enough to register a transmission.

A way to make a sound from an installation that was officially silent.

“Who supplied this?” Amiel asked.

“The nameplate belongs to a company that sells parts to half the port. We need the purchase order.”

The officer photographed the label and sealed the compartment with a numbered strip.

Before leaving, Vela stopped beside the exterior wall. An old trench followed the road toward the net depot. At one point, the cobblestones looked as though they had been laid back in place recently.

“Where does that line go?” she asked.

Peran consulted the plan.

“To the post on the corner. Then it goes back to Central.”

“The tube doesn't lead toward that post.”

The officer followed the trench with her eyes. On the other side of the wall stood an occupied warehouse. Its sign advertised maritime products, although the men unloading it were carrying crates with no commercial markings.

Amiel studied the facade.

“That property isn't covered by my authorization.”

Vela knew that. There were legal limits she couldn't overcome with a good reason and a screwdriver.

“We can request another permit,” she said.

“Yes, if we can justify the connection.”

“The trench justifies it.”

“Suggests it,” Amiel corrected. “We need a measurement from the property boundary.”

***

They returned to the tower at dusk. Peran carried the plan, the meter readings, and the transmission sheet to the copying desk. Vela requested the list of tenants connected to the old auxiliary line.

Koss allowed her to consult the historical index. There was no reason to prevent it: the shed was already under municipal preservation.

Among the contracts were a net makers' cooperative, a salt warehouse, and an alarm service for exceptional evacuations. The last had been canceled when the riverside shelter ceased operating as a refuge.

Vela compared the subscriber number with the identifier of the early-morning signal.

Six digits matched. The final digit, used to distinguish the transmitting station, was different.

“A branch line,” she murmured.

Peran leaned over the table.

“We don't know whether it still belongs to the same subscriber.”

“No. We need the current contract.”

The archive clerk handed them the relevant commercial file. Its cover was intact. Inside was a maintenance invoice paid just seventeen days earlier.

The account wasn't in the name of the closed shelter but of a port services company: Mareval.

Vela read the date on the invoice, then the date of the transmission.

A retired alarm, an auxiliary circuit, and a company still paying to keep it. None of that proved who had caused the flood. But it meant that someone had paid to preserve a voice that, according to every public document, no longer existed.

She closed the file without removing a single page.

“Amiel,” she said. “I think we now have something concrete to justify the next order.”
