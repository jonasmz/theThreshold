# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 019 · The Cold Chamber

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Neria Calden. **Location:** Railway transfer station, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/019-la-camara-del-frio.md`.

***

The cold chamber wasn't shown on the materials depot's floor plan.

Neria Calden found it in the transfer building at kilometer forty-three, behind a wall that the lease described as the boundary of a spare-parts warehouse. Its entrance had an iron door and a thermometer protected by glass.

The needle showed a temperature unnecessarily low for storing ordinary mechanical components.

She was accompanied by a railway inspector, an industrial safety representative, and the depot manager. They had been called in after the court authorized a limited inspection of the diverted spare-parts consignment.

The order didn't permit a search of all the merchandise. It described one crate identified by weight, consignment number, and transport seal.

“I have no objection to showing you that crate,” the manager said. “But the chamber operates under a special regime. You can't open it without a certified technician.”

His name was Elmar Gres, and he wore a key hanging from a chain.

“What do you store in it?” Neria asked.

“Precision regulators for industrial contracts.”

“What class?”

“I'm not authorized to disclose the contents of other clients' consignments.”

Neria accepted the limit and asked for the chamber's operating certificate. The safety of a warehouse with stored energy fell under a different authority from commercial ownership of its crates.

Elmar produced an inspection certificate for conventional refrigeration. It covered insulation, piping, refrigerant pressure, and motor ventilation. It made no mention of accumulators or materials requiring arcane control.

“How do you maintain the temperature?” the industrial representative asked.

“With vapor compressors and a support unit.”

“What kind of support?”

Elmar looked at the door.

“A phase regulator.”

A regulator could stabilize a circuit when power fluctuated, but it needed reserves and dissipated heat. It didn't create cold at will. A chamber that depended on one had to declare its power, charge, and shutdown procedure.

“We need its license,” the inspector said.

Elmar returned with a folder. The license belonged to another warehouse, two streets farther south. The address didn't match.

“We were told the installation was covered by the general concession,” he explained.

“That doesn't authorize the risks at this site,” she replied.

Neria observed the relief valves behind a grille. The chamber might be safe at that moment. Nobody had shown how much margin it retained under load.

Elmar pointed to a sign beside the corridor. It warned that any emergency shutdown had to be ordered from the control room, but didn't say which operator was responsible for carrying it out. The inspector asked where the list of qualified personnel was kept. The manager showed her three names; only one was on duty that morning.

“We're not going to penalize your workers for an administrative omission they don't control,” she said. “But no one should operate a system alone when its procedure is incomplete.”

Neria recorded that limitation too. The risk didn't reside solely in a machine.

***

They were led into a room with an electrical panel and a fixed accumulator mounted on stone. The device had indicators showing capacity and maximum discharge power. Two inspection dates had been crossed out.

According to the recorder's consumption figures, the remaining charge would support several hours of stabilization. It wouldn't be enough to restore full operating conditions if the main compressor stopped. Neria calculated the heat losses from the readings without attributing extraordinary capabilities to the equipment.

“If we open the chamber, warm air will get in,” she said. “How do you bring the temperature back down?”

Elmar showed her an instruction: discharge energy from the accumulator to speed the return to normal operating conditions.

“Its power limit is lower than what that procedure requires,” Neria observed.

“That's how it was installed.”

“Then the procedure isn't safe.”

The inspector made a note. She could order preventive measures affecting the power supply, but abruptly shutting down equipment without knowing its contents could destroy materials or cause a dangerous thermal discharge.

“There are sensitive products inside,” Elmar warned. “If the temperature changes too quickly, they'll deteriorate.”

“We aren't going to shut it down just to demonstrate authority,” Neria replied.

The inspector ordered the conventional refrigeration kept running, the regulator restricted to safe parameters, and any uncertified high-power maneuver prohibited. An independent operator would monitor the system while they arranged the supplementary inspection.

Elmar requested an immediate copy of the measures. He explained that the company could claim the cost of monitoring if it considered the order to exceed the original license. The inspector agreed to record his objection but did not lift the preventive restriction. Neria understood that state authority had a price too, and someone would try to pass it on to others.

Then they examined the crate connected to the transport records.

It wasn't inside the main chamber but in a cold anteroom used to receive goods. Elmar allowed them to photograph it and compare it with the railway consignment note.

The crate had the repaired corner shown in the photograph taken at kilometer forty-three. The original seal was intact; another had been added across the lid.

“Who opened it?” Neria asked.

“There's no record of it being opened here,” Elmar replied. “It arrived like that.”

“When?”

The delivery had taken place on the day of the diversion, several hours after the train departed for the port. The recorded weight matched that of the cargo removed from the wagon, within the scales' margin of error.

That allowed them to trace the crate. It didn't prove what was inside.

The inspector explained that to open it, they first needed to identify the owner and determine whether the contents required special conditions. The court order authorized inspection, but didn't require them to destroy the material in order to carry it out.

Elmar summoned Sena Urt, a specialist in preserving instruments.

“It contains regulating rings,” she said. “Some need thermal stability to preserve their calibration.”

“Are they parts from the retainer contract?” Neria asked.

“No. The retainers are made of iron. This is a different order.”

Sena showed them the consignee's list: six rings and their mountings. It mentioned neither the Kharven batch nor a destination in port maintenance.

The discrepancy could be explained by a legitimate diversion recorded incorrectly or by a change of cargo.

“What are they made of?” the inspector asked.

“Asterite with Veyrite fittings. High-precision stabilizers. They're not fuel.”

Neria knew how both materials were used in certified devices. Their presence in a cold chamber wasn't illegal. Their custody and the energy used to preserve them had to be documented.

“Which installation are they intended for?”

Sena looked at Elmar, who opened the consignment ledger.

“The client is listed under an account code.”

“The safety authority needs its contractual identity,” the inspector said. “We're not asking for it to be made public.”

Elmar promised to obtain it.

***

While they waited, Neria walked through the corridor with Sena. The specialist had installed independent temperature sensors because she distrusted equipment that monitored only itself.

“The losses increased two weeks ago,” Sena explained. “The compressor works harder with every cycle.”

“Did you inspect the insulation?”

“Yes. We found a worn gasket. The consumption stayed high.”

“And the regulator?”

Sena lowered her voice.

“We were asked to use it to compensate without stopping deliveries. I told them that wouldn't fix the cause.”

Neria remembered the eastern floodgate. A system could appear stable while someone spent more and more energy to conceal a defect.

“Put that warning on record,” she said.

“I did. Nobody replied.”

Sena showed her a dated copy with a receipt signature. It didn't prove deliberate negligence, but documented that someone had been informed.

When they returned, Elmar had located the contract. The account belonged to Estuary Cargo, acting as an intermediary for the final recipient.

The company had requested storage of six rings for three months and paid a high rate for each additional day. One clause required that they be available for immediate collection upon receipt of a coded order.

“Who can authorize their release?” Neria asked.

“The account holder or an authorized agent.”

“How do you verify that?”

“By signature and a code sent over the commercial line.”

Neria looked at the panel. A chamber without its own license, a crate shipped under a consignment note for different spare parts, and collection controlled by a cargo company. None of those elements alone explained the flood.

Together, they justified a technical audit.

The inspector ordered the crate preserved under a joint seal, without changing its temperature, until the contents could be verified and its legal status resolved.

Sena seemed relieved. She admitted she'd spent days fearing an order to switch the regulator off abruptly. Now, at least, she could explain to her superiors why she'd kept the installation operating cautiously.

As Neria prepared to catch the train back, a messenger arrived with a commercial telegram. The code was valid and the signature belonged to an authorized agent.

It requested that the crate be collected before the end of the working day.

It had been sent after the depot received notice of the inspection.

Neria looked at Sena, who was holding the paper with both hands.

She didn't know why they wanted to move the rings. She knew someone was prepared to try before the authorities could examine them.
