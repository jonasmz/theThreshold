# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 011 · A Waterlogged Copy

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Vela Noren. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/011-una-copia-mojada.md`.

***

The water had entered the signal tower from above.

Vela Noren realized it when a drop fell onto the sheet she was reading and turned the number six into a blue blot. She looked up at the ceiling. The first rain since the flood was coming through a crack beside the chimney, running down a beam, and landing directly on the cabinet of auxiliary records.

“We need to move the boxes,” she said.

Peran appeared with a bucket.

“The director ordered that cabinet emptied. He says we've already given the investigators everything related to the accident.”

“We gave them copies from the central console. These are the dock repeater records.”

“They're wet, too.”

Vela removed the first bundle. The paper curled at the edges, but the thread binding the pages was still dry. Beneath it lay a spool of tape treated with resonant salts, a medium capable of recording brief pulses when connected to the dike repeater. It produced neither words nor sounds on its own. Reading it required a compatible head, a controlled energy source, and someone trained to distinguish a signal from noise.

The reading head was stored in another building.

“There's no reason to destroy it,” Vela said.

“There's a sanitation order. Soaked paper can contaminate the archive.”

“Moisture doesn't turn a spool into a disease.”

Peran set the bucket beneath the leak.

“I'm not the one who signs the orders.”

A maintenance worker appeared in the doorway with a handcart. He had two sacks labeled for disposal and a routine destruction form. Vela read the date: that very day, at the end of the watch.

“Who authorized disposing of the contents?” she asked.

“Communications management.”

“Does the form list every item?”

The worker pointed to a box labeled “Damaged paperwork and storage media.”

“That's not enough,” Vela said. “That cabinet contains records related to a court preservation order.”

“The order refers to the central tower,” he replied.

Vela didn't contradict him. She had read the text and remembered that its description of the material focused on the timing tapes from the main console. She couldn't claim it automatically covered every box in the building.

She could, however, suspend the destruction of material if the responsible technician had grounds to report potential evidence that had not yet been inventoried. That was what the internal incident manual required: no one was to discard a faulty reading before recording the reason for the fault.

“Leave the cart in the corridor,” she said. “I'm going to report a documented exception.”

The worker looked at her like a man hearing the beginning of a far too lengthy argument.

“What if the water keeps coming in?”

“Put another bucket under it. We don't need the evidence to get any wetter to prove it's wet.”

***

Director Koss received her with a blanket over his shoulders. A fault in the heating had chilled his office, and from the desk she could see the chimney of an unlit stove.

“We can't keep everything that gets damaged indefinitely,” he said before Vela had finished explaining.

“I'm not asking for that. I'm asking to remove three storage items from the disposal batch until we can establish their contents and provenance.”

“What do they contain?”

“I don't know yet.”

Koss leaned toward her.

“Then you're asking me to stop work over something you can't even identify.”

“I know where it came from. The dike repeater recorded the codes the console sent to the outer signal lamps. It might confirm whether a warning reached the canal exit.”

The director fell silent.

“The console already recorded the warning,” he said.

“It recorded a transmission. Not its receipt at the far end.”

“That matters to a captain claiming damages, I suppose.”

“It matters to anyone who was sailing.”

Koss looked out at the rain. Then he signed a twenty-four-hour suspension of disposal, conditional on inspection by an outside technician and on no damp device being connected to the active network.

Vela accepted the conditions. Rainwater had penetrated even the spool's metal case. Attempting to read it immediately could cause a short circuit or destroy what remained of the recorded pattern. The tower's portable accumulator had limited capacity and was not intended to power wet equipment.

She requested two witnesses, dry gloves, absorbent cloths, and a ventilated box. Peran recorded every movement. The maintenance technician noted that the spool had been found beneath a leak, not submerged in the canal. The distinction might be essential when interpreting any later interference.

As she separated the sheets, she found a cover bearing the repeater station's code and the date of the emergency. The paper tore at one corner. Vela placed it between two protective sheets without trying to force it flat.

One line of ink was still legible:

“Transmission to outer canal: unconfirmed.”

Vela moved the lamp closer.

The note might mean the repeater had received no acknowledgment from a vessel. It might also mean the signal had never left the repeater. The protocol distinguished those situations by a final mark—the very part the water had washed away.

“You can't use it like that,” Peran said.

“Not as complete evidence.”

“Then what?”

“As a reason to look for the missing record.”

A gust of wind threw the window open. Water splashed across the desk. Vela shielded it with her body while Peran closed the protective sheets.

For an instant, she felt a childish urge to stuff the spool into her bag and take it home. It would be drier, farther from the disposal cart, and, according to her fear, safer.

It would also lose the verifiable provenance that made it something more than a story about a piece of paper someone had found.

“Call Amiel,” she said. “I need him here to witness the packing.”

***

Amiel arrived at dusk. He had no authorization to remove the medium, but he did have a supplementary preservation order issued after the call from the tower. Vela had insisted that it describe the item as “auxiliary record from the dike repeater,” not merely “damaged tape.”

The inspector examined the condition of the seals before approaching the table.

“Has anyone read it?” he asked.

“No. Energizing it while it's damp would be dangerous.”

“And this note?”

“It's on a paper cover. Any interpretation is provisional.”

Amiel seemed satisfied. He took the statements describing the discovery and signed the report alongside Peran and the maintenance worker.

They still had to decide what to do with the original.

The municipal laboratory had a drying chamber with circulating air, but no resonant reader. The tower had the reader, but nowhere protected from moisture. They chose to transfer the spool to the laboratory under the custody of two officials, documenting the route and the condition of the packaging.

Vela asked to be present when they attempted to read it.

“We'll need an operator,” Amiel admitted. “That doesn't guarantee you access to the entire investigation.”

“I'm not asking for it.”

When the box had been taken away, Peran found a second sheet stuck to the bottom of the cabinet. It was too wet to lift without tearing. Vela called the clerk over before touching it, and they slipped a rigid sheet beneath the paper.

Once it was protected, she could make out the heading.

It wasn't a transmission record. It was a request to test the repeater, issued by the port administration the afternoon before the emergency.

The box marked “Reason for test” was intact.

It read: “Simulation of scheduled opening warning.”

Vela checked the date twice. That didn't prove the simulation had caused the accident; tests were carried out frequently. But it gave them a concrete reason to demand the test schedule and find out why a decommissioned line had been used again.

Koss approached when he saw the clerk preparing another envelope.

“Is there still something missing?” he asked.

Vela raised the protected sheet.

“What's always missing at the end of an order: who decided to carry it out.”

The director didn't answer.

As the disposal cart went past, Vela saw dark water dripping from the sacks. Among the ruined papers were ordinary invoices, expired sheets, and unnecessary copies. She had preserved a single medium because it might explain a signal.

She didn't yet know whether it would help anyone.

She knew that, at least, it wouldn't disappear without a record that it had disappeared.
