# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 056 · A Small Ruling

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Yuna Tor. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/056-una-sentencia-pequena.md`.

***

Yuna Tor read the final page three times before signing it.

The courtroom was almost empty. The hearing had ended an hour earlier, but representatives of the port authority, Mareval, and the families had left incompatible requests on the table. Some demanded that every affected facility be shut down until the investigation was resolved. Others wanted every station reopened without restrictions so they would not lose another working day. Between those positions stood a port that still needed water, power, and freight routes.

The magistrate could not issue a decision that would satisfy everyone. She had to determine what needed to be preserved and what could continue operating.

The first folder concerned the eastern station. The second, North Brine. The third, the inner buoy and its auxiliary repeater. All three sites shared one feature: they contained components whose configuration could be changed during ordinary service, making it harder to reconstruct events if they were altered.

These were not three scenes from a single crime. They were three separate facilities with outstanding records.

“If we immobilize the entire port, the measure will become impossible to enforce,” Amiel Rusk said from the doorway.

Yuna looked up.

“And if we immobilize nothing, tomorrow we may discover that someone replaced components we haven't examined yet.”

“That is why I proposed preserving components, not buildings.”

The magistrate pointed to the list.

“That is what I intend to order.”

The ruling identified the three sites and specified what had to be retained at each. At the eastern station: the main indicator, its external adjustment, the key box, and any components removed during inspections. At North Brine: the installed retaining assembly and the passage mechanism that had shown wear. At the inner buoy: the auxiliary cable, the relay, and the activation log.

No work was prohibited at any of the facilities.

The port authority could operate gates, repair failures, and replace dangerous components, provided it notified the court-appointed expert before any intervention affecting a preserved part. If an emergency required immediate action, it had to photograph the initial condition when safe, identify everyone present, and retain the removed component. The safety of workers and the public took precedence over keeping evidence untouched.

The order had an initial term of thirty days, renewable if specific reasons emerged. It did not authorize searches of private offices, freezes on bank accounts, or a general shutdown of commercial routes.

Yuna included a clause that had drawn objections during the hearing: Mareval could continue supplying equipment and personnel under municipal supervision, but it could not certify the integrity of components it had installed.

“They'll say this measure condemns them before they've been tried,” the clerk remarked.

“Then they'll have to read the whole thing,” Yuna replied. “It doesn't prohibit them from working. It prevents them from acting as their own expert on the very components under question.”

***

By midmorning, she summoned the representatives to communicate the decision. Neria Calden arrived with Irsa and a diagram of the eastern station's circuits. Vela Noren brought the buoy inventory. Orin Farel represented the tugboat cooperative. Breda Lin had been invited as a technical observer for the reconciliations, without regaining the external authority the Mutual had suspended.

Mareval's lawyer read the first section and asked for clarification.

“Can the company replace a component that fails during service?”

“Yes,” Yuna said. “With notice and documentation, unless there is an immediate danger. The removed component remains in custody, and the reason for the intervention must be recorded.”

“What if notice delays an urgent repair?”

“The exception is written into the order. Safety will not wait for the court to answer a letter.”

The workers' representative asked whether the eastern station would remain open.

“I have not ordered it closed.”

“And who guarantees the problem won't happen again?”

Neria answered before the magistrate could.

“No one can guarantee that with a signature. We have independent monitoring, redundant readings, and operating limits. If one measurement contradicts the others, we will not move a gate based on a single needle.”

The man did not seem satisfied.

“That is what you were supposed to be doing before.”

Neria took the criticism without arguing.

“Yes. That is why we are changing the procedure.”

***

The buoy clause prompted a different objection. The navigation authority feared that preserving the auxiliary cable would require an old circuit to remain energized.

Vela explained that it did not. They could isolate the power supply after measuring voltage and documenting the relay's condition. The activation log would be preserved separately. If safety required the line to be disconnected, an authorized technician would do so in the presence of a witness from the river authority.

“Can the buoy still serve as a navigation marker?” Orin asked.

“The casing will remain visible,” Vela said. “The test lamp will not be used as a navigation signal. Until the wiring is clarified, the authority will place an independent marker.”

Yuna added the condition to the supplementary record. She did not want preservation of a component to confuse the captains navigating the channel.

Breda reviewed the component list and pointed out an omission.

“The North Brine maintenance log isn't listed.”

Mareval's lawyer objected that it was a company business record.

“The order does not require you to surrender all your books,” Yuna clarified. “Only to preserve the pages identifying work on the retaining assembly and allow a contradictory copy of the relevant entries.”

The magistrate added that limitation. The case file was not to become a general authorization to inspect every contractor's business activities.

***

The decision began to take effect before noon.

At the eastern station, Amina Dorel placed numbered seals on the adjustment box and checked the tool inventory. At North Brine, Neria and Irsa coordinated a visual inspection with the ceramic works' technician to confirm that operations could continue without putting extra load on the disputed retaining mechanism. At the inner passage, Vela accompanied inspector Lerna Vis during the controlled disconnection of the buoy's pilot light.

No facility was shut down. Scheduled maneuvers were reduced, and duty crews received new instructions. The port lost a few hours of productivity but kept its essential passages open.

Orin watched the activity from the tug. A salt barge was waiting for permission to enter, and two fishing boats had been forced to change moorings. The ruling had not stopped the work; it had added steps, witnesses, and forms to every intervention.

“People are going to hate this,” Solen said.

“People already hate waiting,” Orin replied. “But waiting an hour is better than finding out later that someone opened a box and no one knows what they took.”

***

As evening fell, Yuna received the first compliance report. All three stations had been notified. At one of them, a worker had refused to sign the inventory because he believed the seal restricted his right to make repairs. The magistrate ordered that his objection be included without penalty.

She then opened the case file and wrote a note for the next review: preservation must not be confused with a finding of responsibility.

The clerk asked whether she thought the case was moving forward.

“It is moving forward in the things we can do today.”

“And what happened that night?”

Yuna closed the folder.

“We still don't know enough.”

***

In the corridor, a family was waiting for news about the search for Erven Tal. The magistrate had no answer for them. She could order transport records preserved and request search reports, but she could not turn a custody measure into a person found.

Before leaving, she read the ruling again. Three stations preserved. The rest of the port still operating. No one declared guilty. No disappearance resolved.

It was a small decision against the scale of what had been lost.

But for the first time in weeks, a court order stated precisely what had to be protected and what could not be brought to a halt.
