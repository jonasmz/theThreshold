# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 052 · Vela's Voice

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Vela Noren. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/052-la-voz-de-vela.md`.

***

Vela Noren carried three sheets of paper in a folder, and none of them contained a conclusion.

The summons required her to appear before the magistrate and explain the coastal signal records from the emergency. She had prepared a description of the equipment, a schedule of times, and a list of technical limitations. She had not written a speech. Words could be arranged in a courtroom; recorded pulses could not.

Peran was waiting beside the courthouse door.

“Are you bringing the tape?”

“The original is in the laboratory, under custody. I'm bringing the certified copies and the working sheet.”

“The one you made to compare the sequences?”

“Yes. It's neither an original nor a certified copy. It helps follow the reading; it can't prove what happened by itself.”

Peran nodded. He had taken part in the initial preservation and knew how much depended on the distinction between a physical medium and someone's interpretation of it.

Inside the courtroom, Mareval's lawyer was reviewing a folder. The company's representative had requested that the records be excluded because the auxiliary line had remained connected to equipment listed as withdrawn from service. Vela understood the objection. An old system could transmit pulses without its operator knowing which device would receive them. It could also generate interference that looked like messages.

Magistrate Yuna Tor asked her to sit.

“Ms. Noren, first explain what your role was.”

“I operated the coastal resonant signals. I logged transmissions, checked continuity between repeaters, and verified whether alerts reached their intended receiving points.”

“Could you order a gate to open?”

“No. The signal station does not operate hydraulic controls. It can transmit an alert or record an acknowledgment. Opening a gate depends on its operators and its physical mechanisms.”

Yuna made a note.

“Go on.”

***

Vela explained that the central console kept a timed tape with marks for transmission and reception. Each station had an identifier, but a signal could be repeated at an intermediate node. If the record showed only a transmission, that did not prove the message had reached its destination.

“What does the line translated as ‘Wait for scheduled opening’ mean?” the families' lawyer asked.

Vela opened the certified copy.

“It's the reading of a sequence fragment. The interpretation system renders it as that abbreviated phrase. It isn't a recorded human voice or a person's statement.”

“Was the order transmitted?”

“The record shows that the sequence entered the repeater system. To determine whether it went out over the external channel, we need the acknowledgment from the receiving end. That information is incomplete.”

“Can you identify the sender?”

“Not from this fragment.”

The lawyer seemed disappointed.

“Don't you recognize the code?”

“I recognize the signal family. The frequency had been removed from the public alert table, but it could have remained in an auxiliary configuration. That doesn't identify who used it.”

Mareval's representative stood.

“Could the sequence have been generated by an automatic test?”

“That's possible. It could also have been a manual activation or a repeat of a pulse received from another device. We need the line configuration, the repeater's status, and the record at the output point.”

“Then you don't know whether there was an order.”

“I know an entry was recorded. I don't know who originated it or whether it reached an operational receiver.”

Vela felt the weight of each short answer. She had spent months trying to find a voice behind a code. In the courtroom she understood that saying *I don't know* was also a way to protect the data from people who wanted to turn it into an accusation.

***

Yuna asked her to describe the damp medium recovered from the tower.

Vela explained how the reel had been found, who had witnessed its packaging, and why they had not connected it to a reader immediately. Moisture could alter the medium, and a reader supplied with power incorrectly could destroy the remaining traces of a signal.

“Could it be read afterward?” the magistrate asked.

“The laboratory carried out controlled drying and a surface inspection. Some marks were recoverable. The line ‘Output to external channel: unconfirmed’ remains incomplete.”

“What does that establish?”

“That the record contains no verifiable acknowledgment. It doesn't prove the signal wasn't transmitted. It doesn't prove it was received either.”

The company's lawyer asked that the record reflect that the phrase might refer to an ordinary communications failure.

“It might,” Vela replied. “That's why I haven't presented it as proof of a deliberate interruption.”

Yuna asked for the conservation report to be added. The certified copy included a packaging record and two signatures. The working sheet, by contrast, was a table Vela had prepared to compare sequences from different days.

“Who prepared it?” Mareval's lawyer asked.

“I organized the data. The tower staff made the reproduction of one of the source pages.”

“With a witness present?”

Vela looked at the sheet.

“I don't remember a second witness being there at the time. I didn't request it as an evidentiary copy. It was working material.”

The lawyer asked that the sheet be identified in the record. Vela did not object. She preferred the courtroom to know exactly which document it was looking at.

***

The questioning continued with the inner buoy. Vela explained that its pilot light could be activated by a low-power pulse and that its flashing pattern was not equivalent to a complete message. The buoy's accumulator did not have enough capacity to move a gate; it could power only a relay or a small lamp.

“Did you observe a sequence that coincided with the time of the flood?” the prosecutor asked.

“There is a record of activations above the usual level. The convoy clocks weren't all synchronized. The timing overlap is partial.”

“Can you be certain the buoy received the same signal as the console?”

“No. The cable was connected to a repeater and could carry pulses from other circuits. We need to compare the transmitting end with the local response.”

Mareval's lawyer showed her a photograph of the metal casing.

“Was this cover open?”

“Not during my first inspection.”

“And afterward?”

“A fastener was found replaced with wire. The navigation inspector preserved its condition before any dismantling.”

“Can you say who tampered with it?”

“No.”

The lawyer put away the photograph.

“Can you say the system was used to coordinate the opening of the eastern gate?”

“No.”

Vela held the man's gaze.

“I can say that a line that should have been withdrawn still had continuity. I can say that pulses were recorded on an auxiliary frequency and that final receipt has not been confirmed. I cannot turn those three observations into an order to open a gate.”

The courtroom fell silent.

***

At the end of the examination, Yuna asked whether anyone had requested changes to her report.

Vela remembered her conversation with Koss, the pressure to discard deteriorated records, and Mareval's request to receive a copy before the judicial inspection. She did not want to exaggerate any of those interactions.

“The director asked that preservation be limited to the components listed in the order. That was a question of scope. He also asked me not to disconnect equipment that was active, which was reasonable. I was not directly ordered to change a reading.”

“And the working sheet?”

“I prepared it to organize the comparison. It should not have circulated as though it were an original record.”

Yuna asked whether she was willing to correct the table so that each column identified the exact source of its information.

“Yes.”

The magistrate accepted the testimony and ordered the certified copies to be kept separate from the analytical documents. The working sheet would be identified as auxiliary until its reproduction could be verified.

When Vela stood, she saw Peran waiting in the corridor.

“How did it go?”

“They asked what I could prove and what I couldn't.”

“And?”

“That's what I told them.”

Vela returned to the tower with the folder under her arm. At the copying desk she found an archive note: someone had requested a new reproduction of the working sheet before the hearing, but the log did not say who had authorized it.

She filed it with the other records.

She had heard no voice in the recording. She had identified no sender. But her own voice, at last, had been entered into the record with the precise limits of what she could support.
