# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 037 · Provisional Certificate

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Dr. Sira Belven. **Location:** Costa Interior dispensary, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/037-certificado-provisional.md`.

***

Dr. Sira Belven had signed a treatment certificate in the name of a man who couldn't say what his name was.

It hadn't been a forgery. The patient had arrived at the Costa Interior dispensary with a fever, an infected wound, and a transit card on which only the beginning of a surname could be read. Sira had registered him under a provisional identity assigned by the hospital so he could receive medication, food, and follow-up care.

Three days later, a port inspector asked her why the name didn't match the one on the transfer list.

“Because the list wasn't in the bed,” she replied.

The official didn't smile.

Now, in the small consulting room beside the landing, Sira had a woman in front of her with documents protected by a waterproof sleeve. Two families accompanied her, hoping to cross the canal to give statements about the Port Cendra emergency. The border patrol had stopped their journey after noticing that the identities recorded at the dispensary didn't match the credentials they carried.

The woman introduced herself as Ada Teren. Her daughter had been treated for dehydration during the exodus.

“They say that because the girl received help under another surname, I might not be her mother,” she explained.

Sira examined the certificate without taking it out of its sleeve.

The document bore a clinical number, a date, a signature, and a note: “Identity declared on admission, pending comparison.” No sentence claimed that the hospital had verified civil parentage.

“This confirms that she received care,” Sira said. “It isn't meant to replace a birth certificate.”

“The patrol won't accept it.”

“Then I'll speak with the health officer at the post. I'm not handing over her medical history without knowing which information is necessary.”

The woman nodded, exhausted.

Sira went out into the courtyard and asked the nurse to continue seeing those who were waiting. There were injuries that couldn't be put on hold while an office argued over surnames.

***

The border official was Captain Rendal. Her uniform bore the insignia of a neighboring republic, across the passage canal. The border didn't divide continents or worlds: it separated the jurisdictions of trading cities that shared commerce, water, and health obligations.

“We're checking the movements of witnesses,” the captain explained. “I can't let a minor cross with an identity different from the one on the pass.”

Sira pointed to the provisional certificate.

“At the dispensary, the girl was registered under the surname of the person who brought her in. We didn't claim that was her legal parentage.”

“That's precisely what I need to clarify.”

“We can do that without disclosing her diagnosis. The mother has a family record, and there are witnesses to their arrival. The civil registry must verify the relationship; my part is limited to confirming that the treatment number is genuine.”

Rendal examined the document.

“Why do you use incomplete identities?”

“Because some people arrive unconscious, without papers, or with their documents destroyed. If we wait for civil certification before treating them, the medical emergency will be over for the worst possible reason.”

The captain didn't seem offended. She made a note distinguishing the clinical number from the civil identity.

“I need a procedure I can apply to every family, not an exception invented for this girl.”

Sira accepted the demand. A child's safety shouldn't depend on a guard's mood, any more than the right to treatment should depend on a missing stamp.

She proposed a coordinated check: the hospital would confirm the number and date of treatment; a civil officer would compare the family documents; the patrol would retain only the decision necessary to authorize or refuse the crossing.

“And if the relationship can't be verified today?” Rendal asked.

“Then you arrange provisional protection and accompaniment. You don't declare that the girl has no mother.”

The captain asked for time to consult her regulations.

While she waited, Sira remembered the first day of the flood, when six people had been brought to her covered in mud. One was unconscious, another didn't speak the language of the forms, and a third had lost his credentials along with his boots.

She'd chosen to record injuries and needs first. Since then, her certificates seemed to have become evidence of an alleged transfer operation she'd never authorized.

***

In the dispensary archive, nurse Rema was reviewing the inventory of medicines sent from Port Cendra. She'd come to Costa Interior to coordinate care for the injured, not to hand complete files over to investigators.

“Amiel Rusk requested a discharge table,” she explained. “No diagnoses, just the identities we can confirm and the source of each piece of information.”

Sira examined the judicial request. The order described a limited group of people connected to the emergency and required clinical confidentiality to be distinguished from admission and discharge dates relevant to establishing transfers.

“We can respond about hospital records,” she said. “We can't certify that a patient took a train just because a company paid for a ticket.”

Rema pointed to a case.

A man was listed as “N. Varel” on the night admission record. After waking, he'd given a different name. The initial card had been completed by a companion; the later change bore the patient's signature and those of two witnesses.

“Why don't we correct the first sheet?” Rema asked.

“Because treatment really did take place under that identifier. If we erase it, the trail of the medication he received disappears too.”

Sira showed her how the files linked both entries through a single treatment number. Name variants were preserved as information declared at different times.

The method wasn't elegant. It took up space and required notes that could look contradictory.

It did, however, prevent one body from being split into two patients because a surname had changed.

Another file contained three provisional identities. Sira remembered that the injured man had been brought in on a cart without any stable companions. The first designation had been descriptive; the second, a name pronounced with difficulty; the third came from a partly recovered document.

“Could he be one of the people missing from the dike?” Rema asked.

“Not without more information. We need a positive identification, and we need to establish that his current status can legally be shared.”

Rema nodded. Neither wanted to manufacture a discovery to give a family relief.

***

In the afternoon, a lawyer for Estuary Cargo arrived. He brought a request for the hospital to certify that every person included in the company's eleven transfer orders had been received and treated.

Sira read the form.

“I can't sign this.”

“The company paid for transport, accommodation, and care,” the lawyer said. “It needs to justify the services.”

“The company's accounts aren't the hospital's records.”

“Are you denying that evacuees were treated?”

“We treated displaced people. The identity and number of each case must be checked individually. Some may have arrived under their own power; others came from places other than the dike.”

The lawyer pointed to a column with eleven boxes.

“We're only asking for a general confirmation.”

Sira laid the sheet on the table.

“A general confirmation would make my signature say something I don't know.”

She offered a procedure: the company could provide transfer identifiers to the court; the court would decide which comparisons were relevant; the hospital would certify only admissions corroborated by its own records.

The man put his papers away without thanking her.

“This will delay payment.”

“Perhaps. The accuracy of a treatment record can't be adjusted to the timetable of your invoices.”

When he left, Sira stared at her desk. Her decision might leave the dispensary without reimbursement; it depended on the same companies for bandages and coal. This wasn't an abstract problem: the storeroom held few bottles of sterile solution and too many people still needed care.

Rema appeared with a basket of supplies.

“We can request advances for services actually provided,” she said. “Without claiming what we don't know.”

Sira smiled faintly.

“That's what we'll do.”

***

At dusk, Captain Rendal returned with a provisional ruling. It allowed Ada and her daughter to cross after their relationship had been verified through civil documents and witnesses. The patrol would retain no diagnoses or details of treatment.

The other travelers would be assessed separately.

Ada held the paper as if it were a dry blanket.

“So I can give a statement?” she asked.

“You can request to do so,” Sira replied. “Permission to cross doesn't oblige anyone to explain publicly why they came.”

Before leaving, the woman pointed to a bench in the courtyard.

“On the first night, they brought a man here wearing a port uniform. He couldn't say his name. I heard them talking about changing his card.”

Sira felt the impulse to ask whether it had been Erven Tal. She stopped herself. Ada's observation was too vague to identify him.

“Did you see who brought him?” she asked instead.

“A woman in a red coat. I don't know anything else.”

Sira recorded the statement as a witness account, separate from the clinical history. She couldn't claim the man was one of her patients without comparing the date and his features.

When she turned off the consulting room lamp, she returned to the initial certificate for the nameless patient. Beneath the identity field was a small line: “Patient treated and verified; civil identity pending.”

It was the sentence she'd defended throughout the day.

A person might need medicine long before the state decided what to call them.
