# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 007 · Night Shift

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Neria Calden. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/007-turno-de-noche.md`.

***

The first time Neria Calden heard a stopped clock, she was inside an engine room where everything else was still running.

It was almost midnight. She had returned to the eastern station to fulfill the watch duty agreed with the municipal investigation. The outer corridor smelled of hot grease and damp clothes. Both main doors remained sealed, and the chain installed by Amiel Rusk hung so taut that it seemed part of the architecture.

She didn't need to go inside. The containment regulator could be read through an inspection window in the corridor, installed precisely so workers wouldn't have to open the room every time the pressure changed.

Irsa was waiting beside the viewing window.

“The mechanism hasn't changed its sound,” she said. “But the needle has.”

“How much?”

“Three divisions higher than it read this afternoon.”

“Has the flow increased?”

“Not according to the auxiliary scale.”

Neria left her coat on a railing and unfolded the shift sheet. The station had two instruments for monitoring the return line: a needle gauge connected to a pressure tap and a column scale, visible from the canal, that responded to the actual water level. They didn't measure exactly the same thing. During certain operations, they could differ. In fact, that difference had helped reveal the emergency.

But during hours of steady operation, they should maintain a predictable relationship.

The column remained at its resting level. The needle was too high.

“Did anyone touch it after we sealed the room?” Neria asked.

Irsa shook her head.

“Nobody crossed the seal. I've got the hourly watch signatures.”

“And the neighboring warehouse?”

“Nobody records that access for us.”

Neria looked at the wall. The conduit connecting the gauge to its adjustment box passed through the bricks and ended in a room leased out by the port. On the renovation plan, that room was labeled as storage space with no active equipment. The older report said otherwise.

They couldn't force it open. Nor could they wait for a false reading to justify a dangerous maneuver.

“Let's call the inspector,” she said.

Irsa looked at her with annoyance.

“He'll take an hour. What do we do until then?”

“Until then, we don't move any floodgate based on this needle. We'll use the column and auxiliary measurements to maintain a safe position.”

“Then we'll have to notify the dike watch.”

“Yes.”

Irsa picked up the wall telephone. Neria stayed beside the viewing window and remembered how she had trusted the panel when the water first began rising. She hadn't trusted it for long; she'd looked for an independent reading almost immediately. Even so, it was possible that someone was constructing a mechanism designed to make a tired person follow the wrong needle.

The thought filled her with such tangible anger that she had to rest a hand on the railing.

At twelve twenty, the basin's watch commander called to confirm the instruction. Neria repeated that the equipment would remain stable, with no further maneuvers unless a verified need arose. She had the suspicious reading of the main gauge entered in the log, not a claim that the station was out of control.

She had no intention of causing a second accident to prove the first.

***

Amiel arrived with an officer and a clerk. They brought the preservation folder and a photograph taken when the room was sealed that morning.

“Before I look at anything,” he said, “I want to know who saw the needle change.”

“Nobody saw it move,” Irsa replied. “I saw a different reading when my shift came around.”

“That distinction matters.”

Neria took the photograph. The gauge appeared behind stained glass; the needle's line coincided with a blue mark on the scale. Now the needle crossed a red mark two divisions higher up.

“The reading isn't the only thing that's changed,” she said. “The reference mark has shifted too.”

Amiel brought his lamp closer.

The blue mark had been painted on the protective glass, not on the dial. If someone had moved the glass, the mark would have shifted in relation to the graduations. But the heads of the retaining screws were on the other side, inside the sealed room.

Neria looked at the photograph again.

“The mark hasn't moved. It's the graduated dial behind it that's changed.”

The inspector followed her finger.

In the morning image, the number twenty sat beside the lower edge of the blue mark. Now it was slightly above it. It wasn't a huge difference, but it exceeded the distortion that could be attributed to the angle of the photographs.

“Can the dial be turned from the warehouse?” Amiel asked.

“The shaft adjustment allows the zero point and the mechanism's response to shift within a small range,” Neria explained. “It's used after calibration, not during operation.”

Irsa added:

“And it takes a square-tipped tool.”

Neria studied the locking screw visible beside the shaft. In the morning photographs, its slot pointed left. Now it was almost vertical.

These were not memories. There were numbered photographs, taken before the room had been sealed, and a witness who could explain their significance. That didn't eliminate the possibility of error, but it turned a suspicion into something that could be examined.

Amiel ordered new photographs taken from the same camera support. The clerk recorded the distance to the viewing window and the position of the lamp. It wasn't a perfect reconstruction; the glass caused reflections, and the images could be distorted. They entered those limitations in the report.

“I need the warehouse key,” Amiel said.

“The tenant isn't answering at this hour,” the officer observed.

“Then we'll request an extension of the court order. We're not going to invent an emergency that doesn't exist.”

Neria swallowed a sharp reply. The inspector seemed to live surrounded by doors that he needed permission to open. Yet the previous night she had seen that a rushed procedure could leave her without useful evidence.

Before they left, Irsa asked to speak to her privately. Neria walked with her to the end of the corridor, where they could hear water striking the buttresses.

“Do you remember the breakdown last autumn?” the worker asked.

“The pressure transmitter?”

“The consortium sent a replacement part. They told us the original had too much play in its shaft.”

“Did we return it?”

“I don't know. I signed for them to take it away.”

“Do you have the receipt?”

Irsa pulled a folded, oil-stained piece of paper from her pocket. It bore a date, a housing identification number, and a collection signature. The number didn't match the mechanism photographed that morning.

Neria read it twice.

“It could have been an auxiliary housing.”

“Could have,” Irsa said. “That's why I kept it.”

The receipt didn't prove the instrument had been changed during the emergency. Instead, it raised a question about which component was installed and who had the authority to adjust it.

Neria asked Irsa to hand the paper directly to the clerk and keep a sealed copy. The worker hesitated.

“If this ends up at a hearing, they'll say I helped install it.”

“If you did, you'll have to explain.”

“Is that all you can promise?”

Neria shook her head.

“I can promise I won't hide your name to protect my own.”

Irsa seemed to accept the answer without thanking her.

***

At dawn, Neria returned to the corridor to check the regulation. The auxiliary reading remained stable. The main gauge was still offset. The system continued drawing energy from the reserve, within the established operating range needed to hold the valve in position.

There was no new crisis. There was new evidence.

Toben arrived with breakfast and two copies of the old inventory. He left one on the railing.

“The technician who authorized the replacement last autumn was listed as being on leave that week,” he said.

Neria examined the name and then the signature.

“Are you sure?”

“The leave record's in the personnel office. It doesn't mean he didn't come in to work, but someone needs to ask him.”

Neria raised Irsa's receipt toward the light.

In the margin, beside the housing number, someone had penciled an instruction now darkened by greasy fingerprints: “External adjustment. Do not open room.”

The words weren't a confession. They were a service instruction, without a full date or an identified author.

And yet they described exactly the point where someone could have touched the needle without breaking the seals.

Neria laid the documents on a clean surface.

“Don't touch anything,” she told Toben. “This time I want it to be clear who changed what.”
