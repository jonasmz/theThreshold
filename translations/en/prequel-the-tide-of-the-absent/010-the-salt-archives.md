# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 010 · The Salt Archives

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Breda Lin. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/010-los-archivos-de-sal.md`.

***

Breda Lin distrusted round numbers.

Real losses rarely ended in zero. A broken plank might cost seventeen coins and three copper pieces; an immobilized engine might cost two men's wages for nine days and the hire of a winch. That was why the folder of compensation claims opened after the flood seemed strange to her before she reached the second page.

Every vessel insured by Mareval was claiming the same amount.

Breda worked for the Coastal Protection Mutual, an organization that collected premiums, paid claims, and tried to survive both. Her office occupied the upper floor of an old salt warehouse. On damp days, the wall shed white crystals that clung to the wooden filing cabinets.

That morning she'd requested every case file connected to the eastern basin. Twelve folders had arrived.

Three concerned boats she knew. Two belonged to passing freighters. The other seven were registered to small transport companies with addresses in the same alley.

Breda made a mark beside each address.

There was nothing illegal about several companies sharing an office. Nor was it unusual for an insurance broker to handle numerous policies from a single desk. What was harder to explain was why seven different vessels had reported damage of precisely the same value, sustained in the same minute, on forms written in identical handwriting.

“I need the vessel registration records,” she told her assistant.

Nimo, a young man who habitually sorted documents by paper size rather than date, lifted a box from the floor.

“The port's or ours?”

“Both. And the inspection ledger.”

“The port may take several days to reply.”

“Then we'll start with what we have.”

Breda opened the first policy. The vessel was called Second Dawn. Its declared capacity was enough to carry grain between the docks, and it had a Seleris registration and a hull inspection signed four months earlier. The certificate included a shipyard number.

She looked up that number in the inspection register.

It belonged to a barge scrapped the previous year.

She checked the digits again. One might have been copied incorrectly. She searched the index by name, by owner, and by inspection location.

No second vessel with that name appeared.

“It could be a broker's mistake,” Nimo said.

“It could. I need to check against the public register before I call it anything else.”

Breda wrote “unresolved inconsistency” on a separate sheet. She detested the phrase. She also knew that a suspicion formed too quickly could ruin a legitimate claim or allow a company to deny genuine damage.

The sixth file contained a photograph of the hull. Breda recognized the quay in the background, but the stern lay outside the frame and no name was visible. The image proved that someone had photographed a vessel, not that it was the one insured.

Below it was an inspection signature.

Breda went still.

It was hers.

***

The signature belonged to a template used during a collective verification campaign the previous autumn. Breda had reviewed contracts and invoices, but she hadn't personally inspected every hull. The Mutual had hired outside surveyors to do that work and permitted them to issue forms under her department's supervision.

She had signed the overall certification.

She remembered the meeting. The director had assured her the documentation was sufficient, that spot checks would do, and that auditors shouldn't delay the busy season. Breda had objected to the lack of individual photographs. Then she'd accepted a written guarantee from the provider.

She searched among her copies for that guarantee. It was there, bearing a promise of contractual responsibility and a date.

If the policies turned out to be fraudulent, her signature wouldn't automatically make her an accomplice. It wouldn't leave her outside the problem either.

Nimo noticed the change in her expression.

“Would you like me to call the director?”

“Not yet. Bring me the premium payment ledger.”

The payments were up to date. That made it less likely that the policies were simply forgotten records for decommissioned vessels. Money was still coming in regularly from a commercial account linked to Mareval, through a broker who pooled insured clients.

Breda wrote down every date and amount. The installments had increased two months before the accident, when the vessels' insured values were changed.

“Was coverage expanded?” Nimo asked.

“Yes. From ordinary damage to losses caused by port emergencies.”

“Like the one this week.”

“Exactly like the one this week.”

Nimo leaned over the ledger.

“Then somebody knew there would be an emergency.”

Breda closed the register carefully.

“No. Somebody bought protection against that kind of emergency. Companies do it all the time. Don't confuse the usefulness of a policy with evidence that someone planned what happened.”

Her assistant nodded, disappointed.

Breda was disappointed too. A quick conclusion would have let her put the folder on the director's desk and demand an answer. Instead, she needed to verify the existence of seven vessels and the authenticity of an inspection certified under her responsibility.

She asked Nimo to draft confirmation requests to three independent sources: the port registry, the shipyard named in the policies, and the insurance broker. Each request was to ask for the same information using the same reference dates, so the answers would be comparable.

Midmorning brought a claimant in a grease-stained coat. He introduced himself as Orin Farel, captain of Good Shadow. The Mutual didn't insure his boat, but his cooperative had filed a claim against the port's civil liability coverage.

“They told me you could explain why nobody's paying,” he said.

Breda offered him a chair.

“I can explain what's in our file. I can't force the port to acknowledge a fact it still disputes.”

Orin placed a repair estimate on the desk.

“The hull is in dry dock. Money doesn't wait for some official to make up his mind.”

Breda read the sum. It wasn't round. There were charges for iron, rivets, transport, workshop hours, and a deduction for existing wear. It looked like an estimate of genuine damage, although it still needed assessment.

“Do you have photographs of the boat from before?”

“Two. And ten years of maintenance logs.”

“That will help distinguish new damage from old.”

Orin leaned forward.

“Are you telling me I'll get paid?”

“I'm telling you which documents will let you dispute the amount without depending solely on the port assessor's word.”

He was silent. Then he asked why boats belonging to large companies were already receiving advances.

Breda didn't answer immediately.

She knew that seven Mareval claims had been marked for provisional payment. A committee other than hers had authorized them, and she didn't yet have the authority to cancel them. But she could request a temporary hold based on verifiable inconsistencies.

“I'm going to review those authorizations,” she said.

“For my boat?”

“For every boat that claims to have been in the basin.”

Orin left the estimate on the desk.

“If you find one that wasn't there, ask why it's getting paid before those of us who were.”

***

The first reply from the shipyard arrived at dusk.

The signature on Second Dawn's certificate belonged to a surveyor who had worked there, but the hull number did indeed correspond to a scrapped vessel. The shipyard attached the dismantling record, complete with date and scrap-delivery entry.

Breda now had a concrete documentary contradiction. To resolve it, she still had to rule out a registration transfer or an identification error. She sent the document to internal custody and requested suspension of the advance on that policy until the discrepancy could be explained.

The Mutual's director received her with a stern expression.

“Mareval is our largest shipping client,” he said.

“It's also responsible for submitting accurate information.”

“If we withhold payments without cause, they'll sue.”

“We have a hull number associated with a vessel previously struck from service. That's grounds for verification, not a conviction.”

The director rose and looked out the window. The first lights of the night were coming on around the port.

“How many files might be affected?”

“Seven. I don't know yet whether they all have the same problem.”

“Who approved their inspections?”

Breda held his gaze.

“My department. Under my signature.”

She offered no excuse. The outside surveyor's guarantee existed, but she wasn't going to use it to pretend her own responsibility had vanished.

The director returned to his desk.

“You have two days to report back. In the meantime, don't disclose your suspicions.”

Breda agreed to the requirement of commercial confidentiality on one condition: she would retain certified copies of every document under review and put any instruction affecting the audit into writing.

The director signed.

Back at her office, Nimo was waiting with the port's vessel register. He'd obtained an authorized extract from an employee he'd known since school.

“I looked up all seven names,” he said.

Breda took the sheet.

Four vessels were registered. Two had registrations that had expired before the flood. The seventh appeared on none of the surviving pages of the register.

Beside that missing name, the broker had entered a mooring berth that Breda recognized.

It was the berth where, according to Orin's file, Good Shadow had been unloading provisions before the accident.

Two separate claims placed two boats in the same spot at the same time.

Breda took out a fresh sheet and wrote down a question she couldn't yet answer: “Which vessel was really there?”
