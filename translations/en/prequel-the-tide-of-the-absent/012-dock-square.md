# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 012 · Dock Square

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Orin Farel. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/012-la-plaza-del-muelle.md`.

***

The first man to arrive at the square brought a chair. The second person brought a cooking pot.

Orin Farel had asked the cooperative to call a meeting of those affected by the flood, but he'd expected to find twenty angry captains and a lawyer ready to explain why they all needed to file separate claims. Instead, before noon there were bread sellers, women from the canneries, dike workers, families from the low-lying district, and a small group of students offering to write letters for people who couldn't do it themselves.

The pot belonged to Dalia, Erven Tal's sister. She had made soup for those who came from far away and said she wasn't going to let the gathering end before lunch.

“Have they found my brother?” she asked when Orin approached.

“I haven't heard anything new.”

Dalia looked away. The man she was also waiting to hear about, her husband, had worked on the same lighter that night. He was still in the hospital and couldn't give her an answer about what had happened to the skipper.

“The port wants us to sign a statement saying we don't know where he is,” she said.

“That's true.”

“They want us to sign that he stopped working before the emergency.”

Orin looked at the paper folded in her hand.

“That's not the same thing.”

The form had three boxes and a space for waiving claims for unsubstantiated damages. Dalia hadn't signed it.

Orin asked permission to show it at the meeting. She agreed on one condition: nobody was to speak Erven's name as though he were dead.

Dock Square had no platform. There was a dry fountain, a clock that ran slow, and a statue of a former captain whose plaque had rusted until the date was barely visible. Orin chose to stand on an empty fish crate, not because he liked speaking from above people, but because those in the back row needed to hear him.

“We're not going to decide who caused the flood today,” he said. “We're going to decide what people need while that is being investigated.”

A man shouted that he needed to find the person responsible and hang him from the dike.

Orin waited until the man finished yelling.

“You can demand justice. I can't promise you someone to punish before we know what they did.”

A murmur ran through the crowd. Some applauded. Others moved away in disgust.

Mara Veis, the captain who had offered her tugboat to the cooperative, raised a sheet of paper.

“I've got two injured sailors and a boat laid up. If we argue about who's to blame for three hours, they'll still be without wages tomorrow. I propose we begin by recording damage and urgent needs.”

It was a proposal that could turn into actual work.

Orin asked the students to organize three tables: care for the injured, vessels, and homes. Statements from witnesses would be written on separate sheets from financial claims, so that someone's need for bread wouldn't depend on remembering an exact time.

Neria Calden appeared beside the workers' table. She wasn't wearing a uniform. Her presence prompted two men to start insulting her.

“You opened the floodgate!” one shouted. “My workshop was underwater!”

Neria didn't deny it.

“Yes. I opened it to relieve the rising level inside the basin.”

“Then pay for my engines!”

Orin stepped between them before the argument pulled everyone in.

“The fact that she can explain why she opened it doesn't mean you didn't suffer losses,” he told the tradesman. “And the fact that you suffered losses doesn't prove she could have avoided opening it. Let's record both.”

The man stared at him as though Orin had taken away something he desperately needed: a single enemy.

Eventually, he went over to the housing table.

***

Jaro Velk carried a small notebook. He had copied the names of coworkers willing to confirm that his brother Liron had worked for the dredging crew. At first, he didn't want to hand it over to the square's committee.

“They'll use it to fire them later,” he said.

Orin knew that was a reasonable fear. A public list of irregular subcontractors could make compensation easier to obtain, but retaliation easier too.

“We won't post it on any wall,” he replied. “We'll prepare a copy for the investigation and keep another with two custodians chosen by you.”

“Who?”

“That's for you to decide.”

Jaro looked at the other young people. One suggested Nurse Rema, who cared for the injured and understood the risks of disclosing medical information. Another suggested a teacher from the low-lying district. Both women agreed to safeguard witness records, not to make diagnoses or assign blame.

Orin remembered the log found among the lighter's broken timbers. The note “Await scheduled opening” still had no explanation. He could have read it aloud to the square and won instant silence, followed by the sort of outrage that sometimes made a vote easier.

He didn't.

The paper was damaged, the time couldn't be verified with confidence, and its chain of custody began in a pile of planks salvaged by a sailor. He'd given Amiel a copy. Until an expert examined it, Orin didn't want to turn it into a public accusation.

A woman raised her hand from the housing table.

“My house wasn't flooded,” she said. “But the neighborhood generator stopped working, and we lost the food we sell on Sundays. Does that count?”

“It counts,” Mara answered. “There's a cost even if no wall was broken.”

Another man said the municipal emergency fund only paid for material repairs. An argument broke out over what should qualify as damage.

Orin climbed down from the crate and listened. He'd spent so long worrying about the hull of Good Shadow that he'd almost forgotten the city could lose income without a drop of water touching the floor of a house.

In the end, they agreed not to reduce the requests to a single number. The tables would collect immediate needs, losses that could be substantiated, and losses still awaiting assessment. No one would receive a promise of payment from an assembly that did not administer public funds.

That disappointed those looking for a simple answer. It also meant they could keep working.

***

The meeting was nearly over when three men in port-administration jackets arrived. The first carried a folder and wore a courteous expression.

“We've come to offer a formal channel for claims,” he announced.

Orin asked him to state his name.

“Loden Vair. Repair contracts, Mareval Consortium.”

A murmur passed through the square. Orin recognized the surname from the requests his cooperative had submitted without receiving a reply.

Loden explained that the concession holder was preparing an extraordinary rehabilitation plan and could advance materials to affected businesses, provided they accepted a centralized damage assessment.

“Who chooses the assessors?” Mara asked.

“The port authority, with technical assistance from our consortium.”

“And who reviews their valuations?”

Loden spread his hands.

“The procedure is still being designed.”

Dalia stepped forward with her form.

“Are we also supposed to sign that our relatives stopped working before the alarm?”

Loden examined the paper.

“It's an administrative template. It doesn't mean you're abandoning the search for anyone.”

“Then take that sentence out,” she said.

The crowd began talking all at once. Orin feared that a single shove would be enough to turn the gathering into something the newspapers could describe as a disturbance.

He called for silence in the loudest voice he'd used in years of towing ships.

“The assembly is neither accepting nor refusing any advance today. But we request an independent inventory of the injured, the workers, and the damage. And we demand access to the file for the upcoming concession before it's put to a vote.”

Loden closed his folder.

“The concession is an administrative matter, not a dockside referendum.”

“So were the damages,” Orin replied. “Until someone had to live with them.”

Among the students, a young woman began passing around a sheet for signatures. Orin asked them to read the text aloud before requesting a single one. It needed to be clear that people were signing a petition for a hearing and assistance, not making a criminal accusation.

The list moved slowly through the square. Dalia signed for herself. Jaro signed on his brother's behalf only after clarifying that the document identified him as a family representative, not as a substitute for Liron's testimony.

By late afternoon, there were more than two hundred signatures.

Orin took the petition to the municipal counter with two witnesses and insisted on a registration number. The clerk stamped it, recorded the time, and returned a copy.

At the doorway, Mara caught up with him.

“There's another notice,” she said.

It was a summons from the Civic Council. In three days they would discuss the new concession for port maintenance and recovery.

The announcement specified that the file was considered urgent because the facilities had suffered an “unforeseeable natural event.”

Orin read the words and thought of a windless night, a trapped tugboat, and a name still missing.

“They've already decided what caused it,” he murmured.

Mara folded the notice.

“Then we'll bring our questions before they decide the price.”
