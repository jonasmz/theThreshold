# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 025 · Kelor Dam

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Neria Calden. **Location:** Rural Kelor Dam, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/025-la-presa-de-kelor.md`.

***

Kelor Dam didn't look like a machine designed to hide anything.

From the hillside, Neria Calden could see a wall of gray stone, a long reservoir, and the small settlement where the people who repaired the rural floodgates lived. There were vegetable gardens, a school, and a muddy road.

Neria was traveling with joint authorization from the municipality and the regional water authority. She had no blanket search warrant. Her aim was to inspect a supply line that, according to old plans, connected to the cold chamber at kilometer forty-three.

Vela Noren accompanied her as a specialist in signal records.

“I thought it would be bigger,” Vela said.

“From below, when it's open, you might think the opposite.”

Radan, the watch supervisor, and Elsia, who oversaw measurements, were waiting for them. Both had clay-stained boots.

“We can show you the feeder and the ledgers,” Elsia said. “We won't hand over originals without a formal request.”

“We want to understand how what you've recorded actually works,” Neria replied.

In a gallery stood a distribution board supplied by a hydraulic turbine and a reserve accumulator. The turbine converted some of the energy of falling water into useful work; it didn't create energy on its own. When the flow decreased, the accumulator sustained instruments and controls for a limited period.

A branch line ran toward the railway post. It was designated as a stabilization supply for special cargoes.

“How much power does it deliver?” Neria asked.

Radan pointed to the limiter's plate.

“Whatever the contract permits. If they ask for more, the regulator cuts off before it drains our safety reserve.”

Elsia showed them the subscriber ledger. The account belonged to a commercial intermediary, but the destination was identified by station number. It matched the chamber they'd inspected.

“That explains the line on the drawing,” Vela said.

“It explains why the line exists,” Neria replied. “We still need to know whether there was abnormal consumption.”

During the night of the flood, the feeder had received several stabilization requests. None individually exceeded the limit. Together they required drawing on the reserve, without pushing the dam out of control.

“Did you notify anyone?” Neria asked.

“The regional center. Here's the acknowledgment.”

The date was earlier than the port emergency, although the clocks still needed comparing. Neria didn't interpret it as a prediction.

The consumption had a physical route, an energy source, and a cost.

***

They went out onto the crest. The reservoir was high but below the alert level. Elsia explained that the mountains had received moderate rainfall, not an extraordinary storm over Port Cendra.

“Water doesn't have to fall on the city to reach it,” she said. “What matters is how much flows out, how long it takes, and which channels carry it.”

There were three paths into the valley: the main riverbed, the agricultural canal, and an industrial conduit connected to reservoirs near the city. The gates sent water along one route or another, within limits of level and pressure. No conduit created additional flow.

The ledger recorded an opening of the industrial conduit on the afternoon before the accident. The increase was consistent with a request for factory reserve supply, though higher than the average that week.

“Who authorized the maneuver?” Neria asked.

Radan pointed to a signature.

“The regional office. They had a request for extraordinary supply.”

“Do you still have the request?”

“We can certify its number.”

Neria studied the map. The water would have taken hours to cross intermediate reservoirs before reaching the port valves. She couldn't conclude that this discharge had caused the flood without accounting for losses, storage, and later maneuvers.

“We need the intermediate water levels,” she said.

“And we need to measure the current transit time,” Elsia added. “The old tables assume gates that were modified afterward.”

A bell interrupted the conversation. It came from the settlement, not the alarm board.

Radan looked toward the gardens.

“The mill ditch is rising too quickly.”

Elsia hurried to the panel. One indicator showed the agricultural canal rising despite the absence of a new order.

“Has a gate failed?” Vela asked.

“The return mechanism may be jammed,” Neria replied. “We won't know from here.”

***

The team split up. Elsia stayed at the controls; Radan walked toward the spillway; Neria and Vela accompanied an operator named Tamen to the observation point.

The water was running fast, though still within the channel. A man was moving sacks away from the bank.

“How long before it reaches the mill?” Neria asked.

“If it keeps rising, we'll close the lower path,” Tamen said. “I won't give you a time without measuring.”

Vela used the rural line to report the water level. Fixed stations repeated impulses using reserve power; they couldn't transport people or send information into the past. Each notice required a sender, a time, and confirmation of receipt.

Elsia established that the pressure indicator didn't match the physical scale.

“Don't maneuver on the needle alone,” Neria instructed over the field telephone. “Check the level and the mechanical position.”

Radan found a partially jammed retainer. A piece of driftwood was preventing it from returning.

“I can clear it, but the pressure has to be released first.”

Elsia authorized them to reduce the supply gradually and open an alternative passage into the main riverbed. The winches used assistance from a finite reserve. Neria checked that the minimum safe margin remained before recommending the movement.

Once the pressure fell, Radan removed the wood with a pole and secured the pin. Tamen confirmed that the level had stopped rising.

The mill was safe.

A woman arrived from the road with a child holding her hand. Her name was Orsa, and she cultivated the lowest plot, the first that would have been flooded. She asked whether she needed to move her animals that night. Neria refused to reassure her with invented certainty. She explained that the obstruction had been removed, the levels would remain under observation, and Elsia would notify the settlement if conditions changed again.

“You always tell us when the water's already in the yard,” Orsa said.

“This time you'll be able to consult the watch log,” Elsia replied over the field line. “I'll leave a copy of the readings at the school before nightfall.”

Neria understood that the promise was as important as the repaired pin. It wouldn't prevent another breakdown, but it would let residents act without waiting for an official explanation the following day.

***

Back in the archive, Elsia showed them the authorization for the extraordinary water supply. It came from a municipal office in Port Cendra and requested increased industrial supply for a limited interval.

Neria had seen the code in an annex to the concession. It might be a standard authorization or a reused form. She didn't know whether it had anything to do with the emergency.

She requested certified copies, reservoir levels, and warnings about energy consumption. The originals would remain in the archive for any later judicial comparison.

“What will you do with this?” Elsia asked.

“Compare times and quantities with the riverside reservoirs,” Neria said. “I'm not going to blame the flood on this without proving it.”

Radan approached with another notebook.

“Something doesn't add up in our accounts.”

It was the factory distribution register. The order had increased the volume allocated to Port Cendra, but the declared recipients' total consumption didn't account for the whole supply.

“Where's the difference?” Vela asked.

“Perhaps it was held in an intermediate reservoir,” Elsia replied. “Or lost along the conduit. We need to measure it.”

Neria reviewed the diagram.

One reservoir was listed as out of service for a year. Yet the log for that night assigned it a level reading.

“If it was closed, who sent the data?”

Elsia searched the maintenance sheet. The reopening box was blank.

“There's no record of it being returned to service.”

Neria thought of the workers whose names were missing from registers. She made herself resist combining disappearances and mechanical breakdowns simply because both sets of records had gaps.

But that reservoir had a precise location, a questionable reading, and a physical connection to the port.

“We need to know whether the station transmitted that figure or somebody wrote it down afterward,” she said.

Vela studied the line number.

“I'll request the receipt acknowledgments. If they've been preserved, we can distinguish a transmission from a transcription.”

Neria tucked the map under her coat.

The dam had shown them that water moved through real conduits. Now they had to find out who had decided to open them and which reservoir had received the volume the ledgers couldn't explain.
