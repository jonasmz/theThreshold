# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 028 · The Tides Bank

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Orin Farel. **Location:** Port Cendra, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/028-el-banco-de-las-mareas.md`.

***

The Tides Bank had survived three fires, two trade wars, and an epidemic of dock fever. Orin Farel couldn't understand why it might now fail because of a tugboat's hull.

The manager received him in a narrow office, its windows shut against the smell of salt. Her name was Elira Anet, and she wore a copper brooch shaped like a small boat. Her father had founded the sailors' savings fund that had eventually become a bank.

On the desk lay a notice of precautionary attachment against the cooperative's income.

“The debt isn't new,” Elira said. “What's new is that someone has demanded enforcement of the guarantees before the flood-damage claim has been resolved.”

Orin knew the loan. The cooperative had used it to upgrade safety equipment and buy coal during the previous winter. The contract contained an acceleration clause if three vessels remained out of service for a specified number of days.

The accident had left exactly three laid up.

“We can't pay the entire amount now,” he said.

“I know.”

“Who asked for enforcement?”

Elira slid a copy of the demand across the desk. The holder of the debt was a finance company that had acquired part of the bank's loan portfolio. Mareval wasn't named directly, although the company dealt in transport and storage contracts.

“You sold our debt?” Orin asked.

“We transferred a share of the risk through a financing agreement. We didn't sell your cooperative. The difference matters, even if it's no consolation today.”

Orin rested his hands on his knees.

“To me it means somebody I've never met wants to take our income.”

“And to me it means the bank may have an obligation to honor a guarantee that shouldn't be enforced while the damage is disputed.”

Elira explained the terms carefully. She could seek a temporary stay, provide countersecurity, and contest whether the emergency amounted to a default for which the cooperative was responsible. She couldn't unilaterally forgive a loan backed by other residents' deposits.

“How much do you need to stop the attachment?” Orin asked.

She wrote down a figure.

It was more than the cooperative had gathered since the flood.

***

A line of small depositors waited in the public hall. One woman was saving for her husband's treatment. An apprentice carpenter had put money aside for tools. Two widows received the payments the city sent them, always late, through the bank.

Orin watched them while he waited for Elira to find the original contract.

He hadn't thought of them as part of his problem before. Banks tended to appear in his life as doors demanding signatures and issuing money with conditions. But this bank also belonged to the people who placed there the little they managed to save.

Elira returned with a folder.

“If I use ordinary reserves as security for your cooperative, I'd endanger the liquidity of those accounts,” she said. “I won't do that.”

“I wouldn't ask you to.”

“We could activate the extraordinary stability fund. It's small and needs board approval. Using it would reduce our capacity to respond to another commercial accident.”

Orin understood that the manager wasn't offering a free rescue. She was proposing to shift some risk onto an institution whose existence depended on trust.

“What would the cooperative have to provide?” he asked.

“Your complete income ledgers, an assessment of Good Shadow, and a realistic recovery schedule. I also need assurances that no member is concealing personal debts to inflate the value of the guarantee.”

Orin thought of Mir, who'd requested advances for his household, and Mara, who was financing an injured sailor's wages.

“I'll have to discuss it with them.”

“Then do that before you ask my board to vote.”

He left with a copy of the agreement. At the dock, Solen was examining the temporary planking on Good Shadow.

“Are they attaching our earnings?” he asked.

“They're trying to.”

“And the bank?”

“It can help, but it would have less room to help others.”

Solen gave a bitter laugh.

“We always end up owing someone who owes somebody else.”

“This time we'll have to show every account,” Orin said.

It wasn't the answer his sailor had wanted.

***

The cooperative assembly met that night beneath the same roof they'd used to organize emergency towing.

Mara insisted that Orin read every clause. She wouldn't accept a summary. The members debated whether to pledge future income or sell a spare part to reduce the loan. Nobody was willing to hand over a vessel to a finance company because they'd rushed to sign.

An older skipper opposed the agreement.

“If we let the bank rescue us, tomorrow they'll charge us for breathing,” he said.

“If we don't stop enforcement, we'll lose the income from the next few months' routes,” Mara replied.

Orin showed them the ledgers. There was income expected from jobs already contracted, damages claimed but not acknowledged, and expenses that could be deferred. He separated certain amounts from possible ones. Good Shadow still needed iron for its repairs; the claim against the port couldn't yet be counted as cash.

“I don't want us voting on money that may never arrive,” he said.

Mir asked for a clause protecting crew wages before any surplus was distributed. Solen insisted no skipper should sign freight contracts without manifests just to recover income quickly.

The second condition provoked an argument. Some considered it obvious; others had accepted loads with incomplete documents for years.

Orin told them about the three crates he'd refused and the dizziness Cevan had suffered afterward, the skipper who'd taken the job. He didn't claim the cargo had caused the illness. The clinic was still investigating a possible leak from Cevan's engine. But the episode had shown how little they knew about what they transported.

The assembly approved the agreement with two conditions: transparent accounts and a ban on committing vessels without hazard documentation. Orin was authorized to negotiate, not to sign just anything.

When it ended, Mara accompanied him to the door.

“You didn't save the boat,” she said.

“Not yet.”

“You saved the chance for us to try together.”

Orin wasn't sure he deserved the words.

***

The bank's board heard the application the next day.

There were five members. Three were prepared to assume the countersecurity if the port authority confirmed in writing that a claim was pending. One wanted to reject it to protect depositors. The fifth asked what would happen if the claim failed.

Elira didn't hide the scenario.

“The extraordinary fund could lose part of its capacity,” she said. “The bank would have to reduce new loans for a season. Ordinary deposits would retain their legal priority, but the institution would be less prepared for another contingency.”

Orin felt their eyes turn toward him.

“I can't promise you compensation,” he said. “I can show you our contracts, the damage, the wages, and the jobs we can fulfill once the boat is repaired.”

Elira proposed a narrower alternative: lodge countersecurity for thirty days, require an independent valuation, and apply to the commercial court to suspend enforcement until it could be determined whether the three vessels' immobilization was attributable to a default by the cooperative.

The board approved it by three votes.

It was a risky, reversible decision, not forgiveness of the debt.

Before signing, Elira called Orin into her office.

“The finance company has offered to withdraw its demand,” she said.

Orin felt his chest loosen.

“In exchange for what?”

She slid a letter toward him.

The creditor would defer payments if the cooperative assigned its towing routes exclusively for six months to an operator designated by Mareval.

Orin read the condition again.

Six months would be enough to lose customers, staff, and independence. The debt would recede from view in exchange for surrendering the means of paying it.

“That's not a solution,” he said.

“It's an offer. You decide whether to put it to your members.”

“I will. And I'll tell them exactly what it says.”

Elira put the letter away.

“Then we'll sign the provisional countersecurity.”

As they came out, the bank clerk received a notice from the finance company: enforcement had been brought forward to the following morning. The guarantee deposit had to be registered before the commercial court closed.

Elira called her cashier and ordered the documents prepared.

Orin looked out the window. In the square, customers were still coming in with their coins and small envelopes of savings.

The manager was risking an institution's financial margin to prevent a debt from becoming control over the port.

And he had until nightfall to show that her trust hadn't been given in vain.
