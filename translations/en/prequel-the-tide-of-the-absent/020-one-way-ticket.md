# The Tide of the Absent
## Chapter 020 · One-Way Ticket

**Status:** First draft, English translation. **POV:** Orin Farel. **Location:** Port Cendra and the railway station, EC 548. **Source:** `precuelas/la-marea-de-los-ausentes/capitulos/020-pasaje-de-ida.md`.

***

The man who claimed he'd seen Erven Tal wasn't sure he'd seen his face.

Orin Farel listened patiently, standing beside the dry dock where Good Shadow awaited repairs. The witness was a railway porter named Harel, a man with cracked hands and a hat too small for him. He'd come on the recommendation of one of the cooperative's sailors.

“He was wearing a brown jacket, with a patch on the shoulder,” Harel said. “Like the one described on the missing-person notices.”

“A lot of people wear jackets like that,” Orin replied.

“I know. That's why I didn't go to the police and tell them I'd found him.”

“Where did you see him?”

“At the station's baggage office. The morning after the accident. He left a parcel or asked about one. I'm not sure.”

Orin looked at the hull raised on its supports. Every passing day increased the cooperative's debt. He'd stopped counting the coins needed to save the boat because the amount changed with every unforeseen repair.

He could ignore a weak story and go back to work. But Dalia had spent days visiting clinics, docks, and offices with a photograph of her brother. If there was a concrete lead, it deserved to be checked at least.

“Is the baggage registered?” he asked.

“Yes. They wouldn't authorize me to open it. It's still in storage because the train to the inland coast was delayed.”

“Under whose name?”

“Erven Tal.”

Orin laid his wrench on a plank.

It wasn't proof Erven was still alive. Someone could have left a package under his name. But the detail was too specific to dismiss as a coincidental resemblance between coats.

“Let's go and get his sister,” he said.

***

Dalia received them in the shipyard kitchen, where she was serving soup to the workers on shift. Her husband was still recovering from the injuries he suffered aboard the lighter; she'd slept four hours and seemed to regard even that much rest as an extravagance.

Orin explained what he knew. He didn't mention a rescue or a return. He said there was a piece of baggage registered in Erven's name at a railway station, and that an employee had seen a man he couldn't positively identify.

Dalia put down the ladle.

“My brother never took the train without telling me,” she said.

“That doesn't mean he couldn't have.”

“It doesn't mean he did, either.”

Orin was grateful she didn't ask him for certainty he didn't have.

At the station, a baggage clerk showed them the register of unclaimed items. The package was identified by a baggage number, a commercial label, and a handwritten name: Erven Tal.

“We can't release it on the strength of a relative's word alone,” she explained. “We need to verify the relationship or have a court instruction.”

Dalia took out two civil identity documents and a certificate of kinship. The clerk examined them and asked a supervisor to validate the process. Orin waited without interfering.

The authorization allowed them to inspect the outside of the package in the presence of witnesses. They couldn't open it yet. It was wrapped in gray canvas and secured with a strap that looked like port equipment. One corner bore traces of salt water, but there were no visible stains on the inside.

“Who deposited it?” Dalia asked.

The clerk consulted the ledger.

“A bearer acting on Estuary Cargo's transport account. There's no personal name on this page.”

“When?”

“At five twenty-one in the morning on the day of the emergency.”

Orin compared the time with what he remembered of the canal. By then, Erven had been missing for several hours, and rescue teams were still searching for people in the basin.

“Can you prove the bearer was my brother?” Dalia asked.

“No,” the clerk replied. “The account code allows baggage to be deposited without identifying the messenger, but the transaction number is recorded.”

Orin requested that they preserve a copy of the entry. He had no authority to seize anything, but the family could ask for information about property registered in their relative's name. If a judicial investigation followed, Amiel would have to obtain the documents within the limits of his powers.

The clerk explained that after a service delay, unclaimed packages were kept for seven days before moving into long-term storage. They were still within that period.

“Why didn't it travel?” Dalia asked.

“The line was interrupted for maintenance. The baggage was held until someone confirmed the destination.”

“And where was it supposed to go?”

“Costa Interior station.”

Orin knew the name. It was an industrial riverbank stretch on the far side of the river mouth, inland, connected by rail to grain and salt warehouses. From there, routes continued toward villages where nobody asked too many questions about a new work crew.

It wasn't an impossible destination. It was precisely the sort of place someone could send a man without attracting curiosity.

***

The supervisor authorized opening the package in Dalia's presence and that of a railway officer. She had requested an inventory as a potential family possession; she did not intend to remove it until its origins were clarified.

The strap came loose easily. Inside were a thick blanket, a pair of worn boots, a shirt, and a small metal case.

Dalia recognized the blanket.

“I repaired it last winter,” she said, pointing to a blue seam.

Orin bent to look.

Identifying a used object was stronger evidence than a matching jacket, but it didn't prove who had packed the parcel. The blanket could have been on the lighter, in a home, or in the possession of someone they knew.

The case contained navigation tools and a stopped pocket watch. Dalia said her brother used one like it. She wouldn't swear it was the same one.

A railway ticket emerged from the shirt pocket.

It was a voucher issued in the name of a worker employed by Estuary Cargo. It bore the route Port Cendra–Costa Interior, the name Erven Tal, and a departure time: six fifty in the morning on the day after the flood.

A company account number appeared at the bottom. The ticket had not been punched by the conductor.

“It wasn't used,” Dalia said.

“It has no validation mark,” Orin corrected. “That might mean he didn't turn up, that the journey was canceled, or that someone forgot to punch it.”

The railway clerk nodded.

“And the scheduled departure was delayed. We'll have to check the train's operating report.”

Dalia held the ticket up to the light.

“If they bought it after the accident, who expected Erven to be able to travel?”

Orin had no answer. Perhaps the ticket had been issued earlier with a later travel date. Perhaps the name was part of a group booking. Perhaps somebody wanted it to appear that the skipper had left the city voluntarily.

“We need to know when it was issued,” he said.

The supervisor looked up the serial number in the book of tickets sold to commercial accounts. The transaction was recorded that same morning, minutes before the baggage was checked in. The cost had been charged to Estuary Cargo's account.

“That proves only that the company paid for a ticket in his name,” the supervisor clarified.

“That's enough to ask them why,” Dalia replied.

The baggage was inventoried and preserved against a receipt. Dalia took certified photographs and the case number with her. Orin agreed to accompany her to the courthouse to give the information to the inspector.

***

On the way back, Dalia asked him to walk along the quay before going to the investigations office. The afternoon was clear, and water lapped against the steps with a calm Orin found almost offensive.

“If Erven left of his own accord,” she said, “I want him to tell me. Even if he abandoned the others.”

“We don't know that he left.”

“I know. But when someone disappears, everybody talks about what they did to find him. Hardly anyone asks what decisions he might have made.”

Orin said nothing. He'd known Erven as a lighter skipper, not as a brother or husband. He knew how Erven handled a line under pressure and that he bought rounds when he was paid. He knew nothing about the debts he might have had outside the port.

At the courthouse, Amiel received the inventory and requested preservation of the commercial ticket-issuance ledger. He didn't promise to locate Erven through a piece of paper.

“Can you find out whether anyone boarded?” Dalia asked.

“I'll request the departure report and statements from the staff. A name on a voucher isn't the same as a person in a railway carriage.”

Orin studied the stopped watch listed in the inventory. It seemed that everything he'd done since the flood consisted of separating names from bodies, times from events, and promises from payments.

Before they left, the inspector received a note from the railway supervisor. He'd found a marginal entry in the Costa Interior booking ledger.

The company account hadn't purchased just one ticket.

It had bought eleven, all for the same delayed departure.

Orin remembered the evacuation list from the former shelter. Eleven people, with no record of their arrival.

He looked at Dalia. He didn't tell her he'd found her brother.

He told her there were eleven more names they needed to check.
